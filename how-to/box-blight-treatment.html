Box blight treatment: identify, treat and prevent Cylindrocladium

Brown patches appearing in your Buxus hedge are rarely drought alone. Here is how to tell box blight from other problems, prune it out safely and stop it spreading through the rest of the hedge.

Updated September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Box blight is now so widespread in East Kent that most established Buxus hedges in Sandwich, Deal and the surrounding villages will encounter it sooner or later. The humid air off the coast, particularly in Sandwich Bay and along the Stour valley, creates ideal conditions for the fungus to spread.

The good news is that early treatment works. The bad news is that people often mistake box blight for drought, vine weevil damage or box tree moth, and the wrong diagnosis leads to the wrong fix. This guide walks through identification, treatment and the cultural changes that give your hedge the best chance of recovery.

Pruning and nesting birds

Box blight treatment involves removing infected stems, which counts as hedge work. Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to disturb an active bird nest. Nesting season runs from 1 March to 31 August. Before pruning during this window, check the hedge carefully for nests in use. If you find one, leave that section and return after 31 August.

Before you start: safety

  • Gloves whenever you handle infected material. Box sap is a known irritant and fungal spores should not be transferred from hedge to hedge on bare hands.
  • Do not compost infected cuttings. Bag them in heavy-duty refuse sacks and put them in the general waste. Compost heaps do not reach the temperatures needed to kill Cylindrocladium spores.
  • Sterilise tools between cuts with a 70% isopropyl alcohol spray or a diluted bleach solution (1 part bleach to 9 parts water). Rinse and dry afterwards to protect tool blades.
  • Do not work in wet conditions. Water splashing between stems is one of the main ways the fungus spreads within and between hedges.

Step 1: identify box blight correctly

Box blight is caused by two fungal pathogens: Cylindrocladium buxicola (now correctly named Calonectria pseudonaviculata) and Volutella buxi. Both can appear together.

Cylindrocladium buxicola causes:

  • Leaves turning tan, brown or black, with darker edges
  • Leaves dropping suddenly, leaving bare stems
  • White, fluffy spore masses on leaf undersides in humid or wet weather
  • Dark brown or black streaks on young green stems

Volutella buxi causes:

  • Pinkish or salmon-coloured spore masses on affected stems and leaves
  • More gradual dieback than Cylindrocladium

Distinguish box blight from other common problems:

  • Box tree moth (Cydalima perspectalis): look for webbing inside the hedge, caterpillars (green with black and yellow markings), and frass (droppings). No stem streaking.
  • Drought stress: general bronzing of leaves, often in areas with poorer soil or root competition. No stem lesions, no spore masses.
  • Root problems or waterlogging: yellowing rather than browning, gradual over the whole plant. Check for waterlogged soil and poor drainage.

Step 2: time the work correctly

You need to act quickly once you spot blight, but not carelessly. Work only in dry conditions: a sunny spell of two or more dry days is ideal. Spores travel in water droplets, so cutting in the rain or on a dewy morning spreads infection further.

September to February is the safest window for significant pruning without nesting season risk. If you must treat between March and August, do a careful visual check for active nests before touching the hedge.

Step 3: sterilise tools before you begin

Wipe blades with 70% isopropyl alcohol spray or diluted bleach before starting, between sections of the hedge, and before moving to any other plant. Carry a spray bottle with you. This single step stops you from being the agent of spread.

Step 4: prune out all infected material

Cut back all visibly infected stems to healthy, green wood. Do not just trim the tips: go back until you reach wood that shows no dark streaking when cut across. On a badly infected section this may mean cutting back hard into older wood.

Work methodically from one end of the hedge to the other and catch all prunings on a sheet laid below the hedge. Do not let infected material fall onto the soil and sit there. Cylindrocladium can persist in dead plant matter and soil for several years.

Step 5: bag and dispose of all cuttings immediately

Put all infected cuttings straight into heavy-duty refuse sacks. Tie securely and put in the general waste bin. Do not add to the garden compost heap. Do not leave bags sitting open near the hedge while you continue work.

Step 6: apply a fungicide

Once the infected material is removed, apply a fungicide to the remaining hedge. Products registered for amateur use in the UK that offer some protection against Cylindrocladium include those based on trifloxystrobin or tebuconazole. Read the label, follow dilution rates exactly, and do not exceed the permitted number of applications per year.

For a more organic approach, copper oxychloride-based products offer some protective action when applied to healthy tissue before spores land. They do not cure established infection but may reduce spread from any remaining spores.

Spray in the evening or on an overcast day to reduce the risk of scorching, and do not spray in windy conditions. Coat all surfaces, including the undersides of leaves, where spore masses form.

Step 7: open up the canopy to improve air circulation

Box blight thrives where the interior of the hedge is dense and airless. Once infected material is removed, thin the interior lightly with secateurs to create better airflow through the canopy. Do not over-thin: a box hedge with no internal structure is weakened. The aim is to reduce the humid microclimate where the fungus spreads fastest.

For formal topiary and tightly clipped box edging in Sandwich Old Town and Deal cottage gardens, this means allowing slightly more space between the surface and the internal branch structure, rather than chasing the clipped surface hard.

Step 8: feed, water and monitor recovery

Apply a slow-release balanced fertiliser in September or early spring to support new growth. Avoid high-nitrogen feeds in late summer as they push soft growth that is more susceptible to infection.

Water at the base of the hedge, not overhead. Overhead watering wets the foliage and creates the wet conditions the fungus needs. Drip irrigation or a hose at soil level is better.

Check the hedge every two to three weeks through autumn. If fresh infection appears, retreat promptly. Early intervention on new outbreaks makes a big difference to the eventual outcome.

Long-term prevention

Box blight does not disappear after treatment: the fungus can persist in the soil and in old plant matter for years. The hedge remains susceptible. Long-term management involves:

  • Trimming only in dry conditions, always with sterilised tools
  • Clearing fallen leaves from around the base of the hedge each autumn
  • Avoiding bark mulch against the stems (mulch holds moisture against the bark)
  • Keeping the hedge properly fed but not over-stimulated with nitrogen
  • Checking for new infection after each trimming and treating early

If blight keeps returning despite three or four seasons of treatment, switching to a blight-tolerant alternative is worth considering. Ilex crenata (Japanese holly) gives a very similar formal finish to Buxus and is unaffected by Cylindrocladium. Euonymus japonicus holds up particularly well in the coastal conditions around Sandwich Bay and along the Deal seafront.
