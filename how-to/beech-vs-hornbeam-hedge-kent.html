Beech vs hornbeam hedge in Kent: which should you plant?

Both are native deciduous hedges. Both keep their copper-brown leaves through winter. The difference is mostly about your soil, and on a clay site in East Kent, that difference matters.

Updated September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

We get asked this every bare-root season, usually in November. Someone has looked at their boundary, decided they want a proper native hedge with winter privacy and some colour, and worked out it is either beech or hornbeam. They look essentially the same. The price is similar. So which one?

The answer is usually: look at your soil. Beech on chalk. Hornbeam on clay. Mixed hedge if you're not sure. Everything else, the trimming schedule, the leaf retention, the wildlife value, is almost identical.

The Royal Horticultural Society's guide to planting hedges covers both species in the context of choosing the right plant for your boundary. Their film on the subject is a useful reference: How to plant a hedge and why it's better than a fence (The RHS, YouTube).

The winter leaf question

The dead-leaf retention is why most people choose either beech or hornbeam over a fully deciduous hedge like hawthorn or hazel. The phenomenon is called marcescence: the tree holds onto its dead leaves through winter rather than dropping them in autumn, which means the hedge stays private and somewhat windproof when other deciduous plants are bare.

Both species do this, but not absolutely. In mild Kent winters, young plants sometimes drop more leaves than older, established specimens. Beech tends to hold its leaves a fraction better in a very exposed, windy site. Hornbeam's winter leaves are slightly paler, more buff than copper, and the texture is coarser. Neither is a dramatic difference; from the other side of a boundary, both look like a solid brown hedge throughout winter.

Soil: the one decision that matters

In and around Sandwich, where the soils are largely sandy or chalky (the area sits on Thanet Sand and chalk), beech performs very well. Move to heavier inland sites, the gardens in Worth, Ash, Woodnesborough, and the clay-heavy parts of Eastry, and hornbeam is the safer choice.

Beech in waterlogged clay tends to establish poorly, grow slowly, and show leaf chlorosis (yellowing) as the roots sit in saturated soil. It does not die immediately, but it will never thrive. Hornbeam in the same conditions grows steadily and without complaint.

On free-draining chalk, the situation can reverse: hornbeam is tolerant enough to grow on chalk, but beech is very much at home there and often looks more vigorous.

If you are unsure of your soil type, dig a hole 30cm deep after heavy rain. If water pools and stays for more than a day, you have poor drainage and hornbeam is the better choice.

Trimming: the same for both

One main cut per year, in late July or August, suits both beech and hornbeam. This is after the nesting season is largely over but early enough for the hedge to harden off before winter. It also sets the new shoots that will carry the copper or buff leaves through the cold months.

Nesting season: the rule for both species

It is an offence to damage an active bird's nest. Both beech and hornbeam are used extensively as nesting sites by a range of garden birds. The nesting season runs roughly from 1 March to 31 August. The safe cutting window for both species is late July (once the bulk of nesting is over) through to the following February. Do not cut before late July without checking the hedge carefully first.

A second light tidy in late spring is sometimes done on very fast-growing specimens, but neither beech nor hornbeam usually needs it. They are slower and more manageable than privet or leylandii.

For detail on care of each species, see our beech hedge care guide and hornbeam hedge care guide.

Wildlife value

Both are good wildlife hedges, far better than non-native species. Beech produces mast (the small nuts inside prickly husks) that is eaten by woodpigeons, great tits, nuthatches and mammals including squirrels, dormice and badgers. Hornbeam produces small clustered nutlets that are a food source for hawfinch (one of East Kent's more exciting birds) and other finches.

Both species are native, so both support the invertebrates, caterpillars and other insects that insect-eating birds depend on. From a wildlife perspective, the choice between them is a wash. If you have a particular reason to attract hawfinch, hornbeam edges it slightly.

Cost and availability

Bare-root plants are the most economical option for both species and are widely available from UK nurseries from October through March. Prices fluctuate seasonally but are broadly similar: both typically cost in the range of 60p-£1.20 per plant for 40-60cm whips in a batch of 25 or more. Pot-grown plants are available year-round but cost noticeably more.

Both species need planting at 3 plants per metre in a single row. See our planting spacing guide for a full breakdown.

When to choose a mixed hedge

On a site where the soil varies, or where you simply cannot determine which way it will go, plant a mixed beech and hornbeam hedge. They clip at the same time, look very similar as a finished hedge, and each will thrive in whichever soil zone suits it. The subtle variation in leaf texture and colour from plant to plant adds to the natural look rather than detracting from it. This is often called a tapestry hedge, though in practice a 50/50 mix of just these two species is perfectly effective and less complicated than a multi-species native mix.
