Why does my beech hedge keep its dead leaves in winter?

Your beech hedge is almost certainly fine. There is a name for what it is doing, and it has been doing it for centuries.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Every autumn, we get a version of the same message from customers who are convinced their beech hedge is dying. The leaves have gone brown and crispy, but they are not falling off. The hedge looks, as one customer put it, "like a dead bush that doesn't know it yet".

It is not dying. It is doing something called marcescence, the retention of dead leaves on the plant through winter. It is entirely normal for clipped beech (Fagus sylvatica) and hornbeam (Carpinus betulus). The leaves will fall in spring when new growth pushes them off. They have been doing this for as long as beech hedges have been clipped, which is a very long time.

The decision tree: is this marcescence or something worse?

Most cases are straightforward marcescence. Work through these three questions to be sure.

Question 1: What colour are the dead leaves?

Copper-brown to russet: this is almost certainly normal marcescence. Continue to Question 2.

Pale straw, whitish or patchy: leaves that did not colour up properly may indicate drought stress from the previous summer or a nutrient problem. Jump to "Symptoms that need investigation" below.

Question 2: Is the hedge regularly clipped?

Yes, trimmed once or twice a year: this confirms marcescence. Regular clipping maintains juvenile growth characteristics in the hedge, and juvenile beech and hornbeam retain their dead leaves through winter as a normal feature of that growth stage. No action is needed.

No, the hedge has been neglected or never formally clipped: marcescence is still possible in younger or smaller growth, but is less reliable as a diagnosis. Check for other symptoms below.

Question 3: Is the whole hedge uniformly coloured, or are there bare dead sections?

Uniform copper-brown throughout: healthy marcescence. Nothing to do.

Bare patches with dark woody stems and no leaf retention: this is not marcescence. Check for honey fungus, root disturbance, waterlogging or severe drought stress. See our hedge health troubleshooting guide for the next steps.

What marcescence actually is

Beech (Fagus sylvatica) and hornbeam (Carpinus betulus) retain their dead leaves through winter by maintaining a weak, incomplete abscission layer between the leaf stalk and the stem. In a mature, unclipped beech tree, this happens only in the youngest growth at the branch tips. The older wood drops its leaves normally in autumn.

In a clipped hedge, the story is different. Regular trimming keeps the plant in a permanent state of juvenile growth by constantly stimulating new shoots. Because the surface of the hedge is almost entirely young growth, most or all of it retains leaves through winter. The more consistently the hedge has been clipped, the more pronounced the effect.

The leaves fall in spring when the new season's growth pushes them off, typically March to April in East Kent. In the mild coastal climate around Deal and Sandwich Bay, where hard frosts are rare and springs can be warm, the dead leaves can persist right into late April. This is not unusual and is not a cause for concern.

The ornamental bonus

Many gardeners actually value the winter effect. A beech hedge in December is copper against a grey sky, which is much more interesting than bare sticks. The dead leaves also trap air, which means the hedge continues to function as a windbreak even in mid-winter when a deciduous hedge would otherwise be transparent.

This is one of the reasons beech is often preferred over hornbeam for formal hedging in domestic gardens, despite hornbeam being better adapted to wet soils. Hornbeam shares the same marcescent quality, and its winter leaves are a similar colour, but the effect is generally less reliable in hornbeam, particularly on older or slower-grown plants.

Symptoms that genuinely need investigation

Not every brown hedge in winter is marcescence. These symptoms are worth taking seriously:

  • Pale straw or whitish leaves, not copper: leaves that failed to colour up normally in autumn can indicate drought stress during the previous summer, a late-season water shortage, or in some cases a nutrient deficiency. Check whether there was a prolonged dry period in July or August. Beech hedges in lighter soils, including the chalk soils around Sandwich and Eastry, are more vulnerable to summer drought than those on heavier clay.
  • Bare sections with dark woody stems: bare patches where leaves are absent and stems are dark or dead are not marcescence. They could indicate honey fungus, root disturbance from nearby building work, compaction from vehicles parked against the hedge, or severe drought. See our guide to honey fungus in hedges for one of the more common culprits.
  • A hedge that has always held leaves but suddenly does not: a beech or hornbeam hedge that reliably held its winter leaves for years but is now dropping them early may have a root problem. This is worth investigating, particularly if there has been recent ground disturbance nearby.

Does it need any action?

Usually no. Do not try to rake or blow off the dead leaves while they are still firmly attached. You will damage the young growth at the branch tips and achieve very little. The hedge will shed them naturally in spring when new buds push them off.

If you want to tidy things up in late March or early April, once the new buds are visibly swelling and the old leaves are starting to loosen, a gentle pass with a leaf blower along the hedge face will dislodge them. Do not force it any earlier in the year.

When to cut a beech hedge

For what it is worth: the dead winter leaves do not affect when you should cut the hedge. The best time to cut beech in East Kent is late July or August, once the new growth from spring has firmed up and before the next nesting season really ends. This timing also means the cut edges harden off before autumn and the hedge goes into winter looking sharp.

For full details on timing, tools and technique, see our beech hedge care guide.
