Bare-root vs pot-grown hedge plants

Both work. One is cheaper, one is more flexible. Which you should use depends on what you are planting, when you are planting it, and how much you want to spend.

Updated August 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Bare-root hedging plants are lifted from the ground during the dormant season, with no soil attached to the roots, and sold cheaply in bundles. Pot-grown plants are grown in containers and sold with their root ball intact. You will often find both at a garden centre, with the pot-grown plants looking larger and more impressive and costing several times more per plant.

For a long run of new hedging, the price difference matters. A 25-metre beech or hawthorn hedge at pot-grown prices is a significant outlay. The same hedge from bare-root stock costs a fraction of that, and in most cases establishes just as well. The RHS's guide "How to plant bare root hedges | Grow at Home" (youtube.com/@The_RHS) is a good practical walkthrough of the bare-root planting process and the soil preparation that makes the real difference.

Comparison at a glance

Bare root Pot grown
Cost Low (typically £0.50 to £2.50 per plant for common species) Higher (typically £3 to £10+ per plant depending on size and species)
Planting window November to March only (dormant season) Any time, though autumn and early spring are best
Handling Must be planted within a few days of delivery; heel in or soak roots if delayed Can wait in pot for weeks before planting
Species availability Best for deciduous species: hawthorn, beech, hornbeam, hazel, blackthorn, field maple, privet All species, including evergreens: laurel, yew, leylandii, griselinia, escallonia, photinia
Appearance at planting Small, leafless stems; looks underwhelming Leafy, established-looking; visual result is more immediate
Establishment Fast once over transplant shock; roots grow outward freely Good, but roots take longer to transition from pot shape
Best for Long new runs, native hedges, budget-conscious planting Gap filling, evergreens, summer planting, smaller quantities

When bare root is the better choice

Planting a long new hedge run

If you are starting a hedge from scratch along 20 metres or more of boundary, bare root is almost always the most sensible option on cost alone. Common hedging species (hawthorn, blackthorn, beech, hornbeam, privet) planted at 3 plants per metre for a single row means 60 or more plants for that length. At pot-grown prices, that adds up quickly. The same plants bare-root can cost a fifth to a tenth of the price.

For this type of planting, bare root also gives you a fresh-start advantage: you plant the roots without any pot-restriction, the plants establish a free root system from day one, and by the following spring the hedge looks surprisingly advanced.

Native hedges for wildlife

Most of the species used in a classic mixed native hedge (hawthorn, blackthorn, field maple, hazel, dog rose, spindle, elder) are deciduous and available cheaply bare-root from native plant nurseries and online hedging suppliers from November. For a wildlife hedge like the ones described in our guide to planting a native hedge in Kent, bare-root stock is both cheaper and more representative of the species you actually want.

November to March planting window

Kent's mild coastal climate means the ground usually remains workable well into January and February. In most winters, a bare-root planting planned for December or January can go ahead without problems. The mild winters in Sandwich, Deal and the surrounding villages are an advantage here: soil that would be frozen solid in northern England can still be dug comfortably.

When pot grown is the better choice

Filling gaps in an existing hedge

If you have a single plant that has died in an established hedge and you need to fill the gap, a pot-grown plant matches the size of neighbouring plants more closely and can be planted at almost any time of year. A bare-root plant in the same gap will look undersized for two or three seasons.

Evergreen species

Evergreen hedging plants do not go fully dormant, so bare-root handling is harder on them. Leylandii, laurel (cherry and Portuguese), yew, griselinia, escallonia, photinia and box are all better sourced in containers. For a coastal garden in Sandwich Bay or Deal where a salt-tolerant evergreen like griselinia is the right choice, pot-grown from a local garden centre is the practical option.

Anytime planting

If you decide in June that you need a hedge this season, bare-root is not an option: the stock does not exist until autumn. Pot-grown gives you the flexibility to plant when it suits you, with the caveat that summer planting on the free-draining sandy soils of East Kent requires watering support until the plants establish.

Buying tips for Kent gardens

For bare-root hedging, online mail-order suppliers typically offer the widest species selection and ship nationwide from November onwards. Large bundles (25 or 50 plants) are common and keep the per-plant cost low. Look for suppliers who grade their plants by height so you know what you are getting.

For pot-grown evergreens, local garden centres in and around Deal, Sandwich and Canterbury generally carry griselinia, photinia, laurel and leylandii through spring and summer. If you need a specific size or a less-common species (escallonia, box, thuja), calling ahead to check stock before driving out saves a wasted trip.

On soil preparation: the sandy soils around Sandwich Bay and the coastal strip near Deal and Walmer drain freely and have little natural organic matter. For any hedge planting on these soils, improving the trench with garden compost or well-rotted manure before planting is particularly worthwhile. See our guide to caring for a newly planted hedge for the first-year watering and mulching routine that makes the real difference.

A note on spacing

Bare-root hedging is often planted at three plants per metre in a single row, or two plants per metre in a double staggered row for a thicker hedge faster. Pot-grown plants are typically larger at purchase and can be planted at two per metre or even one per metre for the quickest screening, at higher cost. Whatever spacing you choose, plant to the depth of the original root collar (the point where roots meet stem) and firm the soil well around each plant to eliminate air pockets.
