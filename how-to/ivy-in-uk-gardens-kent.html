Ivy in UK gardens: wildlife benefits and how to manage it

Ivy gets a bad press. It climbs hedges, scales walls and can engulf a fence in a season. But for wildlife, particularly in the coastal landscape of East Kent, a well-managed ivy plant is one of the most valuable things in a garden.

Updated September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

The RHS has documented the ecological role of ivy at their garden at Wisley extensively. In their video "The holly and the ivy at RHS Garden Wisley" (youtube.com/watch?v=QgNt4B2mtB4), published December 2020, the case for keeping ivy in the garden is made plainly: it is not an aggressive parasite but a native climber with a seasonal rhythm that fills gaps in the garden calendar that very few other plants cover. The RHS channel, with over 100,000 subscribers, is the authoritative UK horticultural voice on this question.

This does not mean ivy needs no management. It does. But the decision about how hard to manage it, or whether to remove it entirely, is worth making on the facts rather than the instinct to tidy.

What ivy does for wildlife

Ivy's value peaks in autumn and winter, which is exactly when most other food and shelter sources for wildlife have gone. Three things make it particularly useful in a Kent garden:

  • Late-season nectar source. Ivy flowers from September through November, later than almost any other UK native plant. The nectar is vital for ivy bees (Colletes hederae), red admiral butterflies and several hoverfly species that are still active in the cooler months. For bees preparing for winter, there may be no comparable alternative in the garden.
  • Winter berries for birds. The black berries ripen through winter into early spring. Blackbirds, thrushes, robins and woodpigeons all feed on them. In a mild Sandwich winter, an established ivy on a south-facing fence or hedge can sustain blackbirds through the coldest weeks.
  • Year-round shelter and nesting sites. Dense ivy cover provides nesting habitat for wrens, robins, blackbirds and song thrushes, as well as winter roost sites for several bat species and overwintering sites for butterflies such as the brimstone. Hedgehogs will use dense ground-level ivy for hibernation cover.

Ivy and the nesting season

Ivy is used as a nesting site from March through August, the same window as hedges under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981. Dense established ivy is also used by wrens and robins as winter roost cover and by bats as hibernation roosts, potentially throughout the year. Before cutting back ivy, particularly mature growth on walls or deep within hedges, check carefully for active nests or roosts. Disturbing a bat roost is a separate offence under the Conservation of Habitats Regulations 2017 regardless of the time of year.

Does ivy damage hedges?

This is the most common question we get about ivy in hedges, and the answer depends on the situation. The comparison below covers the four most common cases we encounter across Sandwich, Deal and the East Kent villages.

Situation Effect of ivy What to do
Ivy threading through a dense, healthy established hedge (hawthorn, blackthorn, holly, hornbeam) Minimal. The ivy occupies gaps and climbs using the hedge as support. Vigorous hedge species will not be suppressed by it. No action needed. Trim lightly in October or November to stop it obscuring the hedge top.
Ivy on a struggling or thinning hedge (bare-legged privet, laurel losing density) Can accelerate decline. The hedge is already weakened and competing with ivy for light and water slows recovery. Reduce ivy significantly while the hedge is being renovated. Replant bare sections first.
Ivy reaching the hedge top and spilling over the outer face Can shade out the hedge top and exterior face over several seasons. Adds significant weight to the hedge profile in wind. Cut ivy back to below the hedge top each October or November, once the berries are forming.
Ivy growing at the base of a hedge on a north-facing aspect Usually beneficial. Ivy covers bare legs, adds ground-level shelter, and does not compete significantly with the hedge canopy above. Leave it. Trim only to contain lateral spread.

Managing vs removing: choosing the right approach

Removing ivy entirely is a significant intervention. Established ivy has an extensive root system and responds to cutting by sending up new shoots vigorously. If the aim is to reduce the volume of a plant that is overreaching into a hedge or onto a roof, regular cutting back is usually more effective than a single hard removal, and it preserves the base plant for wildlife over winter.

The case for full removal is narrower: where ivy is climbing into gutters or under roof tiles, where it is growing into a structural wall with lime mortar (cement-mortared brick is fine), or where a specific allergen concern is present. Ivy sap can cause contact dermatitis in sensitive people. Wear waterproof gloves for all ivy-cutting work, and long sleeves for any overhead cutting.

When to cut back ivy

The best window for cutting back ivy in a Kent garden is October and November. By October the nesting season for most species has ended, the ivy berries are forming and will have some time to ripen before a hard cut removes them, and the plant still has time to push some regrowth before the coldest weather arrives.

What to avoid:

  • March to August: Nesting season. Check thoroughly before any work. If there are bats present, do not cut at all without licence advice.
  • September: Ivy is in full flower and ivy bees are foraging. Hold off if possible, or cut only sections not in active flower.
  • December to January: Berries are at their most valuable for birds in winter. Cutting now removes the food source at the worst time.

Practical management: keeping growth in check

Ivy growing up a hedge can be controlled with a single annual cut in late October or early November. Remove growth that has reached the hedge top, cut back any strands beginning to smother the outer hedge face, and tidy up particularly dense lateral spread at ground level. This takes twenty minutes on most domestic hedges and preserves all the wildlife value while preventing the ivy from outcompeting the hedge over time.

For ivy on walls or fences, the same once-a-year approach works. Keep growth away from gutters, window frames and mortar joints. On a brick wall with cement mortar, established ivy presents no structural concern and can be largely left alone. On lime-mortared stone walls, keep ivy off the wall surface itself and manage any penetration into the joints.

Safety when cutting ivy

  • Wear waterproof gloves throughout: ivy sap (falcarinol compounds in the Araliaceae family) is a contact irritant. Change gloves if you touch your face.
  • Use eye protection for any overhead work or when cutting towards your face.
  • For ivy on walls above head height, use a pole trimmer rather than a ladder where possible. If a ladder is necessary, have someone footing it and never overreach sideways.
  • Check for wasp nests before reaching into dense ivy, particularly in late summer and early autumn when nests are at their largest.
