How to train a lollipop topiary standard

A pair of bay lollipops flanking a doorway, or a single standard in a pot on the patio, is a classic East Kent garden feature. Here is the eight-step checklist for training one from scratch, or tidying one up if it has lost its shape.

Updated September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

The eight-step checklist

  1. Choose the right species for your position and desired size.
  2. Buy or select a single-stemmed plant, or reduce a bushy one to a single leader.
  3. Decide on your clear-stem height and mark it, typically 90-120 cm for a garden standard.
  4. Remove all side shoots from the stem below that height, flush to the bark, every few weeks as they appear.
  5. Let the top grow freely in the first season while the stem thickens and establishes.
  6. Begin shaping the head in season two: remove the longest shoots to start encouraging a sphere.
  7. Clip the head twice a year, late May and late August, to maintain the shape.
  8. Feed in spring, water in dry spells, and re-pot container plants every two to three years.

The bay lollipop pruning technique is demonstrated in a video from the Gardening With Alan Titchmarsh YouTube channel (video ID: VL0RfQU7zeY), published October 2020. Alan Titchmarsh is one of the UK's most recognised gardening television presenters, and the video shows the same clear-stem method described below. The steps in this guide follow the same approach, adapted for the range of species we see in Kent gardens.

Clipping and the nesting season

A lollipop standard on a patio or in a pot is unlikely to harbour nesting birds, but a large specimen in a garden border or against a wall may well do. Before clipping any topiary in spring or early summer, part the outer growth and look inside the head. Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to damage or destroy an active nest. Nesting season runs from 1 March to 31 August. If you find a nest with eggs or young, put the clippers away and wait. The safe clipping windows are late May once most first broods have fledged (and if no nest is present), and late August once the season has closed.

Safety: clipping a standard

  • Eye protection is essential when clipping closely. Small shoots spring back unpredictably and clippings fly.
  • Use sharp, clean blades. Blunt shears tear rather than cut, leaving ragged edges that brown and die back. See our sharpening guide.
  • If using a step ladder to reach the top of a tall standard, have someone steady the base and do not overreach.
  • If you use an electric trimmer for the head, use an RCD adaptor and trail the cable behind you, away from the cutting line.
  • Sap from bay and laurel can irritate skin: wear gloves and wash hands after working.

Step 1: choose the right species

Bay laurel (Laurus nobilis) is the most popular choice in East Kent. It is evergreen, aromatic, and its leaves are useful in cooking. Bay is reasonably hardy but dislikes exposed positions: in a spot that gets strong north or east winds, a sheltered corner or a pot that can be moved in winter is preferable. Bay grows at a moderate pace, about 20-30 cm a year once established, and makes a head of up to a metre across on a mature standard.

Portuguese laurel (Prunus lusitanica) is tougher than bay in exposed or cold positions, grows slightly faster, and makes a denser head. Its dark green leaves and red leaf stalks make it an attractive plant in its own right. It responds very well to clipping and can be shaped into a tighter, more formal sphere than bay.

Box (Buxus sempervirens) is slower but makes an exceptionally crisp and dense sphere. Check plants for box tree moth caterpillar damage before buying: this pest is now widespread in Kent and can defoliate a plant rapidly. See our box tree moth guide.

Holly, yew, and Lonicera nitida all work well but are much slower to form the clear stem and head. For pots or instant impact, buy a ready-formed standard from a nursery and concentrate on maintaining the shape.

Step 2: choose or create a single-stemmed plant

Look for a plant with a single dominant central stem rather than multiple shoots from the base. If you have a bushy young bay or laurel in a pot that you want to convert, identify the strongest, most upright stem, and remove all competing stems from the base in late March or April. The plant will initially look odd, but the selected stem will thicken quickly once it is no longer competing.

If the plant was already sold as a single-stemmed standard, this step is done. Skip to step 3.

Step 3: decide on your clear-stem height

A clear stem of 90-120 cm suits most garden situations: enough height to walk or see under the head, but not so tall that the plant needs constant staking. For a smaller patio pot, a half-standard with a 60-75 cm stem looks more in proportion. For a large pair flanking a gate or drive entrance, 120-150 cm works well.

Mark the height with a piece of string tied loosely around the stem if it helps you stay consistent. Whatever you decide, do not change it later: removing a section of established bark or trunk is not possible, and raising the head once the stem has grown beyond your original height is a permanent decision.

Step 4: clear the stem

Remove all side shoots growing from the stem below your chosen clear-stem height. Cut flush to the bark with clean secateurs, do not leave stubs. Do this every two to three weeks through the growing season, from March to September: bay and laurel in particular throw out new side shoots rapidly and persistently from the stem, and each one left to develop thickens at the base and leaves a bump on the trunk when you do eventually remove it.

In the first year, be patient. The stem needs time to thicken and toughen. Resist the urge to remove growth from the top of the plant: let the head develop naturally while you are working on clearing the stem below.

Step 5: let the head grow freely in year one

In the first full growing season, do not clip the head at all. Leave all the growth above your chosen clear-stem height to develop freely. This builds a framework of branches that will support the eventual shape, and it puts energy into thickening the stem below.

Stake the plant firmly with a single bamboo cane tied loosely at the top, just below the first branches. The knot should be loose enough to let the stem flex in wind (this strengthens the stem) but firm enough to prevent snapping. Check the tie monthly and loosen it if the stem is being constricted.

Step 6: begin shaping in year two

In the second growing season, the head will have enough material to begin shaping. In late May or early June, stand back and look at the overall shape. Identify the longest shoots that are taking the head beyond the sphere you want, and cut them back to just above a node. Work around the plant gradually, rather than taking everything off at once.

Do not try to cut to a perfect sphere immediately. Rough-trim to the approximate shape in year two and refine in year three. Bay and laurel have large leaves that will brown at the cut edges if you slice through them with shears; use secateurs for the first clip and hand shears for subsequent maintenance once there is enough small growth to clip without cutting through individual leaves.

Step 7: clip twice a year to maintain

Once the head is established, two clips a year maintain the shape well. Clip in late May and again in late August. Each clip should take off the soft new growth that has extended the sphere since the previous cut.

Work methodically, going around the plant in the same direction each time. Step back every few minutes and view the plant from several angles: it is easy to take more off one side without noticing. A bamboo cane held at arm's length as a rough visual gauge helps keep the sphere round.

For bay, use sharp hand shears or secateurs rather than a mechanical trimmer if you want clean leaf cuts. For Portuguese laurel and box, a small trimmer or hand shears both work well. Always sharpen blades before starting and wipe them clean between plants if you have more than one.

Step 8: feeding, watering and re-potting

Feed with a balanced slow-release granular fertiliser in March, before growth begins, and again in June. A liquid feed high in potassium in August helps harden new growth before winter. Do not feed after August or the plant produces soft new growth that will be damaged by the first frost.

Container-grown standards need watering regularly from April through September. In the dry East Kent summers, check the compost daily and water when the top few centimetres are dry. A plant that wilts badly and recovers often develops brown patches in the head that take years to fill in.

Re-pot into a container one size larger every two to three years, or when roots are visibly escaping from the drainage holes. Use a John Innes No. 3 compost mixed with 20% horticultural grit for good drainage. A heavy terracotta pot suits bay and laurel grown as patio standards: the weight provides stability against wind.

Species-specific notes for Kent gardens

Bay laurel

Protect from prolonged hard frosts. If you are on an exposed site near the coast (Sandwich Bay, the open fields towards Deal) and a severe frost is forecast, wrap the pot in horticultural fleece and move it against a south-facing wall. Bay recovers from moderate frost damage if the roots are protected, but a hard freeze that gets into the stem can kill the plant back to the ground.

Portuguese laurel

Much hardier than bay and well-suited to exposed positions. Tolerates coastal conditions and alkaline chalk soils, which makes it a natural fit for many East Kent gardens. Clips tightly and holds its shape between cuts better than bay.

Box

Check for box tree moth caterpillar (Cydalima perspectalis) each spring and summer. In Kent the moth is now well established and a single generation of caterpillars can strip a lollipop head completely within a fortnight. See our identification and treatment guide. Do not clip in damp or wet conditions: box blight (Cylindrocladium buxicola) spreads rapidly on wet foliage.

A lollipop that has lost its shape is almost always recoverable. Cut it back hard, let it re-grow, and start again. The stem is the work of years; the head is only one or two seasons.
