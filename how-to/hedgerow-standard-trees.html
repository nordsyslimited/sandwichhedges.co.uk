Hedgerow standard trees: letting some hedge stems grow tall

Every old Kentish hedgerow used to carry a tree every 20 metres or so: an oak, a field maple, an ash. Garden hedges lost them decades ago. Getting one back takes a selection, a tag, and the discipline to leave it alone.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Six-step checklist: growing a hedgerow standard

  1. Choose an appropriate species: oak, field maple, ash, wild cherry or hazel.
  2. Select a strong stem, at least 1.5 cm diameter at the base, growing upright.
  3. Tag it and exclude it from the next annual cut and every one after.
  4. Cut competing stems around it back hard for the first two or three years.
  5. Stake it lightly in an exposed position for the first growing season.
  6. Leave it. Check once a year. Be patient: a good standard takes a decade to establish.

What a hedgerow standard is

In the old tradition of British hedge management, the person laying or maintaining a hedge would deliberately spare one stem per chain (roughly every 20 metres) and allow it to grow on into a full tree. These were the hedgerow standards: boundary oaks, field maples, ashes, wild cherries and hazels that gave the English countryside its distinctive tree-lined feel.

Most farm hedges have had their standards removed or killed by repeated hard cutting over the past 60 years. Garden hedges, trimmed annually to tight box shapes, never had them. The practical result is a hedge that looks neat but provides a fraction of the wildlife habitat that a hedge with occasional standards can sustain.

Standards are not a management problem to be solved. They are the point. An oak growing from a hedge base will eventually support over 300 species of insects, which in turn supports a food chain stretching from blue tits to tawny owls. Field maple standards draw pollinators in spring and turn vivid orange-gold in October. Even a hazel standard, allowed to grow into a multi-stem canopy tree, creates a cathedral of nesting habitat above the hedge line.

Step 1: choose an appropriate species

Not every hedge species is suitable for growing on into a standard. You are looking for a woody plant that has the genetic programme to grow into a full-size tree given the chance.

Oak (Quercus robur): the best choice for wildlife, and the traditional Kentish hedgerow tree. Very slow but very long-lived. An oak from a hedge base in Sandwich could still be there in 400 years. The key constraint is space: a mature oak in an open field reaches 20 metres and more, so only choose oak for a boundary where you can tolerate a large tree long-term.

Field maple (Acer campestre): the best choice for a smaller garden. Native, fast-growing to 8-15 metres, tolerant of the chalk and clay soils that run through the parishes around Ash and Woodnesborough, and more manageable than an oak. Field maple hedges are common here; selecting a stem to grow on is the natural next step.

Ash (Fraxinus excelsior): fast-growing and a key bat foraging tree. Ash dieback (Hymenoscyphus fraxineus) has changed the picture, and new ash standards should be planted with caution. If you have an ash growing in your hedge that is showing no signs of dieback symptoms, allowing it to develop is still worthwhile; a mature ash before dieback arrives provides significant habitat value.

Wild cherry (Prunus avium): spectacular in late April, with heavy blossom that feeds early pollinators. Grows to 10-20 metres. Unsuitable for boundary hedges close to buildings or drains because of its root spread.

Hazel (Corylus avellana): naturally multi-stem and more suited to a coppice stool than a single standard. But a neglected hazel allowed to grow without cutting will form a substantial canopy tree. The catkins appear in January, well before most other flowering plants, and provide early pollen for queen bumblebees.

Species to avoid as standards: leylandii, laurel, privet. These are managed hedge plants and will not form useful habitat trees. A privet allowed to grow unpruned becomes a large, ungainly shrub rather than a canopy tree.

Step 2: select the right stem

Walk your hedge in late autumn, when you can see the structure clearly. You are looking for a stem that is already growing more vertically than its neighbours, that has at least 1.5 cm diameter at the base (it has already survived a few cycles), and that is positioned where a mature tree would be appropriate: not directly against a structure, not above a utility run, not in a spot where it would block a sight-line or access gate.

Mark it with a coloured tag or a twist of orange baling twine at eye height so that anyone cutting the hedge knows to avoid it. If you share hedge maintenance with a neighbour or contractor, make absolutely sure they know which stem is the standard-in-waiting before any cut starts.

Step 3: withdraw it from the annual cut

The first and most important step: do nothing to that stem. Not a tip trim. Not a tidy-up. Nothing. The hedge around it will continue to be cut, but the standard stem grows on at whatever pace the species permits.

In the first year after selection, most garden hedge owners find the growing stem looks odd and out of place. This is normal. Resist the urge to tidy it. By year three, it is clearly a young tree rather than a rogue shoot, and the visual awkwardness has passed.

Nesting season: 1 March to 31 August

Under the UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting runs roughly 1 March to 31 August. Your growing standard will attract nesting birds more quickly than the surrounding hedge: check the immediate area around the stem before any trimming of adjacent hedge sections.

Step 4: reduce competition

For the first two or three years, cut the hedge stems immediately adjacent to the standard back slightly harder than the rest of the hedge. You are trying to create a little extra light and space at the base of the growing tree without leaving a gap in the boundary.

Once the standard is above the hedge line (typically 2-3 metres), this management becomes unnecessary: the tree is growing in a different structural layer and is no longer competing with the hedge for the same light and space.

Step 5: staking in exposed positions

A young standard growing from a hedge base in an exposed position (seafront boundaries at Sandwich Bay, for example, or an open arable field edge near Worth) can suffer root rock in gale-force winds before it has established a stable root system. A single short stake, one metre high, placed 15 cm from the stem with a figure-eight tie, prevents rocking without restricting growth. Remove it after the first full growing season.

Most garden positions do not need staking. If the standard is sheltered on two sides by the hedge itself, it will establish unaided.

Step 6: long-term patience

A field maple standard visible from the end of a garden in Eastry or Ash will be a noticeable feature within five years, a real tree in ten, and a habitat asset within fifteen. An oak will be there for centuries. The investment is time, not money or labour.

Once established, the standard largely manages itself. You may need to remove a few low branches that interfere with the hedge maintenance work as the tree grows, but otherwise it is a background presence. Review its condition as part of the annual hedge check: look for significant dead wood, structural weakness after storms, or signs of disease. See our guide to hedge storm damage for how to assess boundary tree failures.

Why it matters: the PTES view on hedgerow management

The People's Trust for Endangered Species (PTES) and WoodlandsTV produced a detailed film in February 2025 on management approaches for healthy hedgerows, including the case for incorporating tall hedgerow trees. Their core message is that hedges managed with a combination of light cutting, occasional rest years, and retained standards support far more wildlife than those cut uniformly hard every year. The film covers four techniques: reducing cut frequency, incremental rather than hard cutting, rejuvenation by coppicing and laying, and growing standard trees from the hedge.
