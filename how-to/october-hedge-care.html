October hedge care in Kent

October is one of the best cutting months of the year. Here is what to do, species by species, before the season closes.

Updated September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

September gets a lot of attention as the main cutting month. Rightly so. But October is arguably better for quite a few hedge types, and it is the last sensible window before the first frosts close things down for the evergreens.

This is what we work through every October, across gardens in Sandwich, Deal, Worth, Ash, Woodnesborough, Eastry and Sandwich Bay.

Nesting season: ended, but worth a quick check

The UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 protects active bird nests: the main season runs roughly 1 March to 31 August. By October, nesting is over for virtually all species. However, wood pigeons sometimes have a late brood. Before cutting, if you notice a pigeon flying repeatedly into the same spot, have a look first. An active nest, however late, is still protected. If the hedge is clearly empty, carry on.

Before you start: safety checklist

  • Eye protection: flying debris from hedge trimmers causes more eye injuries than people expect. Wear safety glasses or goggles.
  • Ear defenders: for petrol or electric trimmers used for more than a few minutes.
  • Gloves: sturdy gardening gloves at minimum; cut-resistant gloves for anything spiny (holly, berberis, pyracantha).
  • RCD protection: if using a corded electric trimmer, plug it into an RCD adaptor.
  • Ladder caution: most garden accidents involve ladders. For tall hedges, a long-reach or pole trimmer from the ground is always preferable. If a ladder is unavoidable, have someone footing it and never overreach sideways.
  • Check for wasp nests: late-season wasps are at their most aggressive in September and October. Before starting, spend a moment watching for wasp activity around the hedge.

Species by species: what to do in October

Yew

October is the ideal month for yew. The annual shaping cut on established yew hedges should be done between late August and mid-October. Do it now and the hedge will have hardened off before the cold arrives. Yew tolerates hard cuts well and will respond with dense regrowth in spring. Do not cut beyond mid-October, as fresh cuts on yew can brown in a hard frost.

For large renovation cuts on an overgrown yew, the dormant season (November to February) is better: yew is one of the few conifers that regenerates from old brown wood, so hard renovation is possible, just time it for dormancy.

Holly

October is good for holly. The annual cut on holly hedges is best done in late September or October, before the berries are formed and set. If you want to keep the berries for winter wildlife value (which is well worth doing in East Kent), leave the berry-bearing branches on female holly plants and cut the rest. Holly is tough and handles an October cut well.

Beech and hornbeam

The annual cut on beech and hornbeam is ideally done in late July or August, when the new season's growth has hardened. If you missed that window, early October is acceptable. Cut before the leaves turn fully copper: once the hedge has moved into autumn colour, the growth has stopped for the year and it is better to wait until full dormancy in November or December for any remaining tidying. One cut a year is all beech and hornbeam need.

Privet

Privet should have its main cut by the end of October at the latest in most Kent years. Privet is semi-evergreen and keeps growing into autumn, so an October cut keeps it looking sharp going into winter. Do not leave it until November: a fresh privet cut hit by an early frost can brown at the tips. In the milder coastal strip between Sandwich Bay and Deal, you can sometimes push this to early November, but it is a risk.

Photinia (Red Robin)

Photinia can be trimmed through the season, but the final cut should be done by mid-October. The new red growth photinia produces after cutting is what makes it attractive, but fresh soft growth is frost-sensitive. An early October cut gives the new growth three to four weeks to harden off before the first real frosts. Cutting later than mid-October is not recommended.

Laurel (cherry laurel and Portuguese laurel)

October is a reasonable cut month for laurel, which is hardier than photinia or privet and less sensitive to a light frost. That said, freshly cut laurel is more susceptible than established growth, so aim to finish laurel cuts before the end of October. Use secateurs rather than shears for a tidy finish, or accept the shredded-leaf appearance from mechanical trimming. Large-leaved cherry laurel looks noticeably better cut with secateurs or loppers on individual shoots.

Leylandii and conifers (lawson cypress, thuja)

Be quick. The cutting window for conifers runs from May to September. By October, you are already pushing it. If the leylandii or lawson cypress has not been cut by early October, do it immediately: cut surfaces on conifers need time to harden before cold damp weather arrives, and autumn cuts that are caught by early frosts can produce persistent brown patches. Never cut conifers back into brown wood: they do not regenerate from there.

If October is nearly done and the conifer is still uncut, leave it until spring (April to May) rather than risk a cold-weather browning.

Box

Box should ideally not be cut after mid-September: fresh cuts on box are vulnerable to both frost and the fungal conditions (damp, cool air) that encourage box blight. If a light tidy is genuinely necessary in early October, do it only on a dry day and ensure good airflow around the plant. In a wet October, leave box alone entirely until late spring.

Hawthorn, blackthorn and field maple

These deciduous natives are heading towards dormancy in October. The standard approach for hawthorn and blackthorn is to wait until they are fully dormant (November to February) for renovation cuts, where you can see the structure clearly. A light tidy of the sides in October is fine but not necessary: deciduous hedges kept as wildlife boundaries often benefit from leaving the berries and seed heads intact through winter.

Hazel

Hazel in a formal hedge context can be trimmed in October. For hazel left as a wildlife hedge or managed for catkins in winter, hold off until late January or February after the catkins have finished.

Other October tasks

Mulch the base

Once cutting is done, apply a 5 to 7 cm layer of bark chip or well-rotted garden compost along the base of the hedge, kept back from the stems themselves. In the free-draining sandy and chalk soils common around Sandwich and Eastry, this is particularly worthwhile: a mulch layer retains moisture through the winter dry spells and insulates roots from hard frosts. Skip it only on clay soils that stay waterlogged, where additional moisture retention is not needed.

Clear clippings from the base

Clippings left at the base of the hedge through winter create ideal conditions for fungal disease to carry over. Clear them away and compost them (most clippings compost well; box clippings should be hot-composted or disposed of rather than left in a garden heap).

Plan bare-root orders for November

October is the planning month for bare-root hedging. The bare-root season opens in November, and the most popular species (privet, hawthorn, beech, hornbeam) sell out at the best suppliers. If you have gaps in an existing hedge or are planning a new one, order now rather than in December. Bare-root plants are significantly cheaper than pot-grown and establish just as well when planted correctly.

Note what needs professional attention

October is also the time to assess what needs doing over winter. Significant reductions, renovation cuts on deciduous species, and hedge-laying are all better done from November onwards. Make a note now while the hedge is fresh in your mind, and book the work for January or February, when most contractors have more flexibility.
