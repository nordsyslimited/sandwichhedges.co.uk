How to renovate an overgrown laurel hedge: cherry and Portuguese laurel

Laurel is one of the most forgiving hedging plants for renovation. Both cherry laurel and Portuguese laurel can be cut back hard, even to apparently bare old wood, and will push vigorous new growth within a single growing season. If your laurel has got out of hand, this is the guide for bringing it back.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Laurel renovation: eight points before you start

  1. Confirm the species: Cherry laurel (Prunus laurocerasus) or Portuguese laurel (Prunus lusitanica)? Both tolerate hard cutting, but cherry laurel is more vigorous and needs different tools.
  2. Date check: renovation outside the nesting season (1 March to 31 August) where possible; best windows are late September to February and mid to late August.
  3. How much needs to come off? Note the target finished height and width before you start, and mark it with canes along the hedge.
  4. Tool choice: use secateurs or loppers for cherry laurel, not a hedge trimmer; cut leaves go brown at the edges. Trimmers are acceptable on Portuguese laurel (smaller leaves).
  5. Clear the base: remove stones, old wire and debris along the full length before any powered work starts.
  6. Waste plan: laurel clippings are very bulky and the leaves take one to two years to compost; arrange disposal before you cut.
  7. PPE: eye protection, ear defenders for powered tools, cut-resistant gloves; cherry laurel berries and leaves contain hydrocyanic compounds, so wash hands before eating.
  8. If the hedge is dead in sections: treat dead and living sections separately; see our guide on overgrown and gappy hedge renovation.

Laurel hedges are some of the most common we work on across the Sandwich area. Many have been let grow beyond their original shape over years, and their owners assume the job is impossible. It rarely is. The two Prunus laurels, cherry and Portuguese, both have the same underlying characteristic: they will push strong new growth from old wood, including from stems that look entirely bare. The renovation process is straightforward once you understand the tool rule and the timing.

The nesting season check

The UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs roughly from 1 March to 31 August. Renovation cuts on laurel hedges should ideally fall outside this window. The best windows are late September through February (for the main structural cuts) and from mid-August for work that can be completed before early September. If you need to cut during the nesting season, check the full length of the hedge carefully on both sides before starting. See our guide on wildlife-friendly hedge trimming for the full legal detail.

Before you start: safety

  • Eye protection: mandatory. Laurel renovation involves cutting through thick stems with loppers or a saw, and debris travels.
  • Ear defenders: for any powered work, including chainsaws and reciprocating saws on large stems.
  • Gloves: cut-resistant. Cherry laurel berries and foliage contain hydrocyanic compounds: wash hands thoroughly before eating or handling food after any work with cherry laurel.
  • Waste handling: laurel clippings are bulky. Have bags or a tarpaulin ready before you start: the volume often surprises people. Do not leave laurel clippings in a heap next to the hedge for more than a few days in warm weather.

Cherry laurel vs Portuguese laurel: renovation differences

Both species respond to hard cutting, but the differences that matter for renovation are in leaf size, vigour and tool choice.

Cherry laurel (Prunus laurocerasus)

Cherry laurel is the larger-leaved of the two and the more vigorous. It grows strongly in almost all soil conditions in Kent, and can reach 3 to 4 metres in a relatively short time if not maintained. The key rule for cherry laurel renovation is to use secateurs or loppers, not a hedge trimmer. The large leaves (8 to 15 cm long) are shredded by trimmer blades, leaving a hedge covered in brown-edged cut leaves for the rest of the season. This is not harmful to the plant, but it looks very poor and there is no way to correct it once done.

When you cut cherry laurel back hard with secateurs, cutting each stem cleanly just above a bud or lateral, the remaining leaves and new growth look presentable within a few weeks. It takes significantly longer than a machine cut, but on a hedge being renovated, the extra time is worth it.

Portuguese laurel (Prunus lusitanica)

Portuguese laurel has smaller, darker leaves (4 to 8 cm, with a glossy surface and a red stalk). It is slower-growing and slightly less vigorous than cherry laurel. The smaller leaf size means that hedge trimmers produce a more acceptable finish: the cut edges on smaller leaves are less visible than the shredded edges on large cherry laurel leaves. A hedge trimmer can be used for the initial cut-back on Portuguese laurel, though hand finishing with secateurs gives a neater result.

Portuguese laurel also tolerates harder cutting in one season, and there is less risk of a prolonged bare period. For a hedge that needs to be brought back quickly, Portuguese laurel is the more accommodating of the two.

When to do the renovation cut

The ideal windows

Late September through to February is the safest and most effective window for laurel renovation. The nesting season has ended, the hedge is not in active growth, and new growth in spring will respond vigorously to the previous autumn's cut. In East Kent, the coastal influence often extends the useful cutting window into November and December: prolonged hard frosts are uncommon near Sandwich and Deal, and mild conditions can allow early recovery growth once a hedge is cut.

Late August is acceptable as a secondary window, particularly for hedges that need to be tidied before the end of the season. The main growth flush has passed and nesting is winding down. Work done in late August should be completed before the weather begins to cool sharply, usually mid-October in Kent.

Avoid late spring and early summer

Cutting cherry laurel in May or June means cutting during the main growth flush. The hedge will produce vigorous new growth, but the amount of new growth can make the hedge look worse for the rest of the season before it settles. More importantly, nesting season is at its peak. The late autumn to winter window avoids both problems.

How to cut

Deciding how much to take off

Before you start, walk the full length of the hedge and decide on the finished height and width you want. Mark a cane or stake at that height at several points along the hedge as a visual guide. Laurel grows strongly and will recover, but over-cutting in one place and under-cutting in another produces an uneven result that takes two or three growing seasons to even out.

For a renovation involving significant height reduction, taking the hedge down from 3 metres to 1.8 metres for example, do the height reduction first and then the sides. This avoids the situation of having spent an hour on the sides only to find that the sawdust from the height cut falls all over the work already done.

Tools for each job

  • Height reduction on large stems: a pruning saw or reciprocating saw, or a chainsaw if the stems are very large. This is the structural cut, done before the finish work.
  • Side cutback: loppers for stems up to 2 to 3 cm diameter; secateurs for smaller stems; a hedge trimmer for the final surface tidy on Portuguese laurel only.
  • Reaching the top: a scaffold platform or long-reach loppers. For renovation on a hedge over 1.5 metres, avoid a free-standing ladder with a saw: the combination is unstable. A scaffold platform hired for a day is worthwhile for a long run.

The cut itself

Work along the hedge in sections of 2 to 3 metres. For each section: address any stems above the target height with loppers or a saw first; then cut the sides back to the target line, cutting each stem cleanly just above a bud or lateral, working from the top corner downward. For cherry laurel, resist the temptation to follow up with a trimmer for a faster finish: the result of a machine cut on large-leafed laurel is a brown-edged surface for the rest of the season.

For Portuguese laurel, once the main structural stems are addressed with loppers, a hedge trimmer can be used to tidy the surface. The result will not be as fine as a secateur finish, but it is acceptable and much faster on a long hedge.

What to expect after the renovation

The first few weeks

After a hard renovation cut on laurel, the hedge will look rough. Cherry laurel in particular looks bare and sparse for three to four weeks after a major cut. This is normal. Within four to six weeks, new growth should begin appearing from dormant buds on the main stems. In East Kent's coastal climate, this recovery growth often starts earlier than further inland: mild conditions in October and early November can extend the growing period and get new shoots established before the first frosts.

First growing season

By the following summer, a well-cut laurel will have covered most of the bare sections with new foliage. The hedge will not be at full density for another season, but it should be presentable. Do not give it a maintenance trim in the first growing season after renovation: let it grow freely and build up the foliage cover it needs. The first post-renovation maintenance trim should come in the second full growing season after the renovation cut.

Watering in dry spells

A laurel hedge that has just been cut back hard in late summer or autumn is managing more bare wood than usual. If the following spring and early summer are dry, water the base of the hedge during any dry spells between May and July. This is particularly important for hedges on the lighter soils of the coastal belt around Sandwich Bay and the Thanet coast, where summer drought stress sets in earlier than on heavier inland soils.

Waste disposal

Laurel clippings are bulky, and the large cherry laurel leaves do not compost easily without shredding first. Practical options:

  • Council green waste collection: if bulky green bags are accepted in your area, this is the simplest route for a maintenance trim.
  • Garden shredder: shredded laurel composts much faster than whole leaves and can be used as a mulch once composted. Worth considering for large hedges where the material will be reused on site.
  • Skip hire: not unusual for a 30-metre hedge being taken back significantly. The volume of a full renovation cut on a tall, wide laurel hedge can easily fill one skip.

Laurel berries and unshredded laurel should not be placed in a compost heap that will be used on edible crops. The hydrocyanic compounds in the leaves break down during composting, but the process takes time and an unfinished heap can still be problematic.
