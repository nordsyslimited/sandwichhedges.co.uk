Forsythia hedge pruning and care in Kent

The yellow flowers appear before the leaves. Whether you see them again next spring depends entirely on one timing decision you make in late April.

Updated August 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Forsythia is one of those plants that splits opinions. In February and March, it looks spectacular: a cloud of yellow on bare wood, long before anything else in the garden is thinking about waking up. Cut it at the wrong time of year, though, and you will get nothing but leaves for the next twelve months.

We see this regularly in gardens around Deal, Worth and Ash. Someone gave their forsythia hedge a good tidy-up in October. By the following February, their neighbours are enjoying bright yellow and theirs is just another brown hedge. The reason is simple, and once you understand it, you will not make the same mistake.

Nesting season: look before you cut

Forsythia is usually pruned in late April or early May, squarely inside nesting season. The UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting runs roughly 1 March to 31 August. Look carefully into the shrub before you start. If birds are nesting, you wait. No exceptions.

The one timing rule

Forsythia flowers on old wood. The buds for next spring's display form on stems produced this year, from roughly June onwards. If you cut those stems off in September, October or November, you have already removed next year's flowers. The plant does not "store" buds from year to year.

The correct time to prune is immediately after flowering, usually late April or early May in Kent. Leave the plant two to three weeks after the last flowers drop: give it time to direct energy back into shoot production, then cut. In East Kent, where mild coastal winters mean forsythia often starts flowering in February rather than March, the pruning window typically falls in the last two weeks of April.

If your forsythia flowered brilliantly this spring and you want it to flower brilliantly next spring, the secateurs come out when the flowers drop. Not in autumn. Not at Christmas. After flowering.

Before you start: safety

  • Eye protection is essential: cut stems spring back and debris flies.
  • Sturdy gloves: forsythia is not thorny but fresh-cut ends are sharp.
  • Ear defenders if using a petrol or electric trimmer.
  • RCD adaptor on any extension lead for a corded electric trimmer.
  • Prefer a pole/long-reach trimmer over a ladder for tall hedges. If a ladder is unavoidable, have someone footing it and never overreach sideways.
  • Check for wasp nests before starting, particularly from June onwards.
  • Check for brambles growing up through the base: gloves not optional.

How to prune a forsythia hedge: step by step

Step 1: wait for the flowers to finish

Do not rush. Two to three weeks after the last flowers have dropped is about right. The plant is directing energy into new shoot production during this window, and that new growth is the wood that will carry next year's buds.

Step 2: remove the oldest, thickest stems

Using loppers or a pruning saw, cut a third of the oldest, woodiest stems down to near ground level or back to a strong sideshoot lower down. These older stems produce fewer flowers than younger wood. Taking them out at the base each year keeps the plant productive. This is the single most effective thing you can do for a forsythia hedge.

Step 3: shorten younger shoots by a third

Once the old stems are out, shorten the remaining shoots by roughly a third to encourage branching and density. For a formal hedge, a hedge trimmer works well here: you are cutting soft new growth, not old wood. For an informal hedge, hand shears or secateurs give a softer finish.

Step 4: tidy the outline

If you want a formal shape, this is the moment to step back, check the outline against your string line or a straight board, and make any final adjustments. Forsythia does not keep a rigid shape like yew or box, so perfection is optional. A tidy rather than immaculate result suits the plant's character.

Step 5: clear the trimmings

Forsythia clippings are soft and compost well if chopped or shredded first. See our guide on what to do with hedge trimmings for practical disposal options.

What not to do

  • Do not cut in September, October, November or January. This removes next spring's flower buds. There is no recovery from this in the same season.
  • Do not cut every stem to the same length. Some age variation in the framework gives forsythia its natural arching character. Topping everything to one height produces a dull, stiff hedge.
  • Do not cut into very old, thick brown wood without good reason. Forsythia usually regenerates from old wood if needed (unlike conifers), but unnecessary hard cutting delays flowering for a year or two.

Renovating an overgrown forsythia hedge

If your forsythia has not been touched for several years and has become a tangle of old unproductive wood, you have two options.

Gradual renovation (less shock, slower result): remove a third of the oldest stems to the ground each year immediately after flowering, for three consecutive years. By year three the whole plant has been renewed and is producing maximum flowering wood again. You will still get reasonable flowers each year during the process.

Hard renovation (more dramatic, faster result): cut the entire plant back to within 30 to 45 cm of the ground immediately after flowering. It will shoot vigorously from the base and, with Kent's mild winters, will produce a solid framework of young growth through summer and autumn. Expect modest or no flowering the first spring after a hard cut, then a full return in year two.

Do not hard-prune a forsythia in autumn with the intention of renovating it. You will remove the flower buds and set the plant back twice: once by removing the flowers, once by cutting back into old wood that will take a season to regenerate.

Common varieties in Kent gardens

Forsythia x intermedia 'Lynwood Gold': the most common in East Kent gardens, and with good reason. Upright, vigorous, covered in large clear yellow flowers, and it holds an RHS Award of Garden Merit. Suits a formal hedge up to 2 m tall.

Forsythia x intermedia 'Spectabilis': a more open, arching habit with strong yellow flowers. Better as an informal screen than a tight formal hedge.

Forsythia x intermedia 'Week End': compact, to about 1.5 m. Good for smaller gardens in Woodnesborough or Eastry where a full-size forsythia would take over.
