How hard can you cut back a hedge?

Some hedges shrug off a very hard cut. Others will not regrow at all from bare wood. Knowing which you have decides what you can do.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Overgrown hedges are among the commonest calls we get across Sandwich, Deal, Worth and Eastry. The question that comes before any work is: how far back can we go? Cut within the green and every species is happy. Cut into bare old wood and the answer depends entirely on what the hedge is.

Nesting season: check before you cut

Under the UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs roughly 1 March to 31 August. The safe cutting window for most hedges is September to February. A light summer trim is only reasonable if you have watched the hedge and checked it is empty first. See how to check a hedge for nests.

Quick answer by species

Hedge Regrows from old wood? Notes
Yew Yes Slow to recover but reliable. See yew hedge care.
Beech and hornbeam Yes Cut while dormant. See beech hedge care.
Hawthorn and blackthorn Yes Can be coppiced or laid. See overgrown hawthorn.
Privet Yes Very tolerant. See privet hard renovation.
Holly Yes Tolerant but prickly work. See holly hedge care.
Cherry laurel Yes Regrows strongly. See laurel hedge care.
Leylandii and most conifers No Stay within the green. See reducing conifer height.
Lavender and rosemary Not reliably Trim lightly each year instead.

The green rule for conifers

Leylandii, Lawson cypress and similar conifers carry foliage only on the outer layer. Behind it the branches are brown and bare, and they do not produce new shoots when cut. Cut back to live green foliage and the hedge is fine. Cut into the brown and you leave a hole that does not fill. If a conifer hedge has already outgrown its space, the realistic options are in our conifer reduction guide. We trim and reduce conifer hedges, but nobody can promise regrowth where there is no green to cut to.

How to stage a big reduction

  1. Year one, late winter: reduce the height, or cut one side back hard.
  2. Year two, late winter: cut the other side, or take the next section.
  3. Between cuts: water in dry spells, mulch the base, and avoid feeding heavily.
  4. Year three: shape and return to normal trimming once fresh growth covers the cuts.

Staging suits old, dry or tired hedges and any hedge growing in poor chalk or shingle soil, where recovery is slower. For a step-by-step reduction approach, see renovating a neglected hedge and reducing an oversized hedge. If it has already been cut too hard, read recovering an over-cut hedge.

Trees growing in a hedge, and notices

A hard cut to the hedge itself rarely needs permission. Trees growing within or alongside it are different. In a conservation area, the council expects notice before certain work on trees, under section 211 of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990. Dover District Council is the planning authority for Sandwich. Our guide to permission to prune trees in a Sandwich conservation area explains the position. If your evergreen hedge is over 2 m and a neighbour complains about light, the High Hedges rules in Part 8 of the Anti-social Behaviour Act 2003 may apply; see hedge heights and neighbour law.
