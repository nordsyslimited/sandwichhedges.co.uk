Moving established hedging plants: a seven-point checklist

Sometimes a hedge plant ends up in the wrong spot: a privet that blocks a gate, a hawthorn that's crept too close to a wall, or a row that simply needs rearranging after a garden redesign. Moving them is possible, but only if you do it in the right order.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

The seven-point checklist

  1. Time it right: late October to early February for deciduous species
  2. Dig the new hole before you lift the plant
  3. Water the plant thoroughly 24 hours before moving
  4. Cut a generous root ball, minimum 30cm radius from the stem
  5. Lift cleanly and replant within 20 minutes where possible
  6. Replant at exactly the same depth, never deeper
  7. Water in, firm around the base, and mulch to 7-10cm

November is one of the best months to do this in Kent. Deciduous hedging plants are fully dormant, the soil is usually workable before the deep frosts arrive, and the plants have the whole winter to put out new roots before the stress of spring growth. If you have been meaning to shift something for a year or two and kept putting it off, now is the time.

That said, moving an established plant is not the same as planting a new one. The roots are established and wide-ranging; cutting through them is a shock the plant needs to recover from. The checklist above is not arbitrary: skip step 2 (the new hole) and you are likely to leave the root ball sitting out while you dig, which dries out the fine roots. Skip step 7 and air pockets form around the roots and they dry out from below. Each step matters.

The checklist in detail

1. Time it right

Deciduous hedging species (hawthorn, blackthorn, field maple, hazel, hornbeam, beech, dog rose) are best moved from late October, once the leaves have dropped, through to early February. After that the sap begins to rise and disturbance sets them back harder.

Evergreen and semi-evergreen species (privet, cherry laurel, Portuguese laurel, box, yew) have different requirements. They never go fully dormant, so the preferred windows are September, when the soil is still warm enough for root re-establishment, or April, when cold frosts have passed. Avoid moving evergreens in June and July when heat and drought stress compound the transplant shock.

2. Dig the new hole first

This matters more than it sounds. The hole needs to be wider than the root ball you are about to lift and roughly the same depth. Prepare it completely, with any compost or organic matter mixed into the base, before you pick up the spade to lift the plant. A root ball left on the surface for even thirty minutes can dry out enough to set the plant back significantly, particularly in a cold wind.

3. Water the plant 24 hours before

Give the plant a thorough soaking the evening before you plan to lift it. This softens the surrounding soil (which helps the spade cut more cleanly), and ensures the root zone is fully hydrated when you dig. Do not try to move a plant in dry conditions without this step.

4. Cut a generous root ball

For a plant up to 1m tall, start your spade cuts at least 30cm from the main stem, working in a circle. For a plant 1-1.5m tall, increase that to 40-45cm. The object is to keep as much of the fibrous feeder-root system intact as possible, not just the structural roots nearest the stem. Push the spade in vertically, not at an angle, so you cut roots cleanly rather than tearing them.

Expect to find roots that run well beyond the circle you marked. Cut them cleanly with the spade or a pair of loppers rather than pulling or levering. Torn roots heal slowly; clean cuts heal quickly.

5. Lift cleanly and replant quickly

Once you have cut the circle, angle the spade inward and lever the root ball out onto a piece of polythene or hessian sacking. This keeps the soil on the roots and allows you to carry the plant without it disintegrating. Move it to the new hole immediately. If you need to carry it any distance, wrap the sacking around the root ball and tie it loosely.

Aim to have the plant in the new hole within twenty minutes of lifting. That is not a hard rule, but it concentrates the mind usefully.

6. Replant at the original depth

Lower the plant into the hole and check the depth. The soil mark on the stem (the dark ring where the original soil level was) should sit at or just above the new soil surface. Too deep is worse than too shallow: burying the stem encourages rot. Adjust the base of the hole if needed.

Backfill with the removed soil, firming as you go in layers to eliminate air pockets. If your soil is heavy clay, mix in some grit or compost. Do not add fertiliser at this stage: it can scorch damaged roots.

7. Water in, firm and mulch

Give the newly planted hole a thorough soaking, let the water drain, then firm around the base with your foot. Apply a 7-10cm mulch of bark chips or well-rotted garden compost in a circle around the base, keeping it a few centimetres clear of the main stem to prevent collar rot. This retains moisture and suppresses competing weeds through winter.

If the plant is taller than about 1m, or in an exposed position, stake it. Use a short stake (no more than a third of the plant height) with a rubber tree tie, allowing the stem to flex slightly. A plant that is held completely rigid does not develop the root anchorage it needs.

Nesting season: the timing constraint

Moving hedge plants involves cutting, which may expose or disturb nesting sites within or near the hedge. Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs from 1 March to 31 August. November through February is the ideal time for transplanting work precisely because it falls entirely within the safe window.

Which species move best

Hawthorn, field maple, hazel and hornbeam move reasonably well when dormant, provided the root ball is generous and the aftercare is thorough. Dog rose (Rosa canina) and blackthorn are more challenging: both spread by suckers, and the root system you can lift is only part of what the plant has in the ground.

Cherry laurel and leylandii can be moved in September if you have to, but the success rate drops with size. A laurel over 1.5m that you want to keep is better treated as a renovation cut-and-new-plant job unless you have machinery to take a large root ball intact.

Box (Buxus sempervirens) moves well in autumn or early spring and can be moved in quite small sections. Yew (Taxus baccata) is surprisingly tolerant of transplanting if the root ball is generous and the plant is not in deep drought stress first.

What to expect in the first spring

Some wilting or leaf scorch in the first spring is normal and does not mean the plant is dying. The root system has been reduced and the plant cannot supply water to its full canopy. Some species drop leaves and appear dead, then push new growth from dormant buds in June. Resist the urge to dig the plant up and inspect the roots after the first month: leave it alone, keep the mulch moist, and check by scratching the bark with a fingernail. If the tissue underneath is green, the plant is alive.

Do not prune the plant hard in its first spring after moving: it needs every leaf it can produce to photosynthesise and rebuild the root system. Light tidying only.
