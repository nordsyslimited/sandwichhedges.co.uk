How to plant a privet hedge in Kent

Privet is the most planted garden hedge in East Kent. It is fast, forgiving, cheap as bare-root whips and hard to kill once established. Here is what to do, start to finish.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Privet planting checklist

  • Plant bare-root whips November to March (or container stock any time)
  • Spacing: 3–5 plants per metre, single or double staggered row
  • Strip turf and remove perennial weeds along a 60 cm strip
  • Dig a trench 25–30 cm deep; work in compost on thin chalk soil
  • Plant at correct depth: root collar level with soil surface
  • Cut back by a third immediately after planting (formative cut)
  • Water in thoroughly; apply bark mulch to 60 cm either side of plants
  • Water weekly during dry spells in year one
  • No trimming in year one beyond the formative cut
  • First light shaping cut in year two, September or October

Privet (Ligustrum ovalifolium) covers more front garden boundaries in Sandwich, Deal and the surrounding villages than any other hedging plant. That is partly habit, partly tradition, and partly because privet genuinely earns it. It grows fast, it takes hard cuts, it recovers from neglect, and it establishes reliably on the chalk soils underlying much of East Kent.

The golden form (Ligustrum ovalifolium Aureum) is widely planted too, particularly in older front gardens. It is a bit slower than the green-leaved type but takes the same treatment at planting time.

Bare-root or container-grown?

Bare-root privet is lifted from nursery fields while dormant and sold from November to March. It is the most economical option, often less than a pound a plant in bulk. The roots are active from the moment they go in the ground and establishment is usually reliable. This is the right choice for planting a full run of hedging.

Container-grown privet is available year-round and gives you more flexibility, but it costs more and pot-bound plants sometimes take longer to establish than bare-root. If you are planting in autumn or winter, bare-root is the better choice. For a gap repair or a small top-up in spring or summer, container-grown is more convenient.

When to plant

Bare-root privet can go in any time the ground is workable between November and March. November is ideal: the soil is still warm enough for some root activity and the plants have the whole winter ahead of them. Avoid planting in hard frost when the ground is frozen solid: wait for a mild spell and plant promptly when it arrives.

Under East Kent's mild coastal climate, the soil is workable for most of the winter. We rarely see the prolonged hard frosts that freeze the ground deep enough to make autumn planting impractical. November to January is a reliable window in most years.

Spacing and rows

Three to five plants per metre works for most purposes. At 25 cm spacing in a single row, the plants begin to knit together within two seasons. A double staggered row fills in faster but requires more plants and more preparation. For a garden boundary of 10 metres or more, a single row at 25 cm centres is entirely sufficient. The hedge will be dense and presentable within three years.

Ground preparation

Privet is not fussy but it establishes more reliably in prepared ground. Strip the turf along a 60 cm-wide strip and remove any perennial weeds, particularly couch grass and bindweed. These compete hard with young plants in year one. Fork over to 25–30 cm depth. On thin chalk soil around Sandwich and Deal, work some garden compost or well-rotted manure into the trench bottom to improve moisture retention.

Planting depth and backfill

The root collar (where the stem meets the roots) should sit at soil level, not buried. Fan the roots into the trench bottom without bending them back up. Backfill with the excavated soil, firming with your boot as you go to remove air pockets. Keep the stem upright.

The formative cut

Immediately after planting, cut each plant back by roughly a third. Use clean secateurs and make the cut just above an outward-facing bud. This step is frequently skipped, and hedges planted without it often develop a gap at the base that takes years to fill. The formative cut forces energy into low, bushy growth from the start, which is exactly what you want from a boundary hedge.

Mulching and watering

Water in after planting even in November: it settles the soil around the roots and removes air pockets. Apply a 10 cm layer of bark mulch along the full planting strip, keeping it 5 cm clear of the stems. The mulch holds moisture, suppresses grass and weeds and moderates soil temperature. Top it up in spring.

In year one, water during dry spells from May onwards. Once a week deeply is better than little and often. Established privet (three years old and beyond) looks after itself in most East Kent conditions, but young plants are more vulnerable to summer drought on free-draining chalk.

Privet and the nesting season

Privet provides useful nesting cover once established. Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs approximately 1 March to 31 August. The safe cutting window is September to February. This applies from the second year of growth once the hedge starts to thicken.

BBC Gardeners' World produced this practical guide to cutting deciduous hedges, covering the technique for getting a clean, level finish. It applies directly to maintaining a privet hedge once it is established and growing well.

Year one to three: what to expect

Year one: limited top growth. The plants are putting energy into roots. Some may look almost stalled through summer. Keep them watered. Year two: noticeably more growth. Begin light shaping along the sides in September or October of year two. Leave the top alone until year three. Year three: the hedge is beginning to look like a hedge. First full top-and-sides cut in September. By year four you have a usable boundary hedge.

Privet grows at 30 to 40 cm per year in good conditions. A hedge planted at 60 cm will reach a presentable 1.2 to 1.5 metres within two to three years. This is fast compared with yew (15 to 20 cm per year) and comparable to laurel. For anyone wanting a quick result on an East Kent garden boundary, privet is one of the most reliable options.
