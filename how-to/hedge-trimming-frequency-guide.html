How often should I cut my hedge? A species-by-species guide

The answer depends entirely on what you're growing. Get the frequency right and you get a denser hedge with less effort. Get it wrong and you're either working harder than you need to or watching it get coarser every year.

Updated September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Quick-reference: cuts per year by species

  • Privet: 2-3 cuts (June light trim; August/September main cut; October tidy if needed)
  • Leylandii and Leyland cypress: 2 cuts (late May/June; late August/September)
  • Laurel (common): 1-2 cuts (late August main cut; June light trim if needed)
  • Photinia (Red Robin): 2 cuts (after first flush in May; late August)
  • Beech: 1 cut (late July or August)
  • Hornbeam: 1 cut (late July or August)
  • Yew: 1 cut (August to September)
  • Holly: 1 cut (late July to September)
  • Box: 2 cuts (late May/June; late August)
  • Hawthorn: 1-2 cuts (August; optional light June tidy)
  • Hazel: 1 cut (late February for coppice renovation; August for formal hedges)

The nesting season rule

It is an offence to damage an active bird's nest under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981. Nesting season runs roughly 1 March to 31 August. The safe cutting window for most hedges is September to February, with a light summer trim permitted only if the hedge is checked and demonstrably empty first. Every species entry below is written with this in mind: any "June trim" referred to is subject to a nest check first.

One reason frequency varies so much between species is growth rate. Privet can put on 30-40cm a season; yew might add 15-20cm in a good year. The density you're trying to achieve also matters: a formal clipped hedge needs more frequent attention than a looser, more natural boundary. In Kent, the growing season can extend well into October in mild years, and coastal areas around Sandwich Bay and Deal tend to run a week or two behind inland villages like Worth and Eastry in spring, a useful quirk for planning around nesting season.

Fast-growing species: 2-3 cuts per year

Privet (Ligustrum)

Privet is the hedge species that punishes infrequent cutting most visibly. The long, soft shoots it puts on through summer quickly give a hedge that looks overgrown even if it isn't. Two or three cuts per year is standard for a formal privet hedge. A light trim in June (after checking for nests), a main cut in late August or early September, and an optional October tidy if growth has continued late into summer. In a mild autumn you might be cutting into early November along the coast.

The good news is privet is very forgiving. It regrows readily, and an overgrown privet can usually be brought back to shape through a hard renovation cut in late winter.

Leylandii and Leyland cypress

Leylandii grows fast but needs careful timing rather than just frequent cutting. Two cuts per year: late May to early June (once growth has started for the season) and late August to early September (the last safe cut before the plant's growth rate slows). For more detail on the limitations of leylandii cutting, see our leylandii guide.

Photinia (Red Robin)

Photinia grows in flushes through the season. The most effective approach is two cuts: once after the first red flush has greened up (usually late May), and once in late August. This keeps the hedge compact and encourages fresh red growth each spring.

Medium-speed species: 1-2 cuts per year

Laurel (Prunus laurocerasus)

Common laurel is a fast grower in the right conditions but its large leaves mean over-cutting produces a ragged, browned finish. One good cut in late August with secateurs rather than shears gives the cleanest result. A second light tidy in June is possible in hot summers if the hedge has grown well ahead of itself. Always use secateurs on laurel; shears cut through the large leaves and the cut edges brown immediately.

Box (Buxus sempervirens)

Box benefits from two cuts: once in late May or early June to take off the new season's growth, and once in late August to sharpen up the shape before winter. Avoid cutting in wet weather (it spreads box blight), and check carefully for signs of blight before you start. In a year with early wet spring weather, you may want to defer the first cut until conditions are drier.

Hawthorn

Hawthorn grown as a formal hedge is usually cut in late August. It can take a second light trim in June if it's grown very strongly, but one annual cut is enough for most hawthorn hedges. Hawthorn grown as a country-style boundary hedge can be managed on a three-year rotation, though this isn't suited to a garden boundary.

Slow-growing species: once per year

Beech and hornbeam

One cut in late July or August. This is the timing that preserves the copper and russet dead leaves through winter, which is the main reason people choose these species. Cut too late (October or beyond) and the new growth won't harden off before the cold. Cut twice and you disrupt the cycle that produces the winter leaves. For the differences between beech and hornbeam timing, see our beech vs hornbeam guide.

Yew

One cut per year, in late August or September. Yew grows slowly but responds well to cutting and can be taken back hard when needed. Cutting later in autumn is fine for maintenance cuts; only avoid December and January if the weather is frosty. Yew is the most tolerant of the formal hedge species when it comes to timing.

Holly

One cut in late August or September. Holly grows slowly and, once established, holds its shape well year-round. Cutting in late summer allows any regrowth to harden before winter.

Hazel

For formal hazel hedges: one cut in August. For hazel grown on a coppice rotation for the wildlife and structural value: cut in late February or early March before growth starts, and only every three to five years rather than annually.

A note on Kent's growing season

East Kent's coastal climate is milder than much of the UK, particularly in winter. This means hedges around Sandwich, Deal and along the coast at Sandwich Bay can continue growing well into November in warm autumns. If you cut privet or leylandii in late September and get a warm October, they can put on noticeable regrowth before Christmas. A light tidy in late October isn't unusual for coastal Kent gardens. Inland villages like Worth, Ash and Eastry tend to cool off faster and generally follow the standard timings more closely.
