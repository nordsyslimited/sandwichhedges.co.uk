The three-year staged hedge renovation: bringing a neglected formal hedge back

A formal hedge that has outgrown its intended shape by a large margin can still be saved. The staged renovation approach, cutting one side hard one year and the other side the next, reduces the shock to the hedge and gives each side a full growing season to recover before the next cut. Here is how that programme works for the main formal hedge species in East Kent.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

The staged renovation approach is what heritage garden teams use on hedges that have been allowed to get seriously out of shape. The National Trust school of gardening demonstrates the technique on yew hedges at Hidcote Manor in Gloucestershire, and the same logic applies at any scale. You do not need to cut the whole hedge back to the bone in one season. Working across one side at a time keeps the hedge functional, reduces the stress on the root system, and usually produces a better result than a single catastrophic cut.

The nesting season check

Renovation cuts often go deeper than a maintenance trim. If you are cutting well back into old wood, nests can be concealed deeper in the structure than a quick visual check would reveal. The UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to damage or destroy an active nest. Nesting season runs roughly from 1 March to 31 August. The best renovation window is late October through February for deciduous hedges, and late August to October for evergreens including yew. See our guide on wildlife-friendly hedge trimming for the full legal detail.

Before you start: safety

  • Eye protection: mandatory for any renovation cut. Hard cutting near old wood produces fragments that travel further than a standard surface trim.
  • Ear defenders and gloves: cut-resistant gloves for work near dense old stems.
  • Ladder safety: for any hedge above chest height, prefer a scaffold platform, tripod step or long-reach tool over a free-standing ladder. Have someone footing any ladder in use and never overreach sideways.
  • Clear the base: stones and old wire at the base of an overgrown hedge are common. Take a few minutes to clear them before you start.
  • Yew toxicity: yew foliage and berries are toxic. Wash hands before eating and keep cut material away from animals and children.

Is staged renovation right for your hedge?

Work through these four questions before you plan the programme.

Question 1: is the hedge one of the species that responds?

Not every hedge species tolerates the hard cutting that staged renovation requires. This approach works well for:

  • Yew (Taxus baccata): the most tolerant of hard cutting of any conifer. Can be cut back to apparently bare old wood and will regenerate. The staged approach is ideal.
  • Beech (Fagus sylvatica) and hornbeam (Carpinus betulus): both respond well to renovation cutting, particularly in the dormant season. Some dieback is possible on very old stems; cutting one side at a time gives a margin for recovery.
  • Box (Buxus sempervirens): tolerates hard cutting and responds well, but is slower to fill in than yew, beech or hornbeam. Allow an extra season in the programme.
  • Cherry laurel (Prunus laurocerasus) and Portuguese laurel (Prunus lusitanica): both respond very well to hard cutting, though the results look rough for one full growing season. See the separate guide on laurel hedge renovation for the specific timing rules.

The staged approach does not work for:

  • Leylandii and similar conifers (Thuja, Chamaecyparis): these will not regenerate from bare brown wood. Their sides can be reduced, but only if green growth remains behind the cutting line. See leylandii renovation for the approach that does work.

Question 2: how much needs to come off?

The staged approach is for hedges that need more than a standard annual trim. If you are removing less than 20 to 25 cm from the surface, that is a maintenance cut, not a renovation. The staged programme applies when:

  • the hedge has grown well beyond its intended height and width
  • one or both sides have several years of unchecked growth
  • the hedge has lost its formal shape and cannot be recovered with a single annual cut

If the hedge is dead or has sections of complete die-back, see hedge rescue or replant: a decision guide before planning a renovation.

Question 3: is there green growth behind the outer surface?

For deciduous species such as beech and hornbeam, check for dormant buds on the older stems: they should be visible even in winter as small raised points on the bark. For yew, green growth behind a brown exterior is almost always present if the hedge is alive. For box, check carefully: box can die from the inside out without obvious external signs, and a hedge that appears solid from outside may have hollow sections within.

A simple test for any species: use a penknife to scrape the bark lightly on a main stem. Green or white tissue beneath the bark means the stem is alive. Brown and dry means dead. Do this on several stems before deciding.

Question 4: when did the hedge last have any significant cutting?

A hedge that has been lightly trimmed for years but never brought back to shape is a good candidate for staged renovation. A hedge that has been completely untouched for more than 10 years is likely to need professional assessment, particularly if the main structural stems are very thick. The older the uncut stems, the less predictable the response to hard cutting.

The three-year programme

This assumes the hedge needs significant cutting on both sides and some height reduction. Adapt it to what yours actually needs: some hedges only need work on one side, or the height reduction is not needed.

Year 1: cut one side hard

Cut the first side back firmly, to the main structural stems if necessary. For yew, this means cutting through the outer green growth entirely in some places and working back to old brown wood. For beech and hornbeam, cutting back to old wood in the dormant season (November to February) is fine: the sap is not rising and the stress on the hedge is lower than in the growing season.

Leave the second side completely untouched. Leave the top at or near its current height. The hedge will look unbalanced for the season: one side will be much more open than the other. This is correct. The cut side will begin pushing new growth in spring, and by the following summer should have a reasonable covering of new foliage.

For yew in East Kent, the first side can be cut from late August through to February. Starting in late August gives the cut side a brief growing period before winter for initial recovery. Starting in November or December loses one full growing season for recovery, but the dormancy reduces the initial stress on the plant. Both timings work: choose based on when you are ready to start.

For beech and hornbeam, the dormant season from November to February is better for Year 1. The structure is visible without leaves, which makes it easier to judge what to remove, and the hedge is not under active growing stress.

Year 2: cut the other side

The first side has had a full growing season to push new growth. It will not look fully recovered yet, but it will have a covering of new shoots from the old wood. Now cut the second side hard, back to the same structural line as the first. Leave the first side alone this year. Adjust the top marginally if needed, but do not make any significant height reduction in Year 2.

National Trust gardeners demonstrating this technique on the yew hedges at Hidcote Manor explain the reasoning: cutting both sides hard in the same year risks leaving the hedge with insufficient photosynthetic leaf area to sustain recovery. One side at a time keeps the hedge in photosynthetic balance throughout the programme. Their demonstration can be seen at the National Trust school of gardening YouTube channel.

Year 3: height and final shape

By Year 3, both sides have had at least one growing season of recovery. Now is the time to address the height if needed. Take the top down in stages: aim to remove no more than 15 to 20 cm per year on the height until you reach the target. For a hedge that is significantly over height, the full height target may not be reached until Year 4 or 5.

By the end of Year 3, both sides should be at or near their intended cutting line, and the height should be much closer to the target. The hedge will not look fully mature until it has had two or three more annual maintenance cuts, but it should be back in a recognisable formal shape.

What to expect along the way

The hedge will look worse before it looks better

This is the hardest part of staged renovation to explain to a neighbour. The cut side in Year 1 looks like a skeleton: bare stems with thin patches of surviving foliage. By the second summer it will have a covering of new growth, but it will still look thinner than the uncut side. By Year 3 both sides will be thin and the hedge will look young. The mature appearance returns over the following two to three annual maintenance cuts.

Setting expectations with neighbours before starting is worth doing. A quick note explaining what you are doing and why the hedge will look worse before it looks better avoids unnecessary complaints about abandonment or die-back.

Watering in the first season

A newly hard-cut formal hedge in a dry Kent summer is under stress. If Year 1 or Year 2 falls in a dry spring or early summer, water the base of the hedge if you can during dry spells between May and July. A depth of 10 to 15 cm at the soil surface is enough: deep watering every week or two is better than a daily light sprinkle. This is particularly important for yew in lighter soils around Sandwich Bay and the coastal belt, where the sandy ground drains quickly.

When to call in professional help

Renovation cuts that require significant access equipment, that involve hedges near boundaries or structures, or that need to be completed on a specific timeline are worth having done professionally. The main risk in DIY renovation is cutting the wrong amount in the wrong season and not having the professional knowledge to judge the hedge's response in context.

We are often called in after a DIY attempt has gone too far or stalled partway through. If you are unsure whether the hedge is recoverable, or how much to remove in a single visit, a professional assessment costs far less than the eventual replanting if something goes wrong.
