Choisya hedge care in Kent

Mexican orange blossom is one of three strongly-scented evergreen hedges that people often compare when choosing what to plant. Here is how to care for it, and how it stacks up against osmanthus and pittosporum.

Updated September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Choisya ternata is a fast-growing, glossy-leaved evergreen that produces clusters of white, orange-scented flowers in late April and May, and then often again in August and September. It can grow to three metres without trimming, but clips well as a medium informal hedge at 1.2 to 2 metres.

People frequently ask us whether to plant choisya, osmanthus or pittosporum when they want a scented, evergreen flowering hedge as an alternative to the usual laurel or privet. The answer depends almost entirely on what they want it to do. Here is the breakdown, starting with choisya itself.

Choisya ternata care

Pruning: when and how

The main pruning window is immediately after the first flowering flush, in late May or June. Cut back the longest shoots by about one-third, keeping the overall shape rounded rather than flat-topped. Choisya does not need a perfectly geometric edge: it naturally mounds to a pleasing shape and fights you if you try to make it perfectly square.

After the second flush in August and September, a light tidy-up removes spent flower stems and keeps the outline neat through winter. Do not cut hard at this point: late cuts remove the ripening wood and leave the plant vulnerable to frost.

Nesting season: 1 March to 31 August

Dense established choisya plants are used as nesting sites by blackbirds and dunnocks. It is an offence under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 to damage or destroy an active nest. Check the plant carefully before your May or June trim: look in from underneath for a few seconds. If you find a nest with eggs or chicks, wait until it is empty before cutting.

Tools and safety

What you need

  • Sharp bypass secateurs or loppers for annual tidying of a loose informal hedge.
  • Hedge shears or a light electric trimmer for a longer, more formal run.
  • Wear gloves: choisya sap can irritate skin and eyes in some people.
  • The aromatic leaves are pungent when cut. Work in a ventilated position; avoid cutting in strong sunlight if you are sensitive to plant compounds.

Hard renovation

If a choisya has grown too large or become straggly in the middle, cut it back hard in April or May: down to 60 cm if needed. It regrows vigorously from old wood, which is unusual for an evergreen. You will lose the season's flowers, but by late summer you will have fresh dense growth, and by the following year it will flower again. Do not hard prune in autumn: the new growth that follows will be hit by frost.

Feeding and watering

Choisya is not a demanding feeder. A dressing of balanced granular fertiliser in April is enough. Avoid high-nitrogen feeds, which push leafy growth at the expense of flowers. Water in dry spells in the first two years after planting: after that it is largely drought-tolerant in East Kent conditions.

Choisya in Kent conditions

Choisya is broadly hardy to around minus ten degrees Celsius, which covers most East Kent winters comfortably. A severe frost can damage young growth and flower buds in spring: if a late frost is forecast after the new growth has broken, a sheet of fleece over a smaller plant is worth the effort. In a sheltered courtyard garden in Sandwich or Deal, choisya is a very reliable plant. In an exposed coastal position it can look battered and the leaves scorch in cold salty wind.

For a seafront boundary or any exposed position, choose griselinia, escallonia or viburnum tinus instead, and keep the choisya for the inner garden.

Choisya varieties

Choisya ternata (the standard species)

Glossy, dark-green trifoliate leaves. Vigorous, to 3 m. The best for a substantial informal screen.

Choisya 'Aztec Pearl'

Narrower, more elegant leaves, slightly smaller plant to about 2 m. More refined in appearance and popular in smaller gardens. Slightly more frost-tender than the species.

Choisya ternata 'Sundance'

Golden-yellow foliage. Needs more sun to keep the colour; in shade it goes pale green and loses its point. Less vigorous and less appropriate for a proper hedge: better as a specimen shrub. Hard renovation should be done gradually over two seasons rather than all at once.

The comparison: choisya vs osmanthus vs pittosporum

All three are scented evergreen flowering hedges. Here is how they differ for a Kent garden:

Which to choose?

Choose choisya if you want a relaxed, large-leaved evergreen screen with strong scent in spring and again in late summer, you are in a sheltered garden, and you do not need a perfectly formal clipped edge. It fills space faster than the alternatives.

Choose osmanthus if you want a formal, clipped hedge with an extraordinary fragrance in April, a longer lifespan, and greater frost and wind hardiness. It is slow but very easy to maintain. See our osmanthus hedge guide for full details.

Choose pittosporum if you want a taller screen, can cope with a slightly more tender plant, and want good coastal tolerance in a sheltered position. It clips well and has a range of variegated and purple-leaved varieties. See our pittosporum hedge guide for full details.

Planting choisya as a hedge

Plant at 60 to 75 cm centres for a continuous informal screen. Container-grown stock can go in at any time of year, but spring and early autumn are preferable in East Kent because the soil is easier to work and retains more moisture. Water well in the first two summers.

Choose a position in sun to partial shade. Against a south or west-facing wall or fence, choisya grows vigorously and flowers heavily. A north-facing position is possible but flowers are sparse.
