Weeds at the hedge base: bindweed, brambles and nettles

The base of a hedge is where weeding gets awkward and weed species get persistent. A straightforward guide to clearing and keeping clear.

Updated September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Weeds at the base of a hedge are the most awkward weeds in any garden. You can't run a hoe through them (the hedge stems are in the way), you can't mulch deeply without smothering the hedge roots, and some of the worst offenders, particularly bindweed and bramble, are almost designed to exploit exactly this situation.

In East Kent gardens we deal with this constantly. Bindweed is everywhere from Sandwich to Eastry to Deal. Brambles colonise any ground that isn't managed annually. Nettles take hold wherever the soil has been disturbed. Ground elder turns up in older gardens where it has been spreading for decades. None of them will kill a mature hedge, but left unchecked they make the base impenetrable, reduce airflow, harbour slugs and make trimming properly impossible.

Here is what actually works.

Before you start: wildlife at the hedge base

Hedgehogs use the base of hedges for nesting and hibernation, and some ground-nesting birds nest at the foot of dense hedges. Nesting season runs roughly 1 March to 31 August; during this period, disturbance at the base of a hedge can destroy nests and young. The safe window for thorough base clearance is September to February. If you work outside this window, check carefully for any signs of a nest before pulling or digging. The Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to damage an active nest.

Before you start: safety

Weed clearance at hedge base does not look hazardous, but there are a few things worth noting before you kneel down and start pulling:

  • Thick gloves. Brambles have substantial thorns, nettles sting even through thin gloves, and many hedge species (hawthorn, blackthorn, pyracantha, berberis, holly) will cut your hands if you reach in carelessly. Use leather or thick nitrile gloves that cover your wrists.
  • Long sleeves and trousers. Nettle stings on bare skin are painful; bramble thorns through thin fabric tear easily. Cover up.
  • Eye protection. When pulling brittle stems that snap upward, or when hacking brambles with loppers, stems and splinters can flick toward your face.
  • Kneeling pad. Prolonged kneeling on hard ground, particularly on Kent's chalk-based soils, puts real pressure on the knee joints. Use a foam kneeling pad.
  • Back care. Hedge base work involves sustained bending or kneeling. Take regular breaks.

Step 1: Identify what you are dealing with

The four main weeds at hedge bases in East Kent are:

  • Bindweed (Calystegia sepium, field bindweed). Twining stems, white or pink trumpet flowers, extensive fleshy white roots that snap and regenerate. The most persistent of all. Calystegia sepium is the large-flowered form common along field boundaries; Convolvulus arvensis is the smaller garden form.
  • Bramble (Rubus fruticosus). Arching, thorny canes that root at their tips. Common at hedge bases in rural plots around Worth, Ash and Woodnesborough. Any fragment of root left in the ground will regrow.
  • Nettles (Urtica dioica). Spreading underground rhizomes. A serious problem in older gardens and anywhere the soil has been disturbed. Dense patches are typically a sign of fertile, nitrogen-rich soil.
  • Ground elder (Aegopodium podagraria). Low-growing, spreading vigorously by underground runners, with white umbellifer flowers in early summer. Common in older East Kent gardens, particularly around Sandwich and Deal where some plots have been cultivated continuously for a century or more.

Identifying which weeds you have matters because the methods differ. Brambles and nettles reward vigorous cutting and digging; bindweed requires patience and persistence; ground elder is the most difficult of all and often requires a season of persistent follow-up.

Step 2: Choose your timing

The best window for base clearance is September to the end of February. By September the nesting season is over, perennial weeds are starting to die back (which makes them easier to trace to the root), and you have time to mulch before the following growing season. Autumn clearance also gives you the chance to spot any structural issues with the hedge itself, such as gaps at the base or stems that have grown into the boundary.

If you are dealing with bindweed or ground elder and want to make visible progress, a second pass in late spring (late May or June, after nesting is over and the hedge has been checked) can help you catch any regrowth before it puts energy back into the root. Be scrupulous about checking for nests before any disturbance.

Kent's mild coastal winters are generally an advantage here: the soil stays workable most winters around Sandwich Bay, Deal and the villages inland, unlike parts of the country where ground freezes solid from November. You can usually get a clearance job done at any point between September and February without much difficulty.

Step 3: Clear brambles and nettles

For brambles, the approach is cut first, then dig:

  1. Use loppers or a long-handled pruner to cut the canes back to just above ground level. Wear thick gloves. Feed the cut canes into a barrow or bag as you go so you don't end up kneeling on thorns.
  2. Once the top growth is removed, dig out as much root as you can access. A narrow border fork is more useful than a spade at the hedge base because you can probe between hedge stems. Get as deep as the bramble root goes, which is often 30-40 cm.
  3. Any root fragment left in the ground will regrow. Expect to do a follow-up pass in spring and again the following autumn. The first year is the hard one.

For nettles, cut back to ground level and then fork out the underground rhizomes. Nettles are less vigorous than bramble; if you remove the top growth three or four times in a season (or mulch heavily enough to prevent light reaching the regrowth) they will usually give up within two years.

Step 4: Deal with bindweed

Bindweed is the most difficult of all hedge-base weeds because the roots are extensive, brittle and spread far beyond the area where you can see the top growth. Here is the most realistic approach:

  1. Do not try to dig out all the roots at once. It is nearly impossible and risks breaking roots into more fragments, each of which will regrow.
  2. Instead, trace the stems back toward their roots at the hedge base and pull the top growth out by hand, trying to pull along rather than break the root.
  3. Follow up monthly through the growing season. Each time bindweed puts energy into top growth, removing that growth forces the root to use its reserves. Repeated removal over one to two seasons substantially weakens even established bindweed.
  4. Where bindweed is growing up through the hedge itself, the most practical method is to carefully unwind the twining stems and compost or bin them separately (bindweed can regenerate from a node if composted in a cool heap).
  5. For very heavy infestations, a glyphosate gel applied directly to the leaves of isolated growths (away from hedge stems and foliage) can help reduce the root mass. This is a targeted, careful application, not a spray. Do not apply on windy days.

The RHS has a good overview of bindweed management that covers both garden bindweed and hedge bindweed with similar methods.

The RHS has covered bindweed management in detail on their YouTube channel (200,000+ subscribers): How to deal with bindweed | The RHS.

Step 5: Mulch the cleared ground

Once the weeds are cleared (or as cleared as they are going to be in one session), mulch the exposed soil. A layer of 8-10 cm of:

  • Bark chip or composted wood chip is the most effective and widely available option. Heavy enough to suppress most annual weed seeds. Does not harm hedge roots and improves moisture retention, which is useful in Kent's summer dry spells.
  • Cardboard mulch under wood chip works well for the first season where bindweed or ground elder is the primary problem: the cardboard blocks light while the wood chip holds it in place and looks reasonable. The cardboard breaks down within a year.
  • Composted green waste (well-rotted council-style compost) is a reasonable mulch if you don't have bark chip. Avoid fresh or only-partially-rotted material as it can introduce new weed seeds.

Keep the mulch 5-10 cm clear of the hedge stems themselves. Mulch piled against the base of hedge plants can encourage collar rot, particularly on leylandii, privet and young specimens. A ring of bare soil immediately around each main stem is fine.

See our guide to mulching hedges for more on materials and technique.

Step 6: Maintain each year

The honest truth about hedge-base weeding is that the first clearance is the hard one and the annual maintenance is the easy one. Once you have broken the back of the initial infestation:

  • A single session each autumn (September to October) to pull any seedling and regrowth weeds before they get established will take 30-60 minutes for a typical garden hedge.
  • Top up the mulch layer each spring to maintain the 8-10 cm depth as the previous layer composts down.
  • Deal with any bindweed or bramble regrowth promptly when you see it, before it gets any root system behind it.

Gardens in Worth, Ash, Woodnesborough and Eastry that we visit regularly are often the same story: a neglected base cleared properly once, then a 30-minute annual pass thereafter, and the problem stays manageable indefinitely.
