Olearia hedge care in coastal East Kent

The daisy bush does something most hedging plants cannot: shrug off a full North Sea gale without dropping a leaf. Here is how to keep one looking its best, from Sandwich Bay to the Deal waterfront.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Olearia is not the first plant most people think of when choosing a hedge. It doesn't appear in every garden centre and it rarely gets a mention in general trimming guides. But along the exposed coastal strip between Sandwich Bay and Walmer, you will see it everywhere: a dense, grey-green wall of leathery leaves and, in June and July, clouds of small white daisy flowers.

It suits coastal East Kent precisely because the mild, salty winters that damage box and stress privet do not bother it at all. Two species are common in local gardens: Olearia macrodonta (the New Zealand holly, a larger-leaved, more architectural plant) and Olearia traversii (smaller-leaved, faster-growing, the better choice for genuine first-line exposure).

Nesting season: check before you cut

The UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs roughly from 1 March to 31 August. The main Olearia cut falls in late August or September, right at the edge of that window. Check the hedge carefully before starting. If you find an occupied nest, leave that section until the birds have fledged.

Before you start

  • Eye protection: Olearia leaves have tough edges. Flying fragments are unpleasant at trimmer speed. Safety glasses or goggles are essential with a powered tool.
  • Gloves: sturdy gardening or work gloves. No thorns, but cut leaf edges are sharp.
  • Ear protection: not needed for hand shears, but essential for petrol or electric trimmers.
  • RCD: if using an electric trimmer, fit a residual-current device in the circuit before starting.
  • Check for wasps: Olearia's dense growth is attractive to wasps building nests through summer. Disturbing one unexpectedly is unpleasant. Look before you cut.

Know your Olearia

Olearia macrodonta

The larger of the two: leaves up to 7cm, serrated edges (hence the common name "New Zealand holly"), glossy dark green above and silvery-white below. Grows to 3-4m left untrimmed. Makes a bold, architectural hedge. Less tolerant of extreme salt exposure than traversii, but handles Sandwich and Deal gardens well and suits any sheltered coastal position.

Olearia traversii

Smaller leaves, faster growth, the best choice for the front line in Sandwich Bay, Walmer or any garden within about 500m of the coast. It was specifically selected for exposed maritime conditions and handles the prevailing south-westerlies and the occasional north-easterly that rolls off the North Sea better than almost anything else you could plant. Widely used along the Deal and Walmer seafront as a first-line shelter belt.

When to trim

The main annual cut should happen in late August or early September, after the summer flowers have finished and before the first frosts. This timing works well for three reasons:

  • Nesting season is effectively over, so the legal and practical risk is low.
  • The plant has finished flowering, so you are not sacrificing this year's display.
  • There is still enough warmth for cut edges to harden before winter, reducing frost damage to fresh growth.

A light summer tidy in late June or early July is acceptable for fast-growing O. traversii specimens that are growing too open. Keep it light: take no more than 10-15cm off the new growth, and always check for nests first.

Avoid trimming in October or later. Fresh growth after a late cut won't harden before the cold arrives, and the new tips can be damaged by even a moderate frost.

How to trim: step by step

Step 1: set your line

Olearia responds well to a formal, consistent shape. Use a taut string line or a long batten to establish the top line before you start. These hedges grow unevenly, particularly O. traversii, and without a reference line the top wanders from one pass to the next.

Step 2: choose the right tool

For small or young hedges under about 1.5m, good-quality long-handled garden shears give the cleanest finish. The larger-leaved O. macrodonta in particular looks better cut by hand: a powered trimmer tends to shred the big leaves at the cut edge, leaving a brown fringe that takes a few weeks to grow out.

For larger or longer hedges, a petrol or battery trimmer is practical. Work from bottom to top on the face, then do the top in one smooth pass. Keep the blade roughly parallel to the hedge face rather than angled into it.

Step 3: remove no more than a third at once

Olearia is fairly forgiving, but a very hard cut in a single pass can leave sections that are slow to re-shoot. For routine annual maintenance, taking 15-25cm off a healthy hedge is usually right. If the hedge is several years overgrown, see the renovation section below rather than trying to correct everything in one go.

Step 4: clear the clippings promptly

Olearia clippings are dense and mat down readily. Collect them and compost them: they break down slowly but eventually. Don't leave them piled against the base of the hedge, as they can harbour fungal problems in a wet autumn.

Hard renovation cuts

An Olearia hedge that has grown well out of shape, or gone leggy after several years without trimming, can be renovated. The right approach differs by species.

Olearia macrodonta: responds well to cutting back hard into older wood in March or April. Take the whole hedge back to the shape you want. New growth breaks from old stems reliably in this species, as long as the plant was reasonably healthy going in.

Olearia traversii: more cautious. Reduce by no more than a third at a time and wait until you can see active new growth before cutting further. A very hard cut in a cold spring can kill weakened sections outright.

Either way, renovate in spring, not autumn. The plant needs a full growing season to recover.

Feeding and mulching after trimming

After the main annual cut, a 5-7cm layer of well-rotted garden compost around the base, kept clear of the stems, helps retain moisture and feeds the roots slowly through autumn and winter. If the hedge looks a little thin or pale, a balanced granular fertiliser scattered at the base in early spring will support new growth.

Olearia isn't demanding. In a coastal Kent garden it generally doesn't need much feeding at all: it's the sort of plant that often does better in poor, freely drained conditions than rich, sheltered ones. After a hard renovation cut, some mulch and a spring feed will speed recovery, but don't overdo the nitrogen or you'll get soft, frost-susceptible growth.
