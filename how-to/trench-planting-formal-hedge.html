Trench planting a formal hedge: beech, yew and hornbeam

Individual holes work for a short run of small transplants. For a long formal hedge of beech, yew or hornbeam, a trench gives you better soil preparation, more even planting depth and a cleaner result.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Most guides tell you to dig individual holes for each plant and that's fine if you're putting in a short run of small bare-root whips at 30cm spacing. But if you're planting a proper formal hedge, the kind that will define the garden for decades, a continuous trench is nearly always the better approach. It lets you improve the soil consistently across the whole run, gives you control over spacing and depth, and makes the actual planting go much faster once the trench is dug.

This is the method Ashridge Trees, one of the UK's main specialists in formal hedging plants, demonstrates in their guide to planting garden hedges. Their video walks through the key stages for beech, yew and laurel, all species that benefit from careful root preparation before going in.

"Planting a Garden Hedge Like Beech or Yew" by Ashridge Trees (YouTube). Ashridge Trees is a specialist UK bare-root nursery and a HedgeLink-endorsed supplier of formal hedging plants.

Plant outside nesting season

Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to disturb an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs roughly from 1 March to 31 August. Digging and planting along a hedge line during this window can disturb nesting birds in adjacent vegetation. The natural planting window for bare-root stock, late October to late March, fits neatly within the safe period. If you must plant during the nesting window, check the ground and surrounding vegetation carefully before starting.

Before you start: safety

  • Dial Before You Dig. Call 0800 096 8970 (UK) or use the online portal before breaking ground along any hedge line, especially near boundaries. Cables and pipes are frequently buried shallow in gardens.
  • Gloves. Bare-root yew is non-toxic to handle briefly, but all parts of yew except the berry flesh are poisonous if ingested. Wear gloves and wash hands before eating or drinking.
  • Back safety. A long trench means a lot of digging. Work in sections. Dig with a spade rather than a fork for clean sides, and keep the soil in a barrow or on a tarp rather than piling it back alongside the trench where you'll trip on it.
  • Firm footwear. Steel-toe boots or solid gardening boots when using a spade. A slip onto the blade edge causes serious injury.

When to use trench planting

Trench planting is the right approach when:

  • The run is 5 metres or longer, at which point digging a continuous trench is faster than 15-plus individual holes.
  • Plants are 60cm tall or above, since larger root systems need more space to spread naturally.
  • The soil is compacted or of mixed quality along the run. A trench lets you improve the whole strip evenly rather than in isolated pockets.
  • You want a very even finish. Trench planting makes it easier to keep plants at the same depth and to set spacing consistently.

Individual holes remain perfectly adequate for a short run (under 3 metres) of small bare-root transplants, typically 40-60cm, in good garden soil.

Step-by-step: trench planting a formal hedge

Step 1: mark the line

Use two canes and a string line to mark a straight run. For a single-row hedge, the string marks the centre of the trench. Allow 30-45cm from a boundary fence or wall to give the hedge room to fill out without growing into the fence. If you're planting a double staggered row, mark both lines before you dig: the rows should be 30cm apart.

Step 2: dig the trench

Dig to roughly 40-50cm wide and 30-40cm deep for most bare-root stock up to 1 metre. For larger plants (1m-plus), go deeper so the root ball sits at natural depth with the nursery soil mark at ground level. Put the excavated soil in a barrow or onto a tarp: you'll want it back in, mixed with compost, rather than compacted underfoot.

If you hit clay within the first 30cm, it's worth breaking it up with a fork below the dig depth to improve drainage. Standing water in the trench is a sign the drainage needs addressing before you plant: see our guide on choosing hedge plants for wet soil.

Step 3: improve the soil in the trench

Mix the excavated soil with well-rotted compost or manure at roughly one part compost to two parts soil. This is the one time you get to improve the soil at root depth for the life of the hedge, so it's worth doing properly. In the free-draining sandy soils around Sandwich Bay and the coastal strip, add more organic matter: a 50:50 mix is not too generous.

Step 4: prepare the bare-root plants

Bare-root plants arrive with their roots wrapped or in a bag. Do not let them dry out: if you're not planting immediately, heel them in temporarily in a corner of the garden by covering the roots with damp soil. Before planting, soak the roots in a bucket of water for 30-60 minutes. Trim any broken or excessively long roots cleanly with sharp secateurs.

Step 5: set the plants in the trench

Place plants at the spacing for your species. For beech and hornbeam, 30-45cm apart. For yew, 45-60cm. For box (low formal hedge), 20-30cm. Hold each plant at the correct depth, with the soil mark on the stem at ground level, and spread the roots naturally in the trench rather than bending them to fit. If you're planting a double row, stagger plants so each one in the back row sits between two in the front.

Step 6: backfill and firm in

Return the improved soil mix in layers, firming each layer gently with your boot as you go. The aim is to eliminate air pockets around the roots without compacting the soil. Once the trench is filled, firm the whole run with your heel to make sure plants are anchored and won't rock in wind. Check each plant is upright.

Step 7: water thoroughly

Water the full run even if it has rained. This settles the soil around the roots and removes any remaining air pockets. A slow soaker or a watering can with a rose is better than a hosepipe blast that washes the soil away. If planting in late autumn, the winter rains will usually take over at this point.

Step 8: mulch the run

Apply a 7-10cm layer of bark mulch along the hedge line, keeping it a hand-width clear of each stem. Mulch dramatically reduces water loss in the first growing season, suppresses weed competition and keeps the soil temperature more stable. This is especially important in East Kent's coastal soils, which drain freely and can dry out quickly in spring.

First-year pruning

This is where most people hesitate. For beech and hornbeam, cutting back by roughly a third after planting encourages bushy side-growth from the base rather than a thin, leggy hedge. For yew and box, leave the leading shoot alone in year one and only remove the worst leggy side shoots. For an instant formal look you can avoid cutting at all in year one, but you will usually get a denser hedge long-term if you are willing to accept a shorter plant for the first summer.

A formal hedge is a ten-year investment. Getting the planting right the first time costs one afternoon. Getting the wrong species or a bad planting job right can take years.

Species notes for East Kent

Beech (Fagus sylvatica)

The most popular choice for a traditional formal hedge in Kent. Holds its coppery brown leaves through winter, which is a real feature in a garden. Bare-root plants establish reliably and are the most cost-effective option for a long run. One cut per year in late July or August keeps it sharp.

Yew (Taxus baccata)

Slow in the first two or three years, then steady and very long-lived. Tolerates hard cutting better than any other formal hedge species, and shapes more crisply. Yew is tolerant of chalk and free-draining soils, which makes it a strong choice for much of East Kent. Plant at 45-60cm spacing. Note the toxicity to livestock: if any part of the garden is accessible to horses, choose something else.

Hornbeam (Carpinus betulus)

Often recommended for heavier or wetter soils where beech struggles. Holds dead leaves through winter like beech. Cuts cleanly and keeps a good shape. A slightly faster grower than beech at establishment stage.

Box (Buxus sempervirens)

For low formal hedges up to 1 metre. Traditionally planted at 20-30cm spacing. Box blight (Cylindrocladium buxicola) is widespread in Kent and has made box a difficult choice in many gardens. Check for signs of the disease in local gardens before committing to a large box run: if it's present on neighbouring properties, you may be fighting a losing battle. See our guide on Ilex crenata as a box alternative.
