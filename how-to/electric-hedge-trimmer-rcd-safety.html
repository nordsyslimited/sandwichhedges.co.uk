Corded electric hedge trimmer safety: RCDs, extension leads and cable management

The cable is the thing most people forget about. Here is a ten-point checklist for getting corded trimmer work right, with the detail behind each point.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Ten-point safety checklist: tick these before you start

  1. Fit a 30mA RCD adaptor (or confirm the outdoor socket has built-in RCD protection).
  2. Use an outdoor-rated extension lead with a thermal cutout, minimum IP44.
  3. Fully unwind the extension lead before plugging in.
  4. Inspect the lead and plug for cuts, cracks, bare wires or damaged pins.
  5. Wear eye protection (flying debris), ear defenders and sturdy, non-slip footwear.
  6. Put on gloves: they protect against cuts and reduce vibration fatigue.
  7. Keep the cable a bright, visible colour. Orange is easier to see in a hedge than black.
  8. Drape the cable over your non-cutting shoulder, trailing behind you.
  9. Check for wasp nests, brambles and birds before starting.
  10. Switch off and unplug before clearing a blockage, adjusting the blade guard or walking away.

Corded electric trimmers are reliable, light and consistent in power. Most domestic Kent gardens have a hedge well within reach of a standard outdoor socket and a 25-metre lead. The risks are real, though, and they mostly come from the cable: either from cutting through it or from using inadequate protection at the socket end.

Electrical Safety First estimates that hundreds of people in the UK are seriously injured by garden power tools each year. Many of those accidents are preventable with the right adaptor and a sensible habit or two.

RCD protection: what it is and why it matters

An RCD, or residual current device, monitors the balance of current flowing into and out of a tool. If that balance is broken, even momentarily, the RCD trips in around 30 milliseconds, well before the current can cause a fatal shock. At 30mA sensitivity, an RCD provides protection against the accidental contact with live metal that happens if you cut through a live cable.

Many modern outdoor sockets installed under current UK wiring regulations have built-in RCD protection. If yours was installed recently by a qualified electrician, it may already be covered. If you are not certain, fit a portable RCD adaptor in the socket before plugging anything in. They cost a few pounds, they fit any standard plug, and they are worth having.

Test the adaptor before each season by pressing the test button. It should trip immediately. If it does not, replace it. Do not use a damaged or corroded adaptor.

One important point: an RCD does not make an inadequate lead safe. It protects against the shock from cable damage, but it will not prevent the lead from overheating or starting a fire if the lead is underrated for the tool or coiled tightly on the reel.

Extension leads: what to look for

Not all extension leads are suitable for outdoor power tools. The right lead for a corded hedge trimmer has three things:

Outdoor rating

Look for IP44 or higher. This rating means the lead is protected against water splashing in from any direction, which matters when you are trimming damp foliage on a Sandwich Bay morning in September. A standard indoor extension lead is not safe outside.

Thermal cutout

A thermal cutout trips the lead if it begins to overheat. This protects against one of the most common hazards with extension leads on reels: the cable left coiled when the reel is plugged in. A coiled cable cannot dissipate heat properly, and the heat generated under load can melt the insulation. The thermal cutout stops this before it becomes a fire.

Fully unwound before use

Even with a thermal cutout, always unwind the lead completely before plugging in. This is not optional. A 25-metre lead coiled on a drum under a 1,000-watt trimmer load can overheat even with a cutout, depending on the cable's current rating. Fully unwound, the same lead runs cool all day.

Choose a lead long enough to reach the far end of your hedge without strain. If you are stretching the lead taut, it is too short. A 25-metre outdoor lead covers most domestic gardens in Sandwich, Deal, Worth and Eastry without extension.

Nesting season: check before cutting

Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs roughly 1 March to 31 August. If you are trimming in that window, check the hedge carefully from underneath, looking and listening for activity, before you start. The safe main cutting window for most Kent hedges is September to February.

Cable management while you work

The cable is safest when it is behind you. Drape it over your non-cutting shoulder at the start and work along the hedge so the cable trails behind your direction of travel. It sounds simple, and it is, but it takes a conscious effort to maintain the habit, especially on a long hedge where you are turning and stepping repeatedly.

A few rules that follow from this:

  • Never reach sideways across the cable with the blades running. The blade contact sensor on most modern trimmers is fast, but not fast enough to prevent a cut lead if you sweep the blades through the cable at speed.
  • If the cable falls in front of you, stop, set the trimmer down with the blade away from you, switch off, and rearrange the cable before continuing.
  • Use a bright orange or yellow lead if possible. A black lead disappearing into a green privet hedge is surprisingly easy to lose track of.
  • On long hedges with multiple runs, unplug and move the socket end of the lead rather than dragging the lead through or over the hedge.

Cutting on a slope or on a ladder

Cable management is harder on a slope or at height. On sloping ground, the cable tends to slide down and end up in front of you. Loop it up and over your shoulder rather than letting it hang loose. If you are working at height, tie the cable loosely to the ladder rungs above your working height so it cannot fall across the blades.

That said: a pole trimmer or long-reach trimmer removes the need for a ladder on most domestic Kent hedges, even on a yew or leylandii that has reached two metres. If the hedge requires a ladder, have someone footing it at all times, and consider whether a professional with the right kit would be a sensible call. See our hedge heights guide for the rules on how tall a hedge can legally be.

When to switch off

Switch off at the tool and unplug at the socket whenever you:

  • Put the tool down, even briefly.
  • Clear a blockage between the blades.
  • Adjust the blade guard or oil the blade.
  • Move the socket or rearrange the lead.
  • Walk away from the work area.

The blades coast briefly after the switch is released on many models. Keep fingers away from the blade area until it has fully stopped.

When a corded trimmer is not the right tool

For hedges more than about 15 to 20 metres from an outdoor socket, a battery or petrol trimmer is simpler and arguably safer: no cable, no adaptor, no lead management. Battery trimmers have improved significantly and cover most domestic Kent hedges on a single charge. See our battery vs petrol comparison for the detail.
