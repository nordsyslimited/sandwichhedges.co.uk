Osmanthus burkwoodii hedge care in Kent: is it right for your garden?

Small, dark, glossy leaves; white flowers with a jasmine-like scent in April; hardy enough for most Kent gardens. Osmanthus burkwoodii gets planted less often than it deserves, partly because gardeners are not sure where it fits. This guide helps you decide.

Updated August 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Osmanthus x burkwoodii is a hybrid evergreen shrub that makes a genuinely good garden hedge: slow enough that you are not trimming it constantly, tough enough to cope with East Kent winters, and pretty enough in April that it earns its place even when it is not being cut. We encounter it occasionally in gardens around Sandwich and Woodnesborough, most often planted by people who were given a pot as a gift and had no idea what to do with it.

Work through the questions below to find out whether it suits your situation.

Is osmanthus right for your garden? Work through these questions

Do you need a formal clipped hedge above 2 m?

If yes: osmanthus is not ideal. It grows slowly (15 to 25 cm per year) and in Kent it typically tops out at 2 to 3 m as a clipped hedge. For a formal screen above 2 m at any useful pace, consider griselinia, hornbeam or portuguese laurel instead.

If no, or up to 2 m is fine: proceed to the next question.

Is your garden directly on the coast (Sandwich Bay, Deal seafront)?

If yes: osmanthus burkwoodii has moderate salt tolerance. It will cope with a coastal garden that has some protection, but it is not the first choice for fully exposed shoreline positions. Griselinia or escallonia are better at handling salt-laden winds on the front line. If you have a sheltered coastal plot, osmanthus will be fine.

If no, or sheltered coastal: proceed.

Do you want the hedge to flower?

If yes: osmanthus burkwoodii flowers in April, with small white tubular flowers that have a sweet, jasmine-like scent. The scent is genuinely good and it carries in still spring air. If you want a flowering evergreen hedge this is a strong reason to choose osmanthus over box, pittosporum or ilex. Bear in mind that clipping immediately after flowering removes the spent blooms and keeps the plant tidy: do not clip before flowering if scent is one of your reasons for choosing it.

If not bothered: osmanthus is still a good choice, but you are not using one of its main advantages. Consider whether pittosporum tenuifolium might suit better if a more formal, faster hedge is the priority.

Are you replacing box because of blight?

If yes: osmanthus burkwoodii is one of the better box substitutes. The leaf is a similar scale and has the same dark glossy quality. It clips to a similar tight, formal shape. It has no known blight problem and is not affected by box tree moth. It establishes more slowly than box, but once it reaches height it needs only one or two trims a year. For small formal hedges in Sandwich, Ash and inland East Kent gardens, it is a very good box alternative.

If not replacing box: proceed.

Is your site in a frost hollow or north-facing?

If yes: osmanthus burkwoodii is rated RHS H5, meaning it tolerates temperatures down to around -15°C. This makes it significantly hardier than pittosporum and slightly hardier than griselinia. Even inland East Kent frost pockets in Eastry, Worth and Woodnesborough are unlikely to trouble it. A north-facing aspect is fine: osmanthus tolerates shade well, though it grows even more slowly in low-light conditions.

If no: osmanthus will be fine wherever you are in Kent.

Are you in a hurry to establish a hedge?

If yes: osmanthus is slow. At 15 to 25 cm per year in Kent conditions, a 1.5 m hedge from 60 cm transplants takes four to six years to reach height. If you need a hedge in two or three years, griselinia or hornbeam will get you there faster. Osmanthus is for gardeners who are prepared to wait for something that, once established, largely looks after itself.

If patient: osmanthus is a genuinely long-lived, low-maintenance hedge once established.

Verdict summary

Osmanthus burkwoodii suits you if: you want a small-to-medium evergreen hedge (up to 2 m) with fragrant spring flowers; you have an inland or sheltered coastal garden; you are replacing box and want something with a similar character; you are patient during establishment; you want low maintenance once established.

Consider an alternative if: you need height above 2 m quickly; you are fully exposed to salt wind; you want a hedge within two or three years.

Trimming osmanthus burkwoodii

Nesting season: check before you cut

The UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs roughly 1 March to 31 August. The safe trimming window for osmanthus is late May onwards (immediately after flowering) and then again in August to mid-September. Before any cut from March onwards, look carefully into the hedge for active nests.

Osmanthus burkwoodii flowers on the previous year's growth. The timing rule is similar to forsythia: trim immediately after flowering, not before. In East Kent, flowering usually ends by late April or early May. Cut within two to four weeks of the flowers finishing.

Because the plant is slow-growing, you can often manage with a single cut per year in late May. A second pass in late August keeps a formal hedge crisp through winter. Do not cut after mid-October: you want the plant settled before any frosts.

Before you start: safety

  • Eye protection: essential when trimming any dense evergreen hedge.
  • Gloves: the cut stems are sharp; osmanthus leaves have small serrated edges.
  • Ear defenders for petrol or corded electric trimmers.
  • RCD adaptor on any extension lead for a corded electric trimmer.
  • Wasp check: inspect before starting, particularly in August and September.

How hard can you cut it?

Osmanthus burkwoodii regenerates well from old wood. Unlike conifers or pittosporum, you can cut it back hard if necessary and it will break into growth from bare stems. This makes it forgiving if it has been allowed to get out of shape: hard renovation in late May after flowering is safe, though flowering the following spring may be reduced as the plant puts energy into vegetative growth rather than flower buds.

Planting notes for Kent gardens

Osmanthus burkwoodii tolerates a wide range of soils. In East Kent, where soils range from the heavy clay of the Stour valley to the chalk-derived loams around Eastry and Worth, it performs consistently. The one thing it does not like is waterlogged ground: if your site holds water in winter, improve drainage before planting.

Plant at 40 to 60 cm spacing for a solid hedge. Mulch well in the first two winters to protect the roots during establishment. In the first year, do not trim: let the plant develop its structure before you start shaping it.
