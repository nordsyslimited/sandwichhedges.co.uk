Renovating an overgrown hawthorn hedge

Hawthorn tolerates harder cutting than almost any other hedge in Kent. Here is how to bring one back, step by step, from a tangled mess to a tight, stock-proof boundary.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

A hawthorn hedge that has been left alone for ten or fifteen years can look intimidating: three or four metres wide, six metres tall in places, full of dead wood, crow nests and bramble. The good news is that hawthorn (Crataegus monogyna) is about the most forgiving hedging plant in Britain. Cut it as hard as you dare, and it will almost certainly come back.

We renovate hawthorn hedges in East Kent every winter: along field boundaries near Ash and Woodnesborough, at the bottom of town gardens in Sandwich and Deal, on old farm boundaries near Eastry that have not been touched in decades. The approach is the same each time.

Nesting season: the rule that sets everything else

It is a criminal offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981. Nesting season runs roughly 1 March to 31 August. Hawthorn is one of the most heavily used hedging species for nesting in Kent (blackbirds, robins, song thrushes, dunnocks and whitethroats all use it). Renovation work must be completed before 1 March or started after 1 September, with a thorough nest check first if cutting in September.

Step 1: assess what you have

Before picking up a tool, walk the hedge and note a few things:

  • How wide is it? Anything over a metre wide on each side is a candidate for the two-year approach (see Step 3).
  • Are there dead sections? A fully dead section of hawthorn will not regenerate. Mark these so you know what to expect after cutting.
  • What is inside it? Bramble, elder and ivy can become structural. They will need dealing with separately, before or after the main cut.
  • Are there old crow or magpie nests? These will be obvious in winter when the leaves are down. They are not protected outside the nesting season, but they do indicate the hedge has been a productive nesting site.

Wear heavy-duty gloves and eye protection throughout. Hawthorn thorns are long, hard and very effective at drawing blood. Safety glasses are not optional.

Step 2: choose your window

November to February is the ideal renovation window. Hawthorn is fully dormant, which means it puts no energy into new growth and can redirect its reserves into recovery once you have cut. The leaves are off, so you can see the structure of the hedge and cut more accurately. And you are comfortably inside the legal cutting window.

In East Kent, January and February are particularly good: dry enough to work, cold enough that any cut ends harden off rather than rotting, and the hedge is at its most dormant. We avoid cutting in hard frosts (below minus 3 degrees or so) as cut ends can suffer dieback.

Step 3: one cut or two years?

This is the main decision:

Single-season renovation

You cut both sides and the top hard in one winter. The hedge will look completely bare for a full growing season. This is acceptable for a hedge that is genuinely too wide or too tall to maintain, and where you do not need a screening boundary while it recovers. It puts more stress on the plant but hawthorn usually copes well.

Two-year approach

In year one, you cut one side very hard (back to within 15 to 30 cm of the hedge centre line, depending on the original width). You leave the top and the other side alone. The hedge retains some foliage on the uncut side, which means it keeps photosynthesising and recovering, and you still have a partial boundary. In year two you cut the other side equally hard, and trim the top to the new target height.

For any hawthorn hedge that is more than two metres wide, or more than four metres tall, we recommend the two-year approach. The hedge recovers more reliably and you are never left with a completely bare boundary in one go.

Step 4: cut the sides

Working from the bottom upwards, cut each side back to your target width. For a garden hawthorn hedge, a finished width of 50 to 80 cm is a reasonable target. For a farm boundary or mixed native hedge, you might leave it at 100 cm.

Use a petrol hedge trimmer or a powerful cordless trimmer for the main work. For stems thicker than about 25 mm, a pruning saw or loppers will be cleaner and kinder to the trimmer blades. There is no need to be tidy at this stage: the goal is to remove the bulk of the old wood and set the new line.

Keep the sides slightly narrower at the top than at the base: a gentle inward taper of 5 to 10 degrees. This lets light reach the lower stems, which is particularly important during the recovery phase when the hedge needs all the photosynthetic capacity it can get.

Step 5: address the top

Cut the top down to your target height. For a garden hedge, aim for the height you actually want, not the height you expect to recover to: hawthorn will almost certainly get back to where you want it within two or three seasons.

If the old hedge had thick horizontal stems at the top, cut these back to within 30 cm of the target height. New vertical shoots will emerge from these and can be trimmed to the finished line in subsequent seasons.

Step 6: clear the debris

Hawthorn clippings are hazardous. Rake or pull the cuttings away from the base of the hedge, and stack or chip them well away from the hedge line. Leaving a thick layer of cuttings at the base can harbour fungal problems, and loose thorny stems on a lawn are a genuine hazard.

Berries that have fallen or been cut off can be left on the ground or raked into a pile: they will rot down or be eaten. Do not compost large quantities of hawthorn stems in a domestic compost bin; they take a very long time to break down and the thorns make turning the heap unpleasant.

Step 7: aftercare

Hawthorn does not demand much after a renovation cut, but these steps help:

  • Mulch the base. A 5 to 8 cm layer of bark mulch or composted wood chips along the base helps retain moisture and suppress weeds, both of which compete with the recovering hedge.
  • Water in dry spells. The first spring and summer after a hard renovation cut is when the hedge is most vulnerable to drought stress. East Kent can turn very dry in May and June; a good soaking every ten days or so if there has been no rain will help.
  • Feed lightly. A general-purpose slow-release fertiliser applied in March can help regrowth, but do not overdo it. Hawthorn growing on reasonable soil does not need heavy feeding.
  • Leave it alone. Do not trim the new growth in the first growing season. Let it shoot freely and build up leaf area before you start shaping again.

What to expect in year one

From late April, you should see green buds breaking along the cut stems. By June, there will usually be strong new shoots 20 to 40 cm long. By August of the first year, a well-recovered hedge will look convincingly green again, though still open and somewhat irregular.

If sections remain bare by July, it may indicate dead wood that did not regenerate. Cut these sections back a little further to find live wood, or accept the gap and fill with a new transplant the following autumn.

Do not be disheartened by the first winter after renovation: a hawthorn that has just been cut hard will look like a pile of sticks from December to March. This is normal. The regrowth is happening below the surface.

If you can see any green regrowth by June, the hedge is alive and on its way back. Give it the summer and judge again in September.
