Hedge rescue or replant? A decision guide for failing hedges

Not every damaged hedge is worth fighting for. Not every dead-looking hedge is actually dead. The decision matters, because the wrong call costs you three to five years working against the grain.

Updated September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Rosy Hardy, RHS Vice President and plantsperson with 24 Chelsea Gold Medals, covers the assessment process for hedges that have been mistreated in her video "How to Prune to Rejuvenate a Hedge" (youtube.com/watch?v=q-fVHKASA38). Her approach, and ours, starts with the same step: assess what you actually have before deciding what to do. That is what this guide walks through.

Work through the four questions below in order. The right answer is usually clear by the time you reach question three or four.

Question 1: Is the hedge alive?

This is the step most people skip, usually by looking at the hedge and declaring it dead or alive on the basis of how brown it looks. Do the scratch test instead.

Take a fingernail or a penknife and scrape a small area of bark on one of the stems you are concerned about. Green or white-green beneath the bark means the stem is alive. Dry, brown and papery means it is dead. Do this on ten or fifteen stems across the whole run of the hedge, testing stems that look reasonable as well as those that look bad. What you are establishing is not just whether any part of the hedge is alive, but what proportion is.

Reading the scratch test results

  • More than 60% of stems pass: The hedge is worth attempting to renovate, subject to species (see Question 2).
  • 30% to 60% alive: Marginal. Renovation will produce a patchy result. Consider whether replanting makes more sense given your timeline.
  • Less than 30% alive: Replant. The remaining live material is not enough to produce a worthwhile hedge in any reasonable timeframe.

One important exception: deciduous hedges in winter (beech, hornbeam, hawthorn, hazel, field maple) go completely dormant and can appear dead when they are in good health. If you are assessing in winter and the stems are passing the scratch test, that is enough. Wait for spring before drawing any conclusion about bare or thin hedges that have been dormant.

Question 2: What species is it?

This is the single most important factor in whether renovation is possible at all.

Species that regrow from old wood: renovation is feasible

Privet, yew, hornbeam, beech, hawthorn, blackthorn, holly, hazel, field maple, laurel, box, photinia. These species will push new growth from old wood when cut hard. For these hedges, renovation is a realistic option if enough of the plant is alive and the cause of the problem is addressable (see Question 3).

Conifers: renovation is usually not possible

Leylandii, Lawson cypress, Leyland cypress, most Thuja and most Cupressus will not regenerate from brown wood. Once the green zone has been cut into or has died back, the bare stems stay bare. If a conifer hedge has gone brown on more than a short section, the realistic answer for those sections is removal and replanting. Yew (Taxus baccata) is the notable exception: it is the most regenerative conifer by far and will regrow from hard cuts on old wood.

Borderline species

Griselinia, escallonia, lonicera nitida, pittosporum and osmanthus all recover from hard cuts but not always predictably. These species benefit from a test cut on one section before committing to a full renovation.

Question 3: What caused the problem?

Knowing the cause affects both the decision and the approach. Some causes are addressable; some mean renovation attempts will fail regardless.

Cause Renovation prospect Key point
Over-cutting into old wood Good for broadleaved species. Very poor for conifers. The cause has ended. The plant just needs time and support to recover.
Neglect and excessive overgrowth Good for most broadleaved species. The plant is alive and vigorous. The challenge is bringing it back to a manageable size.
Drought stress (common on Kent chalk soils around Sandwich, Ash and Eastry) Good if addressed promptly. Poor if the drought stress has killed roots. Test the root zone first: if the soil at 20 cm depth is bone dry, water deeply before concluding anything.
Honey fungus (Armillaria) Poor in affected sections. Honey fungus spreads through root contact. Replanting in place without removing infected roots risks the new plants too. See our honey fungus guide.
Root disturbance (building works, service trenches) Depends on the extent. Partial root loss is often survivable; full root severance on one side usually is not. If a digger cut through the main lateral roots, the above-ground hedge may survive a season before declining.
Box blight (Cylindrocladium buxicola) or box tree moth caterpillar damage Mild blight: renovation plus treatment. Severe or recurring blight: consider a replacement species. See our guide to box hedge alternatives if blight is severe and recurring.

Question 4: What does the renovation timeline actually look like?

The most common mistake is underestimating how long renovation takes. Below is an honest comparison.

Path Year 1 Year 2 Year 3 Year 4 to 5
Renovation Hard cut one side; leave the other Cut other side hard; first side regrowth visible Both sides re-breaking; begin shaping Usable hedge at its new dimensions; maintained normally
Replant (bare-root native species) Remove old hedge; plant whips at 50 to 90 cm Plants establishing; 1 to 1.2 m; weed control critical Growth accelerates; light formative trimming Hedge reaching useful height; first proper trim
Replant (instant hedging, semi-mature plants) Remove old hedge; plant at 1.2 to 1.5 m Hedge usable for privacy; light trim Established; normal maintenance trimming Fully established; indistinguishable from a mature hedge

The timelines are similar enough that for marginal cases, the question becomes: which produces a better hedge by year four or five? A renovated hedge that started in poor condition is often thinner and patchier than a replanted hedge of the same age. A replanted hedge of native bare-root species at the right planting density catches up faster than most people expect.

Any work in nesting season?

Removing a hedge or cutting it hard for renovation between 1 March and 31 August risks disturbing active bird nests. The Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes this a criminal offence regardless of who owns the hedge. Hard renovation cuts and removal work are best done in late winter, February or early March, before nesting begins. For replanting, autumn and winter windows (October through March) give bare-root plants the best start.

When replanting is the right answer

All of the following are clear indications that replanting will produce a better outcome than renovation:

  • A conifer hedge (leylandii, Lawson cypress, most Thuja) where the brown area extends beyond a short section, or where the cause is over-cutting past the green zone
  • A broadleaved hedge where the scratch test reveals less than 30% of stems alive across the affected run
  • Active honey fungus infection across multiple sections of the hedge
  • Root severance from construction works on a substantial portion of the root system
  • A box hedge with severe, recurring blight that is not responding to treatment
  • Any hedge where the honest projection of its condition in five years after renovation is still unsatisfactory

Replanting is not defeat. A well-planted hedge of bare-root hawthorn, hornbeam or mixed native species at the right density will produce a better hedge in four to five years than most renovation attempts on a badly damaged plant. On Kent chalk and clay soils, native hedging establishes reliably when planted in October or November.

The best time to plant a hedge is twenty years ago. The second best time is this autumn.
