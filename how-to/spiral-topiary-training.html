How to create a spiral topiary from yew or box

Marking the ribbon, cutting the turns and keeping the shape sharp year after year. A step-by-step guide from the team who does topiary work across Sandwich, Deal and the villages of East Kent.

Published September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Spiral topiary looks difficult. It isn't, not really, but it is unforgiving in one specific way: there is nowhere to hide a mistake the way you can on a flat hedge. The spiral is the shape. If the turns are uneven or the ribbon is too narrow, it shows.

The good news is that yew and box are both very forgiving plants. Cut too deep in one spot and the plant fills back in within a season or two. The method below is what we use on maintenance jobs in Sandwich, Worth and Eastry, where period gardens often have established yew spirals that need bringing back into shape.

Before you start

  • PPE: gloves, eye protection and a dust mask when cutting yew (the foliage is toxic). Long sleeves recommended.
  • Ladders: for anything above chest height, use a stepladder with a working tray. Never lean a ladder directly against a topiary specimen.
  • Nesting season (Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981): cutting is restricted from 1 March to 31 August while birds may be nesting. The safe DIY window is September to February. Always check for active nests before you start; if you find one, stop and wait.
  • Wasps and brambles: check around the base of the plant before kneeling in close.

Step 1: Choose and assess your plant

A spiral works best from a plant with an existing cone or column shape at least 60 cm tall and ideally over 1 m. The denser the existing growth, the crisper the finished spiral will be. Loose, open plants will need a few seasons of regular clipping first to build the density that holds a spiral edge clean.

Yew (Taxus baccata) is the traditional choice in Kent gardens. It grows steadily rather than fast, responds well to hard cuts and the deep green colour makes the contrast between the clipped ribbon and the cut-back sections very clear. Large established yew spirals are a common feature in gardens in Sandwich and Woodnesborough.

Box (Buxus sempervirens) is better for smaller specimens under 80 cm. The finer-textured foliage gives sharper edges and a more elegant result at small scale. If you are planting new box, source it from a reputable nursery with box blight-resistant stock. Avoid cutting box in wet weather.

Step 2: Decide on the number of turns

Stand back from the plant and imagine dividing its height into equal bands. Each band will become one turn of the spiral. Three turns suits a tall, slender column. Four turns is the most common and most forgiving. Five or more turns works on large specimens but demands very consistent spacing.

Sketch the number out on paper before you pick up a pair of shears. It sounds fussy, but five minutes with a pencil saves thirty minutes of second-guessing.

Step 3: Mark the spiral with twine

This is the most important step and the one most DIY guides skip. Without a physical mark, cuts drift.

  1. Tie a length of garden twine to a short cane pushed into the soil at the base of the plant.
  2. Wrap the twine loosely around the plant in a spiral from base to tip, spacing the turns evenly as you go. The twine marks the ribbon of foliage you will leave; the gaps between the twine will be cut back.
  3. Step back and check the proportions. The ribbon width and the gap width should be roughly equal at the widest point of the plant.
  4. Adjust the twine until you are happy, then tie off at the top.

Do not rush this stage. A badly placed ribbon is hard to correct once cutting starts.

Step 4: Cut the recessed sections

Using sharp hand shears or a single-handed electric trimmer, cut the foliage back in the gaps between the twine. Cut gradually, removing no more than a third of the depth in any one pass.

  • Start at the top and work down. This way fallen clippings do not land on sections you have already finished.
  • Keep the cut face vertical on the sides of each recess. Angling back toward the stem makes the spiral look narrow.
  • Step back every few minutes to check the result from a distance. It is very easy to overcut one section and undercut the next when you are working close in.

On yew, you can cut back quite close to the main stem. On box, aim to leave a thin layer of foliage on the stem to avoid bare patches.

Step 5: Refine the ribbon edges

Once the main recesses are cut, remove the twine and trim the edges of the ribbon itself. The upper and lower edges of the ribbon should be crisp straight lines when viewed from the side.

Use hand shears for this stage rather than a mechanical trimmer. The control is worth the extra time. Run one blade of the shears along the upper edge of each ribbon turn, following the angle of the spiral.

Step 6: Shape the top

A spiral nearly always looks better with a defined point or rounded dome at the top rather than an open flat cut. Shape the top into a small cone or round ball before you finish. This gives the eye somewhere to land when looking up the spiral.

Step 7: Clear up and feed

Rake or sweep all clippings away from the base of the plant. Yew berries and foliage are toxic to livestock and children; bag the clippings rather than leaving them on the lawn. Water the plant thoroughly if the weather has been dry.

Apply a slow-release granular fertiliser at the base (follow the manufacturer's rate for the pot or bed size). This encourages dense regrowth into the recesses over the following weeks, which tightens the spiral outline without further cutting.

Annual maintenance

Once the spiral is established, it needs a single clip per year in late August or early September, after nesting season has ended. Repeat the twine-marking step each year; the spiral can drift slowly over successive cuts if you cut by eye alone.

Yew can take a light tidy in late May as well if the new growth has become ragged. Box is better left to a single annual cut to reduce stress and minimise the conditions that favour box blight.

Nesting season reminder

Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to intentionally damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs from 1 March to 31 August. The safe window for topiary cutting in Kent is September to February. Always inspect for nests before cutting, even in September.
