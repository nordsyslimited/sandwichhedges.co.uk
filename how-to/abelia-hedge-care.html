Abelia hedge care in Kent

The five things that make or break an abelia hedge, from when to prune without losing the flowers to keeping it through an East Kent winter.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Abelia care: the five-point checklist

  1. Prune after flowering, not before. October to November is the main pruning window, once the last flowers have dropped. A light tidy in June is fine; a hard cut in late summer removes the flowering spurs.
  2. Keep it as an informal screen, not a tight hedge. Hard clipping suppresses the flowers. Thin out congested stems instead of shearing the whole plant.
  3. Check for nests before any summer tidy. Abelia is dense and flowering from June onwards: birds use it. The safe cutting window for any hedge is September to February.
  4. Plant in well-drained soil with some sun. Abelia tolerates most soils including chalk, but flowers poorly in dense shade. East Kent gardens with some southerly aspect give the best results.
  5. Mulch the base in the first winter. Established plants are hardy in Kent, but young specimens appreciate a 5 cm mulch of bark or compost over the roots when they go in.

What is abelia and why does it make a good hedge?

Abelia grandiflora (glossy abelia) is a semi-evergreen shrub that flowers continuously from June to October on short lateral spurs along its older stems. The flowers are small, tubular, pale pink to white with a faint honey scent, and they are held among glossy leaves that turn bronze-purple in autumn. As a hedge or screen it gives something that privet, laurel and leylandii cannot: sustained flower display through the second half of summer.

It is not a formal hedging plant. The arching, slightly pendulous growth habit means it looks right as an informal boundary screen up to about 1.5 to 2 metres, but it does not clip into a tight wall the way box or yew does. Kent gardens that want structure and flower in the same boundary plant, particularly where the boundary does not need to be a privacy screen from the ground up, find it useful.

In the villages around Sandwich and Deal, abelia planted on a sheltered east-facing or south-facing boundary can still be in flower when the dahlias have gone over. It is worth knowing about.

When to prune: the timing that protects the flowers

Abelia flowers on short lateral spurs from one-year-old and older wood, not exclusively on new growth. That means the pruning timing matters more than it would with, say, privet, which flowers on new wood in summer and mostly gets cut before the flowers have even formed.

The two safe pruning windows are:

  • Late October to November: once the last flowers have finished and before the first hard frosts, remove any dead, crossing or seriously outward-growing stems. This is the main maintenance cut. Do not cut all the way back to bare old wood unless you are doing a renovation: leave some of the lateral spurs that carried flowers this year, because they will carry flowers again next year.
  • Late February to early March: a renovation cut for plants that have grown oversized or congested. Remove a third of the oldest stems at the base. Do not cut more than a third in one year as abelia, though fairly forgiving, recovers slowly from very hard renovation.

A light tidy in June is acceptable if some stems have grown well beyond the line of the hedge. Go over the plant with secateurs rather than a hedge trimmer, and keep cuts above a leaf joint. Avoid any significant pruning between July and late September: that is when the plant is in full flower and when most of the flowering spurs are actively growing or setting up the following year's buds.

Check for nesting birds first

Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is a criminal offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Abelia is dense, in leaf from spring and in flower from June: it is exactly the kind of shrub that wrens, dunnocks and blackbirds nest in. The safe cutting window for any hedge or shrub is September to February. If you need to tidy in June, go over the plant slowly from underneath and look for nests before any cut. If in doubt, leave it.

How to prune: thinning, not shearing

The right tool for abelia is secateurs or loppers, not a hedge trimmer. The hedge trimmer gives a temporary tidy look, but it shears through the flowering spurs rather than selecting which stems to remove, and the cut ends on abelia's larger leaves can look ragged until new growth covers them.

The method is thinning: take out individual stems that are crossing, dead, or simply too far outside the line of the hedge. Cut back to a fork, or remove a stem at the base entirely. What you are aiming for is a shaped but natural-looking screen, open enough inside for air to move through (which reduces disease), with the main flowering growth left intact.

If the hedge has been left for several years and is genuinely too big, the staged renovation approach works well: one third out each year over three years, rather than taking it all back at once.

Soil, feeding and aftercare

Abelia is adaptable and grows well on chalk, loam and clay soils as long as drainage is reasonable. The chalk and well-drained sandy loam soils common around Sandwich and Woodnesborough suit it. Heavy, waterlogged clay is the one thing to avoid: plant in a raised bed or improve drainage if the site is prone to winter standing water.

Feeding is not essential for an established plant, but a top-dressing of general-purpose fertiliser in April and a mulch of well-rotted compost in autumn keeps the flowering performance up on thin chalk soils. Do not feed after midsummer: soft autumn growth is more vulnerable to frost damage.

Winter hardiness in East Kent

Abelia grandiflora is semi-evergreen: in a mild Kent winter it keeps most of its leaves, and in a hard frost it may drop them all and look deciduous for a few weeks. The plant itself is hardy down to about -10 to -15°C in a sheltered spot, which covers virtually every garden in East Kent. What the plant is less tolerant of is the persistent cold easterly winds that come off the North Sea in March and April, particularly in gardens close to Sandwich Bay and Deal seafront. In those spots, a sheltered position against a south-facing wall, or behind a denser windbreak hedge, makes a real difference to how much growth the plant retains over winter and how well the new shoots establish in spring.

In the first winter after planting, a 5 cm bark or compost mulch over the root zone helps insulate the roots and prevents the frost-thaw cycle that can heave newly planted shrubs out of the ground.

Common problems

Abelia has no serious pests in UK gardens. The main issues are:

  • Poor flowering: usually too much shade, too hard a prune at the wrong time, or a plant growing in waterlogged soil. Move it, prune it at the right time or improve drainage.
  • Winter die-back: on exposed sites, some stem tips may die back in a hard winter. Wait until late March to assess, then cut dead material back to live wood. Do not cut immediately after a frost.
  • Leggy base: if the lower stems have become bare, a staged renovation with one third removed per year lets light into the base and encourages new basal shoots over two or three seasons.
