Hedge overhanging a road, pavement or footpath: your legal obligations

Work through the questions below to find where you stand, what a council notice means, and what you are actually required to do.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

In a typical year we get more enquiries about hedges overhanging roads and pavements than almost anything else. Half the time the person is asking because a neighbour has complained, and half because the council has been in touch. The legal position is reasonably clear, but it's not always what people expect. This guide walks through the key questions in order, so you can find your situation and understand what applies to you.

Note: This page covers hedges overhanging or obstructing public highways, pavements and public rights of way. If the question is about a hedge overhanging a neighbouring private garden, that is covered by our separate guide to overhanging hedge branches and your rights.

Question 1: Does the hedge overhang a public highway?

A public highway includes: public roads (both main roads and residential streets), public pavements and footways alongside those roads, cycle paths on the public highway, and public rights of way (footpaths and bridleways shown on the definitive map).

It does not include: a private road or drive serving only your property or a small number of properties, a shared private driveway with no public right of way, or a footpath that crosses private land but has no formal public right of way designation.

If the answer is yes, the public highway framework applies. If no, the issue is either between you and a neighbour (see the links at the end) or there may be no legal obligation at all.

Question 2: What is the nature of the overhang?

Not every overhang creates a legal obligation to cut immediately, but all of them create a risk of one. Think about which category describes your situation.

Category A: Safety hazard

The hedge is obstructing drivers' sight lines at a junction, bend or access, or the overhang is forcing pedestrians into the road to get past. This is the most serious situation. The council has powers under section 79 of the Highways Act 1980 (sight lines at junctions) and section 154 (general obstruction) to require you to act. They can also instruct you to act under section 80 (overhanging trees and shrubs causing danger). You should cut back without waiting for a notice. If you receive a notice, the compliance deadline is real.

Category B: Pavement obstruction (but not immediately dangerous)

Branches are growing over the pavement and reducing its width, but pedestrians can pass without stepping into the road. This is still an obstruction. The council can and often does issue a section 154 notice. In narrow pavements, the standard most councils work to is maintaining a minimum clear width of around 1.5m and a minimum headroom of 2.5m over the walking surface. These are not fixed legal minimums, but they are the practical benchmarks used in most notices. The sensible thing is to cut back voluntarily and avoid the notice process.

Category C: Minimal overhang, no practical obstruction

A small amount of growth has crossed the boundary but it is not reducing pavement width, not affecting sight lines and not causing any pedestrian difficulty. There is no immediate legal obligation on you, but you remain the responsible party if the situation changes. Cutting it back in the normal course of annual maintenance is the right approach.

Question 3: Have you received a formal notice?

Notices under the Highways Act 1980 come from the highway authority, which in Kent is usually Kent County Council Highways or, for a district council matter, Dover District Council or your relevant local authority. They arrive by post and should clearly state:

  • The legal power under which the notice is given (most often section 154).
  • A description of the vegetation and the location.
  • What you are required to do.
  • A deadline for compliance.
  • What will happen if you do not comply (authority carries out the work and charges you).

If you have received one of these notices, read it carefully. The deadline is real. If you cannot comply by the deadline for a legitimate reason (illness, nesting birds, need more time to arrange a contractor), contact the authority immediately in writing and explain. They have some discretion on timelines, particularly for the nesting season. They do not have to grant more time, but many will if asked promptly rather than at the last minute.

Question 4: Who is responsible?

The landowner on whose land the hedge grows. This sounds simple but it can be complicated by:

  • Boundary ownership: If your boundary is a hedge that grows on your neighbour's side of the boundary line, your neighbour (the landowner whose soil the roots are in) is the responsible party for Highways Act purposes. Boundary ownership documents (deeds, Land Registry entries) can clarify this. For a practical first step: the hedge responsibility follows the land, not the fence line.
  • Rented property: The responsible party under the Highways Act is the landowner, not the tenant. A notice will be served on the owner. A landlord cannot routinely pass Highways Act obligations to tenants without specific agreement. If you are a tenant and receive a notice, contact your landlord immediately.
  • Newly purchased property: You take on any existing obligations when you buy. An overgrown hedge that the previous owner neglected is now your responsibility. A notice outstanding at the time of purchase remains valid against the new owner.

Question 5: What happens if you do not comply?

Under section 154(3) of the Highways Act 1980, if you fail to comply with a notice the authority may enter your land, carry out the work themselves and recover the costs from you. The costs are a debt recoverable as a civil debt.

There is also a criminal offence under section 137 of the Act for wilfully obstructing a highway. This is separate from the section 154 notice process and carries a fine. In practice, prosecution for a hedge obstruction without a prior notice and a failure to comply is unusual, but it is a tool the authority has.

If you dispute the notice, the correct route is to respond to the authority in writing within the notice period explaining your grounds for dispute. You can also seek legal advice. Simply ignoring the notice is not a safe strategy: the authority will act and you will be billed.

The nesting season exception

Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981: nesting season 1 March to 31 August

Even a Highways Act obligation does not override the criminal law on nesting birds. If cutting the hedge would damage or destroy an active bird's nest, that is a criminal offence under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981. The nesting season runs from 1 March to 31 August, though birds can nest outside this period too. If you need to cut during the nesting season, inspect the hedge first. If you find an active nest, contact the authority that issued the notice, explain the situation, and ask for an extension. They cannot force you to commit a criminal offence to comply with a civil notice. Document what you found (a photo helps) and keep the correspondence.

The safe trimming window for Highways Act purposes is September to late February: outside both the main nesting season and the wettest period when silver leaf disease risk is highest for Prunus species.

Hedges at junctions and on rural lanes

In East Kent, the roads through Worth, Ash, Woodnesborough and Eastry include a lot of narrow lanes where the hedges on both sides have grown significantly in recent years. These are exactly the situations the Highways Act junction-visibility powers (section 79) were designed for.

If your property fronts onto one of these lanes and your hedge has grown to the point where it creates a blind corner, the risk is not just legal: someone will have a near miss sooner or later. The sight-distance standard most used by highway engineers for rural lanes is a 2.4m x 43m splay (the distance from the road edge to the obstruction on one side, and the visibility distance along the road on the other). This is a technical standard, not a hard legal minimum, but it gives you an idea of what "blocking sight lines" actually means in practice.

If you are cutting back a hedge at a lane junction as a precaution rather than because of a notice, it is worth cutting to well beyond the sight-line zone rather than just to your boundary. The council is less likely to issue a notice, and the road is genuinely safer.

Practical steps if you are in category A or B

  1. Get it measured. Walk the pavement or road side yourself. If you can't pass comfortably without ducking or stepping aside, it is obstructing. Photograph the extent of the overhang from the road for your own records.
  2. Check for nesting birds before you cut. If it is outside the nesting season (September to February), you can proceed. If it is during the nesting season, inspect thoroughly first.
  3. Cut back to a clear margin. For a pavement, aim for at least 2.5m headroom over the walking surface and the hedge face pulled back to your boundary or slightly inside it. For a road, pull any overhanging branches well clear of the carriageway.
  4. Dispose of the cuttings. You cannot leave hedge cuttings on the public highway. If the volume of material is significant, arrange a skip or a trailer in advance. We can take away the cuttings as part of any hedge work we do.
  5. If you have received a formal notice, keep a record that you have complied: photograph the cut hedge and the cleared pavement, note the date, and write to the authority confirming you have done the work.
