Professional vs DIY hedge trimming: an honest comparison

Written by someone who cuts hedges for a living, but genuinely thinks DIY is the right answer sometimes. Here is a straight comparison: what DIY costs you beyond the quoted price, what a professional brings beyond a tidier finish, and the situations where each is plainly the better choice.

Updated September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

There is no universal right answer here. The variables that matter are the hedge's height, species, condition and access. They are also: how much you enjoy garden work, what equipment you own, and whether your weekend is worth more to you than the cost of getting someone else to do it.

This comparison works through the dimensions one at a time. By the end, you should have a reasonable sense of where your situation sits.

The comparison at a glance

Equipment: the hidden cost of DIY

The first time cost of DIY hedge trimming is often higher than people expect. A decent cordless hedge trimmer from a reputable brand (Bosch, Makita, Stihl) starts at around £120 and rises to £350 or more for a model that handles tall hedges and thicker stems. Add a second battery if the hedge is more than 30 metres, an RCD adaptor if you use a corded model, eye protection, ear protection and cut-resistant gloves.

The BBC Gardeners' World Magazine buyer's guide to hedge trimmers covers the main categories well: cordless for flexibility, corded for sustained power on longer hedges, and petrol for heavy renovation work well away from a power source. Worth watching before spending money on equipment you will use twice a year at most.

A professional arrives with commercial-grade equipment, blades serviced and sharpened. The machine cuts faster, more cleanly and through thicker stems than most domestic trimmers. That is relevant mostly for dense hedges or renovation cuts; for a tidy privet in Deal, the difference in finish is marginal.

Time: how long does it actually take?

The quoted time for a hedge cutting job is almost always just the cutting. There is also: setting up sheeting or tarpaulin to catch clippings, blowing or raking up what missed, loading clippings for disposal, and, for DIY, the time to get the equipment out, charge the battery if it is flat, and put everything away again.

An experienced professional cutter is genuinely faster at the cutting itself. What takes a competent amateur two hours, including set-up, will often take a professional forty-five minutes including clearing. That gap is worth money to most people. It is also worth something that you are not spending a warm September Saturday on a ladder.

The argument for DIY on time is that you can cut when you notice it needs doing, rather than waiting two weeks for a booking slot. In late August, when most people want their hedges cut before autumn, a good local cutter in East Kent is often booked up. Booking in spring for an August or September cut avoids this.

Safety: the genuine case for professional help

Falls from ladders are among the most common causes of serious injury in UK domestic gardens. Hedge cutting from a ladder, especially with a powered trimmer, is not a trivial risk. The Health and Safety Executive's own guidance is unambiguous: any work at height should be done only when unavoidable, with secure equipment and ideally without working alone.

For hedges above shoulder height, the practical options are a long-reach pole trimmer (which extends your reach to around 3 metres without a ladder), working from a stable platform rather than a ladder, or hiring a professional who has the right pole equipment and knows how to use it. Our guide to first-time hedge trimming covers the safety basics in more detail.

Hedges near overhead power lines are a category apart. If there is any doubt about whether branches are approaching power cables, get a professional to assess before cutting. Contact with a live power line is fatal; it is not a risk worth managing yourself.

Finish quality: does it actually matter?

On a close-clipped formal yew or box hedge, the finish quality matters a great deal. A slightly wavy top or a face that tapers in the wrong direction is visible at 20 metres and does not improve between cuts. On a loose privet hedge in a back garden that nobody inspects closely, the difference between a professional finish and a competent DIY job is negligible.

The species makes a difference too. Yew, box and hornbeam reward a precise cut. Leylandii, privet and laurel are more forgiving. If your hedge is the focal point of a formal garden, or if you have invested in slow-growing species precisely because you want a refined look, the skill and experience a professional brings is worth the cost.

Our article on trimming a hedge straight covers the string-line and sighting techniques that produce a clean finish if you are doing it yourself.

Waste disposal: underestimated every time

Hedge clippings are bulky. A 20-metre beech hedge in leaf produces a significant pile of waste that does not bag easily and will not fit in a green bin. Most householders either hire a skip, make multiple trips to the household waste recycling centre, or let the clippings sit in a pile for far longer than they intended.

Most professional hedge cutters either include waste disposal in their price or quote it separately. If the logistics of clippings are the reason you have been putting off the job, that alone is often worth the cost of hiring someone. Always check what is included in the quote: some cutters charge for disposal as an add-on.

When DIY makes sense

  • The hedge is below shoulder height throughout and does not require a ladder at any point.
  • You already own suitable equipment in good working order.
  • You enjoy garden work and have time available.
  • The hedge is a forgiving species (privet, laurel, leylandii) where finish precision is less critical.
  • You have a way to deal with clippings on the day (green bin collection, shredder, existing compost system).
  • The hedge is not near a boundary with a public path or road.

When a professional makes sense

  • The hedge is above shoulder height or requires a ladder at any point.
  • The hedge has been neglected and needs a renovation cut deeper than this year's growth.
  • You are paying for precision: formal yew, box, hornbeam, or a hedge that is the centrepiece of the garden.
  • The hedge is near overhead power lines or overhangs a public path or road.
  • Waste disposal is a problem: you have no reliable way to deal with a large volume of clippings.
  • You do not own equipment and would need to buy or hire it for a one-off job.
  • The species needs careful, experienced treatment to avoid dieback (yew, box, hornbeam renovation cuts).
The honest answer is that most domestic hedges in Sandwich, Deal and the villages nearby are completely doable as DIY if you have the right equipment and the hedge is not tall. The ones that come back to us are generally: "I tried it, it's taller than I thought and I don't trust myself on the ladder" or "it's got out of hand over lockdown and I don't know where to start." Both of those are exactly what we're here for.

Nesting season: March to August

Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to disturb an active bird nest. Birds nest in hedges from approximately 1 March to 31 August. Before cutting in that window, check the hedge carefully for active nests. If you find one, leave that section until late August. Our guide on checking a hedge for birds' nests gives the full method.
