Hedges as windbreaks for exposed East Kent gardens

Wind matters more in East Kent than people expect. A hedge that slows it down is more useful than a fence that stops it dead, and the right species makes all the difference in exposed coastal and farmland sites.

Updated September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

People ask us about privacy hedges and height hedges and neat-looking hedges. Fewer people ask specifically about windbreak hedges, but the question comes up regularly when we're working in Sandwich Bay, on the open plots above Deal, or around farms in the Worth and Ash area where the south-westerly really gets going across the fields.

A windbreak hedge is different from a privacy screen in one important respect: the aim is to slow the wind, not block it completely. A solid barrier creates turbulence on the sheltered side, which can be worse than the original wind. A dense but permeable hedge filters it. The question is which species are dense enough, tough enough and appropriate for your particular East Kent site.

Nesting season: the rule for all hedge work

Under the UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs roughly 1 March to 31 August. Windbreak hedges, especially native species like hawthorn and blackthorn, are prime nesting habitat. The safe cutting window for most species is September to February. If you're trimming an established windbreak hedge, check first: watch the hedge quietly before approaching, and look for birds flying in and out of the same spot.

Windbreak vs privacy hedge: what's different?

A privacy hedge is typically evergreen, planted to block a sightline. A windbreak hedge can be deciduous, because wind is a year-round problem but is at its worst in autumn and winter when deciduous species like hawthorn still hold bare twigs and a reasonable amount of branching density. That said, many windbreak species are also good privacy hedges, and the two purposes overlap considerably.

The key difference is density at height. A windbreak works best when it is at least 1.5 m tall and consistently dense from ground level upwards. Gaps at the base let wind tunnel underneath and accelerate, which is worse than a smaller hedge with no gaps.

Monty Don of Gardeners' World demonstrated this approach on his orchard boundary in the November 2024 series, planting a new hawthorn hedge to shelter the space over winter: Gardeners' World 2024 Episode 32 (Gardening With Monty Don, YouTube). Hawthorn's dense winter twig structure is exactly what makes it effective for this purpose.

Coastal sites: elaeagnus and griselinia

For gardens in Sandwich Bay, along the Deal seafront, or on the exposed Pegwell Bay headland, salt spray is the constraint that rules out most species. Elaeagnus and griselinia are the most reliable evergreen choices. Both will take full Channel wind and moderate salt loading.

Elaeagnus (particularly Elaeagnus x submacrophylla or E. pungens 'Maculata') is faster-growing than griselinia and has slightly more texture and density. It clips well and stays evergreen even in hard winters, though in very exposed positions the leaves can brown at the edges after prolonged salt-laden gales. See our full guide at elaeagnus hedge care.

Griselinia grows slightly more slowly but is arguably more elegant, with bright apple-green oval leaves. It is popular in New Zealand and was brought to the UK partly because of its coastal hardiness. In East Kent it has become a standard choice for gardens above the shingle at Sandwich Bay. See griselinia hedge care for trimming timing and variety notes.

Escallonia is a useful third option: smaller than elaeagnus or griselinia, informal in habit, but very salt-tolerant and producing pink or red flowers in summer. It suits smaller coastal gardens or can be used as the front row of a tiered windbreak planting.

Inland exposed sites: hawthorn, blackthorn and the native mix

Across the open farmland east of Sandwich, in the fields around Worth, and along the exposed lanes between Ash and Eastry, the historic windbreak hedge is the native mixed hedgerow: hawthorn, blackthorn, field maple and hazel in a double staggered row.

Hawthorn is the backbone. Its dense, spiny branching structure breaks wind effectively even when leafless in winter, and by spring it is covered in white blossom and teeming with insects. For a pure windbreak, it is hard to beat. A single-species hawthorn hedge 1.8 m tall and 60 cm wide will reduce wind speed in the sheltered zone to roughly 20% of the open field speed.

Blackthorn is thornier and even denser. It also suckers, which is a nuisance in a formal garden setting but exactly the right behaviour for a farm boundary or country hedge: the more established it gets, the more impenetrable it becomes. The sloe berries in autumn are good for wildlife and gin, in that order.

For a mixed native windbreak, planting five species (hawthorn, blackthorn, field maple, hazel, dog rose or elder) in a double staggered row at 45 cm spacing, two rows 30 cm apart, gives maximum density and wildlife value. The mix matters: monoculture windbreaks are more susceptible to disease and offer less habitat variety.

Positioning and orientation

The hedge needs to run across the prevailing wind, not along it. In East Kent, the prevailing wind is broadly south-westerly, coming from the English Channel across the open farmland of the Romney Marsh to the south-west and the Stour Valley to the west. A hedge running roughly north-south intercepts this most effectively.

The sheltered zone extends to roughly ten times the hedge height on the leeward side and two or three times on the windward side. A 2 m hedge shelters a strip around 20 m wide. If you need shelter over a larger area, a series of parallel hedges at intervals is more effective than one very tall single hedge.

Avoid planting a windbreak hedge immediately upwind of a solid structure (wall, building). The hedge deflects wind upward, the building deflects it back down, and the turbulence between the two is worse than open conditions. Leave at least one hedge-height clearance between the hedge and any solid structure.

Planting a new windbreak hedge

Bare-root planting from November to March is the most economical method for a long windbreak run. For a double-row mixed native windbreak, allow 5-6 plants per linear metre across both rows combined, in a staggered pattern. Single-species formal windbreaks (elaeagnus, griselinia, leylandii) are typically planted in a single row at 3 plants per metre.

Ground preparation is worth doing properly. Windbreak hedges are often planted along boundaries where the soil has been compacted, disturbed or repeatedly drought-stressed. Break up the compaction in a trench 40-50 cm wide and 35-40 cm deep. Work in organic matter, and mulch immediately after planting to keep moisture in and weeds down. For a long run in a field or open garden, a biodegradable membrane mulch under bark chippings is practical.

Stake newly planted elaeagnus and griselinia in exposed positions. Until roots establish (12-18 months), plants in full wind exposure can rock loose and die. Individual tree stakes, or a continuous post-and-wire support for a new row, are worth the effort for coastal sites.

Water in dry spells during the first two summers. Exposed sites dry out faster than sheltered ones. A newly planted windbreak cannot do its job if it is also fighting drought stress.

Neighbour and height considerations

A windbreak hedge that benefits you may cast shade or exceed an acceptable height from a neighbour's perspective. The High Hedges legislation (Anti-social Behaviour Act 2003, Part 8) applies to evergreen or semi-evergreen hedges over 2 m if they block light or access to a neighbouring property.
