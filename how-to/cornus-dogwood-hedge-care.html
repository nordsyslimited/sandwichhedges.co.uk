Cornus hedge care: growing dogwood as a garden hedge

Dogwood is one of the few shrubs that earns its place twice: once for its wildlife value in summer, and again for the vivid bare stems that light up a Kent garden in January. Here is how to grow and manage it as a hedge.

Updated September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Cornus (dogwood) turns up in two very different contexts in East Kent gardens. Common dogwood, Cornus sanguinea, is a UK native that grows wild in the hedgerows between Sandwich, Worth and Eastry. It has good berries for birds, autumn colour and the structural resilience of something that has been in these chalk and clay soils for centuries. Then there is the ornamental end: varieties like Cornus alba 'Sibirica' and Cornus sanguinea 'Midwinter Fire', planted for the bare winter stems that glow red, orange and yellow once the leaves have dropped.

As a hedge, cornus suits informal boundaries, wildlife corners and front-garden plantings where you want something that looks good all year without the precision demands of box or yew. It will not give you a sharp clipped line. What it will give you is texture, wildlife interest and, if you manage it correctly, a genuinely striking winter display.

Nesting season: 1 March to 31 August

Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs roughly 1 March to 31 August. Cornus provides excellent nesting cover, especially in the dense ornamental varieties. Do not cut or disturb the canopy in this period unless you have checked carefully and found no active nests. The safe window for most cornus work is September to February. The spring coppice of ornamental dogwood falls in early March; check for any low nests before you start.

Choosing your cornus species

Common dogwood (Cornus sanguinea)

The native species, and the backbone of traditional mixed hedgerows across East Kent. It handles chalk soils around Sandwich, Deal and Sandwich Bay well, and grows readily on the heavier clay around Worth, Woodnesborough and Ash. White flowers in June attract insects; the blue-black berries feed birds through autumn. Grows to 2 to 3 metres if left unpruned, but clips down readily to a maintained hedge height. This is the species to choose if your priority is wildlife value and a sense of the local landscape.

Ornamental dogwoods (Cornus alba and Cornus stolonifera varieties)

These are grown for their winter stem colour rather than structure. Cornus alba 'Sibirica' produces vivid scarlet stems; 'Midwinter Fire' gives a gradient of orange and yellow. Both are manageable at 1 to 1.5 metres with annual coppicing. They work well in a mixed informal hedge alongside hazel, hawthorn or blackthorn, or as standalone ornamental plantings in a wetter corner of the garden, where they are particularly at home. Cornus alba varieties tolerate waterlogged ground better than almost any other hedging plant.

Planting

Bare-root cornus can be planted any time between November and March. Pot-grown plants can go in outside that window, though autumn planting still establishes faster in East Kent's climate where the soil holds moisture through October and November. Space plants 50 to 60 cm apart in a single row for a hedge, or closer if you want faster cover. Cornus is not fussy about aspect and will grow in partial shade, which makes it useful under taller trees or on a north-facing boundary.

No particular soil preparation is needed for the common dogwood on typical East Kent ground. Ornamental varieties benefit from some added garden compost on the chalky free-draining soils around Sandwich, to help retain moisture in the first summer.

Annual care: the pruning calendar

March to early April: coppice ornamental varieties

This is the key job for ornamental dogwood. Cut all the stems back hard, to about 10 to 15 cm above ground level. It looks brutal but it is exactly what the plant needs. The new growth that pushes up over spring and summer produces the brightest stem colour come the following winter. Without this annual or biennial coppice, the stems gradually lose their intensity: older wood is pale and dull compared with young growth. You do not have to coppice every year, every other year works well if you want more height, but every year gives the best colour.

Common dogwood grown as a wildlife hedge does not need this treatment unless it has become very overgrown. It is more tolerant of irregular management.

Late August to September: autumn shape work

Once the nesting season is firmly over, late August onwards is the window for any shaping or height reduction on cornus hedges. This is especially relevant for common dogwood hedges that have grown beyond their intended size over the summer. Cut back to the shape you want; the plant will put on a final flush of growth before the end of the season to soften the cut ends before winter.

November to February: leave it alone (mostly)

The bare winter stems are the point of ornamental dogwood, so resist the urge to tidy them. The stems are at their most vivid in January and February, when frost and low winter light catch them at the right angle. This is the season to look, not cut. You can remove any dead or crossing stems in winter to improve air circulation, but hold the main coppice until March.

Before you start: safety

Before you start

  • PPE: sturdy gloves (cornus stems snap cleanly but can be sharp when freshly cut), eye protection when cutting older woody stems that can flick back. Ear defenders if using a petrol trimmer for any hedge shaping work.
  • Tools: sharp loppers or secateurs for coppicing. A bowsaw for any stems over about 2 cm in diameter. For a larger informal hedge boundary, a cordless hedge trimmer is quicker for shaping, used with an RCD adaptor on a corded model.
  • Ladders: cornus rarely needs cutting above head height, so a ladder is not usually required. If any trimming is above 1.5 metres, a long-reach pole trimmer is safer than a ladder.
  • Wasp nests: check the base of the hedge and any dense areas before starting late-summer work. Cornus can host ground-level wasp nests in a dry August.
  • Nesting check: before any March coppice, part the stems gently and look for any low-level nests from early nesters (robins, wrens). If you find one, wait until it is clearly empty before proceeding.

Common problems

Stems losing colour

The most common complaint with ornamental dogwood: the stems are pale and unimpressive in winter. This is almost always because the plant has not been coppiced recently. Old wood does not produce the vivid colour. Coppice back hard in March and the following winter's display will be dramatically better.

Sparse or leggy growth

Cornus in deep shade or competing with tree roots can become leggy and reluctant to bush out. Move it if you can, or hard-prune to encourage growth from the base. Adding a mulch of compost around the base each spring helps on poor soils.

Aphids on spring growth

Soft new growth after coppicing sometimes attracts aphids. In most Kent gardens the ladybirds and blue tits deal with them within a fortnight. Only consider an intervention if the infestation is severe and persistent.

Cornus in the East Kent garden

Common dogwood grows naturally on the chalk of the North Downs and is a species with genuine local roots, a proper part of the traditional East Kent hedgerow alongside hawthorn, blackthorn, hazel and elder. Planting it as a garden hedge connects the garden to that landscape rather than importing something exotic.

For the ornamental varieties, the mild coastal winters around Deal and Sandwich Bay are broadly helpful: the plants are not stressed by hard, sustained frost, and the winter stems stay vivid longer than they might in a colder inland county. On exposed coastal sites, some shelter helps establishment, but once the root system is established cornus is a tough plant that copes well with the persistent East Kent sea wind.
