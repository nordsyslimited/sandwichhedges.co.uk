Ceanothus hedge care in Kent

California lilac works beautifully as an informal flowering boundary in Kent gardens, but there is a catch: unlike most hedges, it will not come back from the wrong cut. This guide covers the two types, when to prune each, and how hard you can go.

Published September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Ceanothus is one of the most spectacular flowering shrubs you can grow as an informal hedge in Kent. The deep blue flower clusters in late spring can stop people in the street. The problem is that it punishes mistakes. Cut into old bare wood and the plant dies back from that point. There is no second chance, no "it'll push through," no rescue cut the following spring.

The good news is that once you understand the one rule (never cut into old wood), ceanothus is not difficult. The key is knowing which type you have, because the pruning timing is completely different between evergreen and deciduous forms.

Before you start

  • PPE: gloves and eye protection for the cutting work. Ceanothus stems are brittle and can snap sharply.
  • Tools: sharp secateurs for stems up to pencil thickness; loppers for anything larger. A hedge trimmer is not appropriate for ceanothus, it tears the foliage and the fine branches.
  • Nesting season (Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981): it is an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. The nesting season runs roughly from 1 March to 31 August. Evergreen ceanothus is pruned in June or July, so always check for active nests before cutting.
  • Wasps and brambles: check around the base of the plant before reaching in.

Evergreen vs deciduous ceanothus: the key differences

Most ceanothus grown in Kent gardens is evergreen, with clusters of blue or occasionally white flowers appearing in April or May. Deciduous types are less common but produce larger, slightly softer flower heads in late summer. The pruning rules are opposite for each.

Pruning evergreen ceanothus

Prune straight after the flowers have finished, typically in June. The aim is to remove the spent flower clusters and trim back the new growth to keep the plant at the size you want. Work with sharp secateurs, cutting to just above a healthy leaf or bud.

The critical point: do not cut back into the old wood behind the current season's growth. Look at a ceanothus stem and you will see fresh green growth at the tips, then a section of older stem behind it with no live buds. If you cut into that older section, the stem will die back to the main branch. Do this on too many stems and you lose whole sections of the plant.

If the plant is getting too large, the answer is to reduce it a little each year after flowering rather than making a large cut every few years. Gradual reduction keeps it within the green, which is where the buds are.

Pruning deciduous ceanothus

Deciduous types like C. 'Gloire de Versailles' flower on new wood produced in the current season. Prune them in early spring, March or early April, before the new growth gets going. Cut the previous year's stems back to two or three buds from the main framework, similar to the way you would prune a buddleja or late-flowering shrub rose.

This type tolerates a more substantial renovation cut if it has become overgrown: cut back to the main structural stems over two seasons rather than all at once, which reduces the shock to the plant.

Growing conditions and Kent soils

Ceanothus needs full sun and well-drained soil. It actively dislikes waterlogged or heavy clay. Kent's chalk and sandy soils suit it very well, which is one reason you see it growing so confidently on garden walls in Sandwich, Deal and Worth, where the free-draining ground keeps the roots dry over winter.

Coastal conditions in and around Sandwich Bay are generally mild enough for most evergreen varieties, though a severe frost will damage the tips of the current season's growth. A south- or west-facing position against a fence or wall gives the best combination of warmth and shelter. North-facing sites in East Kent, or low-lying frost pockets, are risky for evergreen forms.

Once established, ceanothus is drought tolerant and rarely needs watering in a normal Kent summer. It does not need regular feeding; a mulch of well-rotted compost around the base in spring is usually enough.

Nesting season reminder

Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to intentionally damage or destroy an active bird's nest. The nesting season runs from 1 March to 31 August. Pruning evergreen ceanothus in June or July falls within this window, so always check the plant carefully before cutting. Look for a nest, listen for chick calls, watch for a parent bird returning repeatedly to the same spot. If you find an active nest, wait.

Can I train ceanothus as a formal hedge?

Not really. Because you cannot cut back into old wood, you cannot maintain a tight, clipped formal edge the way you can with yew, box or privet. What you can do is maintain a loose, flowing boundary that keeps its shape with a once-yearly tidy after flowering. It works well as an informal screen, and the annual display is considerably more impressive than a flat green wall.

If you need the boundary to be narrow, choose a wall-trained variety (such as C. thyrsiflorus) and train it against a fence rather than letting it grow as a free-standing hedge. This keeps the plant within a manageable width without requiring cuts into old wood.
