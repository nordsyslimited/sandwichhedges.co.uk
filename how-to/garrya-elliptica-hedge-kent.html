Garrya elliptica as a hedge or screen: comparing it with Viburnum tinus and Lonicera nitida

Garrya elliptica produces long, silver-grey catkins in midwinter when almost nothing else is doing anything interesting. It makes a solid evergreen screen in a sheltered Kent garden, but it is not the right choice for every situation. Here is how it stacks up against the two most commonly planted alternatives.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

What Garrya elliptica actually is

Garrya elliptica is a large evergreen shrub from the west coast of North America. It is grown in British gardens almost entirely for one reason: the catkins. In January and February, male plants produce pendulous catkins 15 to 20 centimetres long, pale grey-green and slightly silky, hanging in clusters from every stem. The effect on a bare winter wall is striking, and it earns the plant its common name of the silk-tassel bush.

Outside catkin season it is a handsome, unremarkable evergreen with leathery dark green leaves. It does not have the fussiness of many wall shrubs, but it does have preferences: it likes a sheltered position, reasonably free-draining soil, and does not appreciate being cut back hard into old brown wood. The cultivar 'James Roof' has especially long catkins and is the one most commonly sold by nurseries.

How the three plants compare

When Garrya elliptica is the right choice

Garrya suits a situation where you have a sheltered wall or fence, particularly south- or west-facing, and you want strong winter interest that Viburnum and Lonicera cannot match. The catkins in January and February, when the garden is at its most bare, make a real difference to a dark wall or north-facing fence corner. In the mildest spots along the East Kent coast, including sheltered gardens in Deal, Kingsdown and around Sandwich Bay, Garrya can be grown without wall protection altogether.

It also suits gardeners who want a plant that largely looks after itself for most of the year. One trim after the catkins finish is genuinely all it needs in a typical season, compared with Lonicera's appetite for repeated clipping. The trade-off is that you cannot renovate it if it outgrows its space: hard cutting produces poor regrowth from old wood, so you need to be reasonably confident it has the room to reach its mature size.

When Viburnum tinus is the better option

Viburnum tinus (laurustinus) is the more practical choice for the majority of Kent gardens. It tolerates clay, chalk, and most other soils in this part of East Kent. It grows in exposed coastal positions where Garrya would struggle. It produces white flowers from autumn through to spring, with pink buds that provide colour even before the flowers open, and the blue-black berries that follow are popular with birds. It also responds well to renovation cutting if it gets out of hand, which Garrya does not.

The main shortcoming of Viburnum tinus is that it lacks the drama of Garrya's catkins. It is a very good, reliable evergreen. It is not a plant that makes visitors stop and look. If dependable year-round cover is the priority and the winter interest is secondary, Viburnum tinus is the lower-risk choice.

When Lonicera nitida is the better option

Lonicera nitida, the shrubby honeysuckle used as hedging, has one genuine advantage: speed. It establishes quickly and fills gaps fast. If you need screening coverage within a season or two and you are prepared to trim three or four times a year to keep it looking respectable, Lonicera nitida does that job well. It is also very coastal-tolerant and handles the salt-laden winds off the English Channel better than either Garrya or Viburnum.

The downside is the trimming commitment. Left untrimmed for a season, Lonicera nitida becomes open and straggly. It also rarely looks as good as a well-established Viburnum tinus or a Garrya in full catkin. It is a utility hedge plant, not a feature one.

Trimming Garrya: timing and technique

Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981: nesting birds

It is an offence to damage or destroy the nest of any wild bird while it is in use or being built. Nesting season runs roughly 1 March to 31 August. Garrya's main trim falls in April or May, which is within nesting season. Check the plant thoroughly for active nests before you begin, and delay if any are found. The September to February window is the safest for any significant cutting work on garden shrubs and hedges.

Trim Garrya once per year, in late spring, after the catkins have dropped. In East Kent this is usually April or May. The aim is to lightly shape the plant, remove any dead or damaged stems, and keep it within its allotted space. Do not shear it mechanically as you would a formal hedge: use loppers or secateurs and work stem by stem.

Avoid cutting back into stems with no leaves on them. Garrya does not regenerate reliably from leafless old wood. If you need to remove a large old stem, take it back to a junction with a younger lateral rather than cutting it flush to old bare wood.

Planting Garrya in East Kent

Plant in autumn (September to November) or spring (March to April), once the risk of hard frost has passed. Garrya establishes slowly, so give it a good start: dig a generous planting hole, mix in some grit if the soil is at all heavy, and incorporate well-rotted compost. Stake the plant firmly in its first couple of years, as the large evergreen leaves catch the wind. Water during dry spells in the first summer.

Against a wall, allow at least 30 to 40 centimetres between the plant and the wall base so rainwater can reach the roots. Wall bases are often very dry even in wet weather.
