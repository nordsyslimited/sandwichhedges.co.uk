The hedge cutting finishing check: how to know when your hedge is actually done

Most people stop cutting when their arms give out. Ten minutes with this checklist is the difference between a tidy hedge and a great one.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Before the finishing check: confirm the hedge is safe to cut

Any finishing pass still involves live blades. Keep PPE on: eye protection, gloves, ear defenders if you're using a petrol trimmer, and sturdy footwear. Keep bystanders back. If you're on a step ladder for the top, have someone foot it. Do not rush the last pass.

Nesting season: 1 March to 31 August

It is an offence to damage a nest in active use. Check before cutting, and if you find birds nesting during the job, stop and wait. This applies to every cut, including the finishing pass.

At a glance: the 8-step finishing check

  1. Step back 5 to 10 metres and assess the overall shape
  2. Walk the full length of the hedge from both sides
  3. Sight along the top line from one end
  4. Inspect each corner for a clean right-angle
  5. Check the taper: slightly narrower at top than base
  6. Look for tramlines along each face
  7. Make a single slow pass for any stray shoots
  8. Clear clippings from the top and from plants below

Each step takes less than two minutes for a standard garden hedge. The sections below explain what you are looking for and how to fix it.

Step 1: step back and look at the overall shape

Put the trimmer down and walk back until you can see the whole hedge in one view, at least five metres, ten if the garden allows. This is the view your neighbours and visitors have. Dips, bulges and height variation that are invisible from a metre become obvious at a distance.

Look for the broad silhouette: is it the same height throughout? Does it bow outward in the middle? Does one end slope away? Note the sections that need attention before picking up the trimmer again.

Step 2: walk the full length from both sides

Walk the hedge from end to end, looking at the face as you go. Do this on both sides if it is a free-standing hedge rather than one against a fence. You are checking for sections that were cut slightly differently: areas where the arm got higher, where the angle changed, where a knot of old growth pushed the blade out. These show up as slight ridges or recesses in the face of the hedge.

Mark any sections you want to revisit mentally or with a piece of string as a sightline.

Step 3: sight along the top line from one end

Crouch or kneel at one end of the hedge and look along the top from ground level, as you would along a length of timber to check for bowing. Any deviation in the top line is clear from this angle. Most people stand and look down at the top of the hedge, which makes it almost impossible to spot a five-centimetre dip over a ten-metre run.

If the hedge follows a sloping garden, decide now whether you want the top to follow the slope or to be level with the horizon. Either is valid, but it needs to be consistent throughout.

Step 4: check the corners

Corners are where the most common errors concentrate, because the trimmer changes direction and the operator often changes posture and arm height at the same time. A soft, rounded corner that was meant to be crisp is one of the first things a visitor notices.

Stand at the corner and look along both outgoing faces. They should meet at a clear angle. If one face overruns the corner, it will give a concave appearance from outside. A single careful pass, holding the trimmer vertically along the corner line, usually sorts it.

Step 5: check the taper

A correctly cut formal hedge is slightly narrower at the top than at the base. Two to five centimetres per metre of height is enough. The purpose is to let light reach the lower branches, which keeps the base dense rather than bare and woody.

A hedge that is wider at the top is one of the most common causes of a bald base, and it develops over years rather than immediately. Check the taper by looking at the cross-section from the end: the sides should lean very slightly inward as they rise. If they lean outward, a corrective pass on the upper section brings it back.

Step 6: look for tramlines

Tramlines are parallel grooves or ridges running in the direction of travel, caused by the blade passing over the same line at a consistent slight angle each time. They are most visible on the flat faces of a formal box or yew hedge in raking light.

To remove tramlines, make a slow second pass with the trimmer held at a contrary angle, essentially the mirror image of the angle that created them. Moving slowly gives the blades time to clear rather than ride over the ridges. Tramlines in a box or laurel hedge will not be visible once the hedge grows on; in yew they can persist longer because yew grows more slowly.

Step 7: the final pass for stray shoots

With the shape confirmed, make one last slow pass along each face, looking specifically for individual shoots that have escaped the main cut. These are most common at the top of a fast-growing hedge (privet, photinia) where the uppermost shoots grow quickly and can stand above the cut surface.

This pass should be methodical rather than rushed. You are not re-cutting the whole hedge; you are picking off outliers. One hand on the trimmer, the other keeping the cable or battery lead clear.

Step 8: clear the clippings

Clippings left sitting on top of a hedge smother new growth and look untidy for weeks. Rake or brush them off the top surface and clear any that have fallen into beds or onto plants below. Box and yew clippings, in particular, should be bagged and taken away if the hedge has had any disease issues, rather than composted or left in beds near susceptible plants.

A quick sweep of the surrounding area completes the job properly, which is something visitors notice even if they cannot say exactly why the garden looks well kept.

The single most useful thing you can do: step back further than feels necessary. Most people check from two metres. The hedge looks very different from ten.
