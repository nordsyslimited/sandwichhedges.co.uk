Staking and sheltering a new hedge on an exposed site

On a sheltered garden most hedging plants stand up by themselves. On an exposed plot near the coast, wind rock can loosen them before the roots take hold.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Planting a hedge in a sheltered Sandwich garden is one thing. Planting along the open edge of Sandwich Bay, or on a field boundary above Deal, is another. The south-westerly arrives with nothing to slow it, and a young plant that rocks in its hole cannot make new roots into the surrounding soil. A gap opens up round the stem, rain and frost get in, and the plant dies back.

Staking and temporary shelter help a new hedge through its first winters. They are a short-term aid, not a permanent fixture.

Who needs staking and who does not

Plant type Stake? Notes
Small bare-root whips Normally no Firm them in well; cut back after planting is often better than a stake.
Taller bare-root or pot-grown plants On exposed sites One stake per plant, or a post-and-wire run for a long row.
Large root-balled or instant hedging Usually yes Heavy tops catch the wind. See instant hedging.
Broad-leaved evergreens near the sea Often, plus shelter Large leaves catch wind in winter. Pair with a temporary screen.

Choosing smaller plants helps too. They put down roots and settle faster than big ones, which is one reason we often suggest smaller stock for exposed plots. See bare-root versus pot-grown hedges.

How to fit a stake

  1. Drive the stake in first, before you set the plant. That way you do not spear the roots. Put it on the side the wind comes from, which in East Kent is usually the south-west.
  2. Keep it short. The top of the stake should reach about a third of the way up the plant. A tall stake whips against the stem.
  3. Use a soft tie. Rubber or cloth tree ties with a spacer, not wire or twine that cuts in. Tie snugly, not tight, so the stem can still move a little.
  4. Firm the soil. Heel or press the soil round the plant so there is no gap round the stem, and water it in.
  5. For a long row, a line of posts with wires strung between them, and each plant tied to the wire, is quicker and neater than individual stakes.

Temporary shelter for the first winters

A barrier of permeable windbreak netting, hessian or woven screening on stakes, set a short distance upwind of the row, takes the edge off a gale and cuts salt-laden spray reaching young leaves. It needs to be porous. A solid sheet creates turbulence on the lee side and can do more harm than good. Take it down once the hedge is established. For permanent shelter planting, see hedges as windbreaks for exposed East Kent gardens.

Check and take off

  • Each spring and autumn: check that ties are not rubbing or cutting in.
  • After any gale: re-firm soil if a plant has rocked, and re-tie anything that has slipped.
  • After about two growing seasons: loosen and take away stakes and ties if the plant stands firmly when pushed gently.

For what else a young hedge needs, read newly planted hedge care and new hedge establishment mistakes. Before planting an evergreen hedge near a boundary, check hedge heights and neighbour law.
