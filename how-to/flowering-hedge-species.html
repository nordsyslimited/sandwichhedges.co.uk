Flowering hedge species for Kent gardens

The best shrubs for a boundary that earns its place in summer: flower timing, height, and which tolerate the Kent coast. No lofty horticultural prose.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Most garden boundaries are evergreen or native mixed, chosen for privacy and wildlife value. That is entirely sensible. But a hedge that flowers, even briefly, pays different dividends: a reason to look at the boundary rather than through it, nectar for early-season bees, and sometimes scent.

The question we hear from customers in Sandwich, Deal and Worth is usually the same: "Will it grow here?" The honest answer depends on two things, soil and exposure, and on the Kent coast those two things can run to extremes. The following eight species are ones we see performing well in the area, with notes on where each tends to struggle.

Escallonia: the coastal workhorse

If you are on the Deal or Sandwich Bay seafront, or any exposed position within half a mile of the coast, escallonia is the first species to consider. It shrugs off salt spray and wind in a way that defeats most other flowering evergreens. 'Red Hedger' is the variety grown most often as a formal hedge: deep pink flowers from June through September, tight small leaves, responds well to cutting.

It performs well on sandy soils and free-draining chalk around Sandwich. What it will not tolerate is a waterlogged winter, so in a wet inland hollow it tends to grow poorly. See our escallonia hedge care guide for cutting schedules and how to rejuvenate an escallonia that has grown leggy.

Viburnum tinus: winter flowers when nothing else is out

Viburnum tinus is unusual in that it flowers through winter: pink buds from November, opening to small white flowers through December, January, February and into April. The flowers are modest individually, but on a hedge they give the boundary a different quality in the months when everything else is bare or brown.

East Kent's mild coastal winters suit it well. It is frost-tolerant but dislikes prolonged hard frost at or below minus 10, which is rarely an issue in this corner of the country. The small metallic-blue berries that follow in summer are a food source for birds. It is forgiving about cutting: trim in April once flowering is largely over, or in late summer.

Rosa rugosa: salt, hips, and bees

Rosa rugosa is one of the most salt-tolerant hedging plants available, which makes it genuinely useful on coastal sites. The flowers are large, pink or white depending on variety, appear from June through August, and are strongly scented. The hips that follow in autumn are substantial, orange-red, and eaten by birds through the winter.

It is a suckering deciduous shrub, so it spreads and fills gaps without being asked. That is useful if you want a dense informal hedge; it is less useful if you need a crisp boundary. For a wildlife-friendly informal hedge on a coastal site near Sandwich Bay, it is hard to beat. See our rosa rugosa hedge care guide for trimming and maintenance.

Choisya ternata: Mexican orange blossom

Choisya is a compact evergreen with glossy aromatic foliage and two flushes of white scented flowers, usually in May and again in September. It makes a rounded, slightly informal hedge rather than a crisp formal one, which suits some gardens better than others.

It dislikes exposed coastal winds and is moderately tolerant rather than fully wind-resistant. In a sheltered town garden in Sandwich or Worth it performs well; on an exposed plot it can brown off at the edges in cold winters. Free-draining soil suits it and it will not tolerate waterlogging. See our choisya hedge care guide.

Forsythia: early spring yellow, then invisible

Forsythia is a reliable early-season performer, producing its yellow flowers on bare wood in March and April before the leaves appear. After flowering it becomes a fairly unremarkable green shrub, which is worth knowing before you commit a whole boundary to it.

That said, the spring display is genuinely cheering, and it is one of the first sources of pollen for emerging bees. The only trim timing issue is that it flowers on the previous year's wood: cut it too hard in summer or autumn and you remove next year's flowering stems. Trim immediately after flowering in April or May. See our forsythia hedge care guide.

Ribes sanguineum: flowering currant

Flowering currant is easy, hardy, and very good for wildlife. The deep pink flowers in March and April provide early-season nectar when little else is available, and birds use the dense growth for nesting. It is fully deciduous, grows on most soils, and tolerates a reasonable degree of shade.

Trim it immediately after flowering, in May. This removes the flowered wood and encourages the vigorous young shoots that will carry next year's flowers. See our ribes sanguineum hedge care guide for more on cutting and renovation.

Deutzia: summer white flowers, low maintenance

Deutzia produces cascades of small white or pale pink flowers in May and June. It is a compact, well-behaved deciduous shrub that rarely causes problems and rarely gets much praise either. If you want summer white flowers on a hedge that stays at 1.2 to 1.8 metres without much intervention, it is a solid choice.

Trim after flowering in June or early July. It grows on most well-drained soils and is reasonably wind-tolerant. See our deutzia hedge care guide.

Abelia: late-season flowers and semi-evergreen

Abelia flowers from July through to October, overlapping with the tail end of escallonia's season and extending well into autumn. The flowers are small, tubular, and attractive to bees and butterflies. It is semi-evergreen: in mild East Kent winters it often holds most of its leaves; in colder winters it drops them.

It stays compact at 1 to 1.5 metres, which makes it useful where height needs to be kept down. It performs best on free-draining soil in a sunny position and dislikes waterlogging. See our abelia hedge care guide for cutting schedules.

Nesting season: check before you trim

It is an offence under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. The nesting season runs roughly from 1 March to 31 August. Many flowering hedge species bloom during this window. For spring-flowering species (forsythia, ribes sanguineum, choisya), you may trim immediately after flowering, usually April or May, but only after inspecting the hedge for active nests. If nesting is in progress, wait. The completely safe cutting window is September to February.

Timing your cuts to keep the flowers

The most common mistake with flowering hedges is trimming at the wrong time and removing the following season's flower buds. Trim shrubs that flower on old wood (forsythia, deutzia, ribes, choisya first flush) immediately after flowering. Trim shrubs that flower on new growth (escallonia, abelia, rosa rugosa) in late summer or early spring before the new flush begins.

Forsythia April-May, after flowering Flowers on old wood
