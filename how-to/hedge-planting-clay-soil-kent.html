Planting hedges in clay soil in Kent

Clay soil covers a good chunk of East Kent. It is heavy, sticky and can waterlog over winter. But it also holds nutrients, and the right hedge species will thrive in it once you get the planting right.

Updated September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

People in Worth, Ash and Woodnesborough tell us the same thing: they bought the plants, dug the holes, put them in, and half of them died. Usually, it's the clay. Not because clay is impossible, but because it behaves differently from the free-draining soils the nursery instructions assume.

This guide covers what actually changes when you're working with clay: species choice, ground preparation, planting technique and the aftercare that keeps new plants alive through their first summer.

Before you start: safety and timing

Hedge planting is physical work. Wear sturdy boots with ankle support on wet clay (it is easy to slip), thick gloves when handling thorny species like hawthorn and blackthorn, and eye protection if using a powered post-hole auger. For longer runs, work in short stretches to avoid back strain from repeated heavy digging. A mattock or digging bar is worth borrowing for compacted or stony clay.

If you're planting into a boundary where an old hedge or shrubs previously grew, check the ground for old root systems or debris that could impede planting or drainage.

The Royal Horticultural Society published a useful short film on the particular challenges of clay soil planting. Their advice on soil structure and drainage improvement matches what we see on the ground in East Kent: Planting in clay soil (The RHS, YouTube).

Which hedge species cope with clay?

Clay varies enormously. There is well-structured clay that drains reasonably and clay that holds standing water in winter puddles for weeks. Start by watching what your soil does after heavy rain: if water sits on the surface for more than a day, treat it as poorly-drained clay and avoid the more drainage-sensitive species.

Good choices for Kent clay

Hornbeam is the standout choice for heavy or damp clay. It is arguably the most clay-tolerant of the formal hedge options, it holds its dead leaves through winter like beech, and it is a UK native that benefits wildlife. For clay sites in and around Woodnesborough, Worth and the low-lying areas near Ash, hornbeam is usually the first recommendation we make.

Hawthorn and blackthorn are native hedgerow species that have been growing in Kent clay for centuries. Hawthorn is better suited to a mixed or informal boundary; blackthorn is thornier and forms an impenetrable hedge once established. Both will handle very heavy clay without complaint.

Field maple is underused as a formal hedge but grows well on clay, tolerates wet winters and contributes good autumn colour. It clips tightly and looks excellent in autumn.

Hazel is tolerant of clay and produces catkins in winter and nuts in autumn. Less formal in appearance than hornbeam but excellent for a boundary hedge where wildlife value matters.

Cherry laurel and Portugal laurel are both tolerant of moderately heavy clay, though Portugal laurel is slightly better drained. Both are evergreen and make a dense screen quickly. Good choices for the rear boundary of a garden on a clay site, though cherry laurel is aggressive if not trimmed regularly.

Leylandii and other conifers can grow on clay, though they prefer it on the drier side. If the clay holds water in winter, add drainage before planting rather than relying on the trees to dry it out.

Privet is one of the most forgiving hedges going and will tolerate clay reasonably well. It is semi-evergreen and needs two or three cuts a year but can fill a gap cheaply and quickly.

Species to avoid on poorly-drained clay

Yew will die in wet clay. It needs free-draining soil, full stop. On chalk or sand soils in Kent it is outstanding; on waterlogged clay it will fail.

Box on heavy clay is a risk. Box prefers free-draining, moderately fertile soil. On wet clay it is vulnerable to box blight (which is already a serious concern across Kent gardens regardless of soil), and the drainage conditions can stress it. If you have clay soil and want a formal evergreen hedge, laurel or hornbeam are safer bets.

Photinia (red robin) prefers well-drained, moderately fertile soil. On heavy clay it tends to be slow, prone to phytophthora root rot and generally disappointing. Save it for better-draining ground.

Step 1: Prepare the ground

Ground preparation makes a far bigger difference on clay than on other soil types. Skip it and you get plants sitting in compacted, waterlogged pockets; do it properly and you give roots the loose, aerated zone they need to push out into.

Mark your planting line. For a single-row hedge, dig a trench roughly 45cm wide and 40cm deep along the full planting run rather than individual holes. On stiff clay, individual holes can behave like pots, pooling water around the roots. A trench allows you to break up the surrounding soil structure and distribute organic matter evenly.

Fork over the base of the trench. On clay, the base of the hole often seals into an impermeable layer called a pan. Break this up with a fork to allow downward drainage. If the pan is very hard, use a digging bar.

If the clay is very heavy, mix a small amount of coarse grit into the base and lower sides of the trench. Do not add a thick layer of grit at the bottom, as this can create a lens that holds water above the grit layer rather than draining through it. Work it in thoroughly with a fork.

Mix in well-rotted garden compost or leaf mould across the full trench. Work it into the upper half of the trench, not just the hole: you want to improve the zone roots will grow into over the first two years, not just the immediate planting pocket. Two or three spadefuls per metre run is about right.

Step 2: Planting

Bare-root plants are the most economical choice for a hedge run and they establish well in clay if planted correctly. The bare-root season runs from November to March, which is also when clay is most workable (though not when it is frozen or waterlogged).

Soak bare-root plants in a bucket of water for 30 minutes before planting. Do not leave them soaking for hours; you just want the roots re-hydrated after transit.

Set plants at their correct spacing: 3 plants per metre for most species in a single row; 5 per metre for a double staggered row. See our planting spacing guide for species-by-species figures.

Hold the plant at the correct depth (the soil mark on the stem should sit at ground level or just below), spread the roots out into the trench without coiling them, and firm the soil back in around the roots in layers. On clay, this firming step is particularly important: clay compacts but also leaves more air pockets than it appears to. Tread the soil in firmly with your boot after backfilling, then check that the plant does not rock.

Leave a slight depression around each plant to hold water during the first dry spell, rather than a mound that sheds it.

Step 3: Mulch immediately

Clay soils behave badly at the surface. They crack when they dry out in summer and cap over after rain in winter, forming a skin that roots cannot penetrate. A mulch of bark chippings, wood chip or straw 7-10cm thick, kept a few centimetres clear of the plant stems, prevents both problems. It also suppresses the weeds that compete heavily with new hedge plants.

Lay the mulch immediately after planting, before the ground has a chance to firm back over. Maintain it through at least the first two growing seasons.

Step 4: Watering on clay

This sounds counterintuitive: clay holds water, so why water new hedges? The answer is that clay drains poorly but it also holds onto water so tightly in dry spells that roots cannot extract it easily. In a dry East Kent summer, even a clay site can dry out enough to stress new plants seriously.

Water new hedges deeply in dry spells during the first two summers: a thorough soaking once a week is better than a light daily sprinkle. Water at the base of the plants, not over the foliage.

Once the hedge is established (after two or three growing seasons), most clay-tolerant species will manage without supplemental watering in a normal Kent summer.

Step 5: Formative pruning

New hedge plants need formative cutting in the first two or three years to build density, regardless of the soil type. For deciduous species, cut back by about a third on planting day, then follow a year-by-year programme. See our formative hedge pruning guide for the full cutting regime.
