Spiraea hedge care: pruning and growing spiraea in Kent

Spiraea is one of the most common informal hedging and border plants in East Kent gardens. It flowers prolifically, tolerates most soils and handles hard pruning without complaint. The catch: cut it at the wrong time and you lose the flowers entirely.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Spiraea care: the short version

  • Spring-flowering varieties (arguta, vanhouttei): prune immediately after flowering, late May to June.
  • Summer-flowering varieties (japonica and cultivars): prune hard in early spring, February to March.
  • Never prune in nesting season (1 March to 31 August) if the plant is dense enough to hold a nest.
  • Hard renovation is fine: cut to 30-40cm from the ground in early spring to rejuvenate a congested plant.
  • One annual prune is enough for most established spiraea. Two passes (post-flower tidy plus an autumn shape) for fast hedges.
  • Avoid blunt shears: spiraea is twiggy and thin-stemmed; sharp secateurs or hand shears give a cleaner result than a hedge trimmer.

Spiraea is deciduous, which puts it in a different category from most formal hedging. You will not be trimming it to a geometric box shape with a long-reach electric trimmer: the technique is closer to shrub pruning than hedge cutting. That said, a well-established row of spiraea makes an attractive informal boundary, and some of the larger-growing varieties (particularly S. x vanhouttei) can form a dense, arching hedge two metres or more in height that most people would not walk through.

Which spiraea do you have?

The pruning timing depends on the variety. The two main groups are:

Spring-flowering spiraea (prune after flowering)

Spiraea arguta (bridal wreath) and Spiraea x vanhouttei are the most common. They flower on wood produced in the previous year, in a mass of small white flowers in April and May. Pruning before flowering removes this year's flower buds. The correct time is immediately after the flowers finish, which in East Kent is usually mid to late May, or into June in a cool spring.

Prune by removing up to a third of the oldest, thickest stems completely at the base, and shortening the remaining stems by up to a half. This opens up the centre of the plant, encourages new growth from the base, and keeps the overall size in check. A hedge of S. x vanhouttei can be tipped back lightly at the same time to keep the boundary line tidy.

Summer-flowering spiraea (prune in early spring)

Spiraea japonica and its many cultivars (Anthony Waterer, Goldflame, Little Princess) flower on growth produced in the current year, with pink, red or crimson flower heads appearing from June through to August. These are cut back hard in late February or early March, before growth starts. Cut all the previous year's flowered stems back to a low framework of main branches, 15-20cm from the ground. This looks severe but the plant regenerates quickly and produces better flowers on vigorous new growth than on old, congested stems.

S. japonica is the variety most often planted as a low informal hedge or edging, particularly the compact forms like Little Princess. It rarely grows more than 0.6-0.9 metres tall, which puts it into a different scale of maintenance from the taller vanhouttei.

Dealing with an overgrown spiraea

Spiraea that has not been pruned for several years becomes congested: a tangle of old stems, dead wood in the centre, poor flowering and gradual spreading at the edges. The remedy is renovation pruning, which spiraea handles very well.

In early spring (February to March, before growth starts), cut the entire plant back hard to 30-40cm from the ground. Remove dead and diseased wood entirely. The regrowth in the first season will be vigorous but the plant may not flower. By the second season it will have formed a new framework and be flowering well again.

For a large hedge of spiraea, you can stagger the renovation over two years: cut half the plants hard one year, the other half the following year. This preserves some flowering and some hedge height while the renovation is underway.

Nesting season applies to spiraea too

Dense established spiraea, particularly S. x vanhouttei, is good nesting habitat for wren, dunnock and other small birds. Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, damaging an active bird's nest is an offence. Nesting season runs from 1 March to 31 August. Check the plant carefully before any pruning or renovation work during this period. If you are in doubt, wait. The plant will not suffer for an extra few weeks.

Spiraea as an informal hedge in East Kent

In cottage-style gardens from Sandwich to the villages around Ash and Woodnesborough, spiraea features regularly as an informal boundary hedge or front-garden divider. S. x vanhouttei in particular works well along low walls and as a gentle, arching boundary that looks welcoming rather than defensive.

It is not the right plant for a formal, geometric hedge: the arching growth habit resists being cut flat on all sides. But for a soft, flowering boundary that needs minimal intervention and provides reliable early-season nectar for bumblebees and other pollinators, it is hard to beat.

A few practical notes for East Kent conditions:

  • Spiraea is generally drought-tolerant once established, which suits the drier summers of the East Kent coast.
  • The soils around Sandwich, Ash and the Stour valley vary from sandy to clay. Spiraea tolerates both, though it establishes faster in a reasonably well-drained soil.
  • Birds (particularly bullfinch in wooded parishes) occasionally take spiraea flower buds in late winter. This rarely causes serious harm but can reduce flowering on stems that were targeted. It is not a reason to delay pruning.

Before you start: PPE and tools

  • Good sharp secateurs are the primary tool. Long-handled loppers for older, thicker stems.
  • Hedge shears or a light electric trimmer can be used for a final shape on informal hedges, but are not the right tool for the main renovation cut.
  • Gloves: spiraea is not thorny, but the cut stems can be sharp at the tips.
  • Eye protection if using any powered tool.
