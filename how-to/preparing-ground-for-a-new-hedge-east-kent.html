Preparing the ground for a new hedge in East Kent

The hour you spend on the soil before planting matters more than the price of the plants. Most failed hedges were let down by the ground, not the stock.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Planting is quick. Preparation is where a hedge is won or lost. In East Kent the ground changes fast: shingle and sand at Sandwich Bay, thin soil over chalk on the rises above Deal, heavier loam and clay in pockets around Worth, Ash and the Stour valley. Each wants a slightly different approach.

Checklist: ground preparation in order

  • Mark the line and check for buried services.
  • Check the boundary position and likely mature height.
  • Test drainage with a water-filled hole.
  • Clear perennial weeds from a strip at least 1 m wide.
  • Dig and loosen a trench, 40 to 50 cm wide.
  • Improve the soil across the whole trench, not just the holes.
  • Plant, firm in, water and mulch.

1. Mark the line, check services and the boundary

Run a string line along the row so it stays straight. Before digging, check where water, gas, electric and drain runs are. Keep the row well away from drains and foundations: roots follow moisture. Our guide to hedge roots, foundations and drains covers sensible distances by species. Also think about how wide the hedge will get and how tall, especially next to a neighbour. The neighbour law guide and shared boundary planting cover the points to settle first.

2. Test the drainage

Dig a hole about 30 cm deep and fill it with water. Once it drains, fill it again and time it. The second filling tells you how the soil behaves:

  • Drains within minutes: free-draining sand or shingle. Plants will need regular watering. Read hedge planting on sandy soil.
  • Drains over a few hours: typical loam. Good for most hedges.
  • Still standing after many hours: poorly drained clay or a high water table. Pick suitable species, see hedge plants for wet soil and clay soil planting.

If the garden sits over chalk, expect thin, stony, alkaline soil that dries quickly. See hedge planting on chalk.

3. Clear perennial weeds first

Couch grass, bindweed, bramble and ground elder will outgrow and starve young hedge plants. Clear a strip at least a metre wide, digging out the roots rather than just cutting the tops. Our guide to weeds at the hedge base covers the worst offenders. If you use weedkiller, keep it well clear of the plants and read weedkiller drift damage first.

4. Dig and loosen the trench

Dig a trench about 40 to 50 cm wide and 30 to 40 cm deep, breaking up the base with a fork. Compaction is common along old boundaries and new-build plots, and a hard layer under the plants makes roots turn sideways in the trench. Full planting detail is in trench planting a formal hedge and hedge planting spacing.

5. Improve the soil across the trench

  • Sand and shingle: mix in well-rotted organic matter to help it hold water and nutrients. Mulch heavily afterwards.
  • Chalk: dig in organic matter too, and choose species that tolerate lime. Do not expect to make chalk acid.
  • Clay: add grit and organic matter to open it up, and plant slightly proud on a low mound where water sits.
  • All soils: mix amendments through the whole trench. Filling only the holes with rich compost leaves roots reluctant to spread beyond it.

6. Plant, firm, water and mulch

Plant at the same depth as in the pot or nursery, firm the soil, soak well and mulch. Then follow the aftercare in how to water a new hedge and newly planted hedge care. If the site is exposed, read staking and sheltering a new hedge too.
