Training a new leylandii screen in years one, two and three

Most people plant leylandii and step back. The first three years of formative cuts are what separates a dense, well-shaped screen from a top-heavy one with bare gaps at the base.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Leylandii is the go-to screening plant in gardens across Sandwich Bay, Deal and the coast, and for good reason: nothing else gives you a windbreak as fast. The trouble is that the speed works against you if you leave the hedge to its own devices in the first few years. You end up with a tall, straggly screen that is thin at the base and almost impossible to fix without cutting back to bare wood, which leylandii will not regrow from.

Three years of the right formative cuts solves this. The approach is straightforward, but it runs counter to what most people want to do, which is to let it grow as quickly as possible and worry about shape later.

Before you start: safety

Leylandii grows fast. A screen planted at 1.2m can reach 2m or taller within three years, and you will need a step ladder for the top cut once it gets there. Work with a second person when using a ladder. Wear eye protection (chips and sawdust), ear defenders if using a petrol trimmer, and sturdy gloves. Use an RCD adaptor with any electric trimmer. Before cutting, walk the length of the hedge and check for wasp nests, hidden wires and brambles growing through the base. A pole-mounted trimmer removes the need for a ladder for side cuts up to about 2.5m.

Nesting season

The UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs roughly 1 March to 31 August. The safe cutting window is September to February for the main formative cuts. A light side trim may be done in late May or June only if you have checked carefully and the hedge is clearly empty of nests.

Year one: the formative cut you probably won't want to make

In year one (or at planting if you are putting in bare-root plants), the counterintuitive move is to trim the sides. Not the top, just the sides, lightly, by a few centimetres. This encourages the lower branches to thicken up and send out side shoots, rather than putting all their energy into reaching for the sky.

This cut is usually done in late May or early June, once new growth is properly established. If you planted in autumn or winter, wait until the following spring before touching anything. The plant needs one full growing season to settle its roots before it can afford the energy required to respond to cutting.

Do not reduce the top in year one. The goal at this stage is a bushy base, not maximum height. If the plants are going thin at the bottom already, a light side trim now will pay dividends in years two and three when the hedge needs to look good from knee height up.

One regional note: leylandii planted in the sandy coastal soils around Sandwich Bay often puts on less growth in year one than the same plant in heavier loam inland. This is normal. Do not be tempted to add extra feed to compensate; let the roots establish at their own pace and the growth will come in year two.

Year two: trimming both sides and starting to shape the top

By year two, the hedge should have put on considerable growth. This is when the shaping work becomes more serious.

Trim both sides in late May or June (checking carefully for nests before you start). Keep the cuts light: you are still encouraging density, not reducing bulk. Never cut back to bare brown wood, and keep each trim to no more than a third of the current green growth.

If the hedge has reached or exceeded your target height, you can start to address the top now. Keep the top cut level and slightly narrower than the base. This brings us to the batter, the slight inward taper that runs from base to top. The base of the hedge should be around 10 to 15 per cent wider than the top. This taper means the lower branches receive light from above rather than being shaded out by the upper growth. It is worth introducing this profile in year two, because it becomes much harder to achieve once the hedge reaches full height.

In year two, most hedges in East Kent benefit from a second cut in late August or September. This is the main cut: sides and top together. The hedge will put on a last flush of growth before autumn and the cut edges will harden up nicely before winter.

Year three: the maintenance regime begins

By year three, the hedge should be approaching its intended size. The formative phase is almost done and you are moving into a regular maintenance pattern.

Cut twice a year: once in late May or June (sides only if the hedge is still thickening, or sides and top if it is at height), and again in late August or September for the main cut covering both sides and the top. Two cuts per year suits leylandii well in the East Kent climate.

If the hedge has reached its target height, be consistent about maintaining it. Leylandii grows at 75 to 90 centimetres per year, so skipping a summer means an extra metre to deal with the following year. Once the hedge has overshot your target by a metre or more, you are in difficult territory, because bringing the height back down means cutting into older wood where regrowth is slow or absent.

For height limits and your obligations to neighbours once a conifer hedge exceeds 2 metres, see our leylandii and high hedges guide.

What not to cut: the brown wood rule

Leylandii (and almost all conifers except yew) will not regrow from old brown wood. If you cut back beyond the green growth, you are left with a permanent bare patch. This is the single most common mistake we see, and it is why so many established leylandii screens have those brown bald patches along one or both sides.

Always cut well within the green growth. Never remove more than a third of the green in a single cut. This applies at every stage, but it is especially critical on the sides in year two: if you allow the hedge to spread too wide before you start trimming, you cannot reclaim the shape without cutting into brown wood.

The good news is that if you follow the year-one and year-two regime above, you will never find yourself in that position. Light, consistent cutting from the start keeps the green growth zone thick all the way to the outer surface of the hedge, which means you always have something to cut back to.
