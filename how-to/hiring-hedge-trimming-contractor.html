Hiring a hedge trimming contractor: what to ask before you book

A 12-point checklist for getting the right person, the right price and the right result, from a team that trims hedges for a living across Sandwich, Deal and the villages of East Kent.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Most people hire a hedge cutter through word of mouth, a local Facebook group or a quick Google. That is fine as a starting point. But before anyone puts a blade near your hedge, there are a dozen things worth checking. Some are about protecting you legally; some are about protecting your hedge from a botched job; and one is about protecting the birds.

Checklist at a glance

  1. Public liability insurance confirmed in writing
  2. Nesting season checked (do not book March to August without a bird-check clause)
  3. Waste removal: agreed in scope and included in the price, or excluded clearly
  4. Written quote covering all hedges and the finish standard
  5. Access agreed (gates, parking, neighbours)
  6. Boundary clarified (whose hedge, and which sides)
  7. Tools to be used (trimmer type, long-reach for tall hedges)
  8. Nesting check before starting: in writing if possible
  9. PPE and safe working practices confirmed
  10. Payment terms clear, and no large upfront deposits
  11. References or reviews checked for a new contractor
  12. Confirm the contractor is the person doing the work, not a subcontractor

Here is what each point means in practice.

1. Public liability insurance

This is non-negotiable. If a contractor damages your property, nicks a power line, or breaks a fence panel belonging to a neighbour, their public liability insurance pays for it. Without it, you may end up liable. Ask for the insurer's name and policy number, and check the policy has not lapsed. A reputable contractor will have this to hand without hesitation. Anyone who is evasive about it is a red flag.

2. Nesting season timing

Under the UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs roughly 1 March to 31 August. The safest cutting window for most hedges is September to February. If you are booking work inside the nesting window, a responsible contractor should carry out a visual check of the hedge before starting and should refuse to cut if active nests are found. Ask them explicitly what they will do if they find a nest, and be cautious about any answer that involves cutting anyway.

3. Waste removal

A long hedge produces a surprising volume of cuttings. Whether the contractor takes them away, piles them for your compost heap, or loads them into a green waste bin is something to agree before the job starts. Removal costs time and disposal fees, so it is rarely included automatically in a basic quote. If it is listed as extra, get the cost upfront. Leaving a large pile of cuttings on the pavement or your drive is nobody's idea of a finished job.

4. Written quote

A verbal quote is fine for small jobs with a contractor you know well. For anything larger, get it in writing. The quote should state which hedges are included, whether both sides are being cut, the finish standard, whether waste is included and the total price. This protects both you and the contractor if there is any disagreement on the day.

5. Access and parking

Hedge trimming involves moving equipment around the garden, possibly including a van with a trailer. Check whether the contractor needs access through a side gate, whether there is parking nearby, and whether any work on the front hedge will temporarily affect the pavement. In narrow village lanes around Sandwich, Deal, Ash and Woodnesborough, parking can be genuinely difficult: worth sorting out in advance.

6. Boundary and ownership

Whose hedge is it? If the hedge sits on or near a boundary, clarify which sides the contractor is cutting, and that both you and (if relevant) a neighbour have agreed to the work. Cutting the neighbour's side of a boundary hedge without agreement can cause dispute, and a good contractor will want this clear before starting. Our guide to hedge boundary ownership covers the basics of who owns what.

7. Tools and equipment

For a hedge under about 1.5 metres, a standard electric or petrol hedge trimmer works well. For tall hedges, ask whether the contractor has a long-reach trimmer or will need to use a ladder. Ladders on sloped or uneven ground increase the risk of accidents, and a long-reach pole trimmer is safer for most situations. A contractor who only has a standard trimmer and plans to balance on a stepladder for a two-metre hedge is worth questioning.

8. Nesting check before starting

Even outside the main nesting season, it is good practice for any contractor to walk the hedge briefly and look for signs of bird activity before starting. This should take five minutes and costs nothing. Ask whether this is part of their routine. It is for us, and it should be for anyone else cutting hedges professionally.

9. PPE and safe working

Hedge trimming carries real risks: flying debris from trimmers, cuts from blades and electric shock from cutting through a cable. Any professional contractor should be using eye protection, ear defenders (for petrol trimmers especially) and sturdy gloves as a matter of course. If you see a contractor starting a job without any PPE, that tells you something about their standards generally.

10. Payment terms

Most small garden contractors expect payment on completion, or within a few days. Be cautious about requests for large deposits before work begins, particularly from contractors you have not used before. A 10–20% deposit for materials on a large hedgerow job is reasonable; paying 50% upfront to a stranger is not. Cash-only requests are worth noting: not necessarily a sign of anything wrong, but a receipt should always be available.

11. References and reviews

For a new contractor, asking for a couple of local references is entirely reasonable. Most contractors doing good work locally will have happy customers nearby. Online reviews (Google, Facebook) are useful too, though the more recent the better. One or two bad reviews among many good ones is normal; all reviews older than a year, or very few reviews at all, is worth factoring in.

12. Who actually does the work?

If you are booking through a larger company or an app-based service, clarify whether the person quoting is the person doing the work. Subcontractors are common in the trade, and not always a problem, but it does mean the person you vetted may not be the one who turns up. For a regular job with a local firm, this is usually straightforward. For a one-off booking through a platform, it is worth asking.
