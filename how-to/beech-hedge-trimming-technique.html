How to trim a beech hedge in late summer: timing, technique and winter colour

Get the cut in by mid-September and the hedge enters winter with a clean profile and that coppery-brown covering that lasts until April. Wait too long and the winter silhouette loses the quality that makes beech worth growing. A practical guide from people who cut beech hedges across East Kent every August and September.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Beech is the hedge where timing makes the most visible difference. Get the cut in by mid-September and the hedge enters winter with clean, well-defined edges and a full covering of coppery-brown leaves that last until spring. Wait too long and you get a rough outline with many leaves shed early, and the winter silhouette loses the quality that makes beech worth growing.

The technique matters too. Beech is not as forgiving as privet, but it is not as demanding as yew. This guide covers both: when to cut and how to cut, with the specific details that give a beech hedge a reliably good result year after year.

The nesting season check

The UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs roughly from 1 March to 31 August. By mid-August, most birds have finished breeding, but late-nesting species (wood pigeons, house sparrows, collared doves) occasionally raise a final brood into September. Walk both sides of the hedge and check the canopy carefully before making the first cut. See our guide on wildlife-friendly hedge trimming for the full legal detail.

Before you start: safety

  • Eye protection: safety glasses or goggles. Flying stem fragments travel several metres, and cutting above shoulder height is common on taller beech hedges.
  • Ear defenders: for petrol or electric trimmers.
  • Gloves: cut-resistant gloves, particularly if working near the base where stems are thicker.
  • Ladder caution: for beech hedges over 1.5 metres, a long-reach (pole) hedge trimmer is safer than a ladder. If a ladder must be used, have someone footing it and never overreach sideways.
  • Blades clean: beech sap hardens quickly on blades. Clean and lightly oil before starting.

When to cut: the August-September window and why it matters

Beech (Fagus sylvatica) is marcescent, meaning it retains its dead leaves through winter rather than dropping them cleanly in autumn. This is what gives a beech hedge its distinctive bronze appearance from October through to April, when the new spring growth pushes the old leaves off.

The connection between timing and winter appearance is direct. When you cut, you expose fresh shoot tips. If the cut is made while the leaves are still green and actively growing, the remaining foliage adjusts and the hedge enters dormancy with the characteristic bronze colour intact.

If the cut is made after the leaves begin to turn, in October or later, the exposed tips remain visibly fresh-cut through the winter. The hedge looks slightly ragged at the edges, and more leaves tend to drop early rather than hanging on. It is not fatal for the hedge, but the visual quality in winter is noticeably lower.

The practical target in East Kent is mid-September. The colour change on beech along the North Downs ridge and in the villages around Sandwich typically begins in the second half of September, slightly later than further inland because of the coastal influence. The earlier end of September is usually safe, but watch the hedge: if leaves begin yellowing before mid-September, cut immediately.

How much to cut

A beech hedge responds well to being cut back firmly to the previous year's cutting line. This means removing the full season's extension of growth. For an established beech hedge in reasonable condition, this is usually 15 to 25 cm on the top and 10 to 15 cm on each side, depending on how strong the growing season has been.

Do not cut into the dark brown older wood unless the hedge needs renovation. Fresh green growth is forgiving; old wood on beech regrows slowly and the results can look sparse for two or three seasons. For a hedge that has been left for more than two years, see our guide on hard pruning versus light trimming before deciding how much to take.

The technique, step by step

Step 1: cut the top first

Start with the top of the hedge, working along its full length before moving to the sides. Maintain a slight inward slope so the base of the hedge is marginally wider than the top. This taper, the batter, is especially important on beech: a hedge cut with perfectly vertical sides loses foliage at the base more quickly than one with a gentle A-frame profile, because the lower leaves get less light. Even a few degrees of inward angle makes a difference over several seasons.

For a beech hedge over about 1.5 metres, a long-reach (pole) hedge trimmer makes the top cut much easier and allows the batter to be maintained consistently. The extended reach also means you stay on the ground rather than on a ladder, which is safer. See our guide on cutting tall hedges safely for the access options.

Step 2: clear the top before moving to the sides

Before cutting the sides, rake or brush the clippings off the top of the hedge. This takes two minutes but prevents the accumulated weight of beech clippings from pulling any outer branches downward and distorting your side-cut line. Beech clippings are denser than privet and compact quickly when they pile up.

Step 3: cut the sides in overlapping vertical passes

Starting at the top corner of each side, work downward in overlapping passes (10 to 15 cm overlap). Keep the bar at a slight angle so the top of the cutting bar is fractionally closer to the hedge than the bottom: this produces the batter naturally without any measuring.

Beech leaves are larger than privet or box, and a blade that is not sharp will tear them rather than cut cleanly. Torn leaves brown more quickly at the edges. If the cut finish looks ragged, the blades need attention before you continue.

Step 4: check from a distance

Once each section is done, step back five metres and look down the line. Slight dips and rises that are invisible from close up become clear from distance. Adjust and re-cut before moving on. This is worth doing every five to ten metres on a long run, particularly on the top where any variation is immediately obvious in winter when the leaves are set.

Step 5: clear up carefully

Beech clippings compact firmly when wet and can smother grass if left on the lawn. Rake them promptly into a compostable bag or onto a tarpaulin. Fresh beech clippings are good compost material but take longer than grass clippings to break down; a hot heap with added nitrogen (grass clippings mixed in) speeds the process.

What to expect after the cut

A well-timed late-summer beech cut typically shows the following over the following weeks:

  • A short flush of new growth within two to three weeks, especially if the weather is warm and mild, as it often is in East Kent through September and into October.
  • Colour transition: remaining leaves begin to turn bronze from late September. The hedge locks into winter colour by October.
  • By November, the hedge should have a clean profile with a dense coppery-brown covering that persists until April.

If the new growth after the cut is excessive, it is usually not worth cutting again: a brief autumn flush on beech will not damage the hedge and the new tips simply add to the winter colour. Only cut again if the regrowth is substantial enough to significantly blur the finished line.

Dealing with tall beech hedges

Beech hedges over 1.5 metres increasingly need a tall-hedge approach for the top cut. Many of the beech hedges around Sandwich's older properties, and in village gardens across Worth, Ash and Eastry, have been in place for decades and reach 2 metres or more.

For these hedges, a long-reach pole trimmer handles the top cut comfortably without a ladder. The head angle typically needs to be set nearly horizontal for the top (0 to 15 degrees), switching to 90 degrees for the sides. The batter principle still applies: the head should be angled slightly inward on the sides even when using a pole trimmer. It takes a little practice to maintain the angle consistently, but the results over several seasons are clearly better than a vertical cut.

STIHL GB's official YouTube channel demonstrates long-reach hedge trimmer technique on a mature garden hedge, showing top and side cuts and the head angle adjustments used at different heights. The same technique applies directly to beech hedges over 1.5 metres.
