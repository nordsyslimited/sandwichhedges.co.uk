Disinfecting hedge cutting tools: stop box blight and leaf spot spreading

One small step between plants can stop a fungal disease that has no cure once it takes hold. Here is exactly how to do it.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Tool hygiene is one of those things that seems fussy until you have spread box blight from the front garden to the back in a single session. We have seen it happen. A hedge that looked fine on a Monday is showing yellow patches by the following week, and the only thing that changed was which hedge the trimmer visited first.

Box blight, photinia leaf spot, fireblight, Phytophthora: all travel on contaminated blade residue. The spores are microscopic, the journey is short, and the damage lasts for years. Disinfecting takes four minutes. It is not a complicated job.

Before you start

Always disconnect or power down any tool before cleaning or handling blades. Wear close-fitting gloves when wiping blade edges. Avoid getting disinfectant solutions directly onto plant foliage or roots, particularly bleach-based products. Work on a stable surface and keep the tool flat until blades are fully dry before storing.

Nesting season: 1 March to 31 August

This guide covers tool care. But before any cutting work on a hedge, check for active bird nests. It is an offence under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 to damage a nest in use. If birds are nesting, step back and wait.

Why tool disinfection matters for hedge disease

Most gardeners know box blight is devastating, but fewer know how easily it moves on a blade. The fungus responsible (now classified as Calonectria pseudonaviculata) produces sticky spores that adhere to metal surfaces and stay viable long enough to infect the next plant you touch. The same is true of Entomosporium mespili, the fungus behind photinia leaf spot, which has become a real problem in Sandwich, Deal and Ash gardens over the past several years.

Honey fungus (Armillaria species) is soil-borne rather than air-spread, but contaminated tools moving between root zones can carry infected material. Fireblight (Erwinia amylovora) affects hawthorn, cotoneaster and pyracantha hedges, and spreading sap from an infected plant is one documented route of entry.

The good news: standard disinfection, done consistently, breaks all of these transmission routes. You do not need specialist chemicals or expensive equipment.

Step 1: remove loose debris and sap immediately after cutting

While the tool is still outdoors and unplugged, scrape off visible clippings, sap and leaf material with a stiff brush or a piece of old cloth. Do not wait until you get back inside; sap dries and sticks, and dried material is harder to remove properly.

Pay particular attention to the gap between the two blades on a hedge trimmer. Clippings pack in there and carry the highest concentration of plant material, which is where most disease transfer happens.

Step 2: wash with warm soapy water

A washing-up bowl of hot water with a squeeze of washing-up liquid, and a stiff-bristled brush, will remove most of the organic residue that disinfectant cannot penetrate. Work the brush between the blade teeth from both faces. Rinse well to remove all the soap; soap residue can reduce the effectiveness of some disinfectants.

This step matters because disinfectants work on surfaces, not through a layer of sap or compacted debris. A blade that looks clean from a metre away may still have a film of residue in the gaps. Washing before disinfecting doubles the effectiveness of the next step.

Step 3: disinfect the blades

Three options all work well. Use whichever suits your kit:

  • Dilute household bleach: one part bleach to nine parts water. Spray or wipe both blade faces and between the teeth, leave for two to three minutes, then rinse. Cheap, widely available, effective against both fungal and bacterial pathogens.
  • Jeyes Fluid: dilute as per the label (typically 1:50 for garden equipment). Wipe or spray on, leave briefly, rinse. Traditional and well proven for garden hygiene.
  • Proprietary tool steriliser spray: products sold as garden disinfectant or tool steriliser (look for those containing quaternary ammonium compounds). Follow the label. Some are designed as no-rinse formulations.

Between plants when disease is confirmed, a quick wipe with methylated spirits is faster than mixing a solution and is adequate as a between-plant measure on the same day. It is not a substitute for a full wash-and-disinfect at the end of the session.

Step 4: dry the blades thoroughly

Pat or wipe dry with a clean cloth. Do not leave blades to air-dry while standing in a bucket or leaning against a wall; moisture sitting on metal promotes rust. If you are working through a large garden with multiple species, dry the blades between each disinfection and reapply fresh solution rather than letting the tool sit wet.

Step 5: apply a light coating of blade oil before storing

A thin film of camellia oil, or any purpose-made blade oil, protects against rust and keeps the cutting action smooth. Apply after drying, wipe off any excess. Do not use WD-40 as a long-term rust preventative (it is a water displacer, not a long-lasting lubricant), and avoid any oil that you would not want on plant material, as traces will transfer at the next use.

Properly cleaned and oiled hedge trimmer blades will last longer, run quieter and cut more cleanly. The disease prevention benefit is almost a bonus.

When to disinfect more frequently

The five steps above describe end-of-session practice. The following situations call for more frequent disinfection during a job:

  • You are cutting a hedge that has known or suspected box blight. Disinfect between every plant and every cut section, not just between hedges.
  • You are cutting a photinia red-robin hedge with visible orange-brown spotting on the leaves. Treat the tool as contaminated throughout.
  • You are moving between hedges in different properties. Disinfect before loading the van and before starting at the next job.
  • A hedge has recently been treated with a fungicide. Check the product label; some recommend that tools are not used on treated plants for a set period.
If you only cut hedges occasionally, keep a small spray bottle of dilute bleach solution and a cloth in the same bag as your tools. Two minutes at the end of every session is the whole routine.
