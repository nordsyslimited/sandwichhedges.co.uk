September hedge care in Kent: the safe cutting window opens

After six months of nesting-season caution, September is the month that everything changes. The legal restriction lifts on 1 September, the main hedge-cutting season resumes, and two or three species have deadlines that cannot be missed before the month is out.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

September checklist at a glance

  1. Beech and hornbeam: cut now, before leaves turn colour (mid-September deadline)
  2. Leylandii and other Leyland cypress: September is the ideal month
  3. Yew: cut now for a sharp winter profile
  4. Privet: second cut of the year if the June or July cut was the first
  5. Box: second cut if first was May or June
  6. Thuja, Lawson cypress: cut while still warm
  7. Holly: light tidy acceptable; heavier work better left to November
  8. Quick nest check: still worthwhile in early September for late-nesting species
  9. Begin planning bare-root orders for November planting
  10. Assess what needs professional attention this autumn

September is the month that hedge professionals look forward to all year. The nesting season closes officially on 31 August, the new growth on conifers has hardened after the summer flush, and the weather in East Kent is usually still warm enough to work comfortably. The rush starts immediately: beech and hornbeam will not wait.

We are out across Sandwich, Deal, Worth, Ash, Woodnesborough, Eastry and Sandwich Bay from the first week of September, and what follows is what actually matters this month.

Nesting season ends 31 August: a legal note

The UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. The main nesting season runs roughly 1 March to 31 August. From 1 September you can cut without legal restriction. However, some species (house sparrows, wood pigeons, collared doves) occasionally raise a late brood into September in mild years. A quick look along the hedge line before the first cut of the season is good practice, especially in the first two weeks of September. By mid-September, all UK birds have finished breeding for the year.

Before you start: safety

  • Eye protection: safety glasses or goggles for any power trimmer work. Flying stem fragments are a real hazard.
  • Ear defenders: petrol and electric trimmers both exceed safe noise levels quickly.
  • Gloves: sturdy cut-resistant gloves, especially for spiny species.
  • RCD adaptor: essential for corded electric trimmers.
  • Ladder caution: a long-reach pole trimmer is safer than a ladder for tall hedges. If a ladder is unavoidable, have someone footing it and never overreach sideways.
  • Yew: all parts are toxic. Wear gloves and wash hands after handling clippings. Keep pets away from yew trimmings.

Species-by-species: the September priorities

Beech and hornbeam: the September deadline

Beech (Fagus sylvatica) and hornbeam (Carpinus betulus) are the two hedging species with a genuine September deadline. Both are marcescent, meaning they hold their dead leaves through winter rather than dropping them cleanly in autumn. This is what gives a beech or hornbeam hedge its distinctive bronze winter appearance, and it is a quality gardeners value.

The catch: once the leaves begin to turn colour, usually from late September into October, any new cut tips will be left exposed rather than covered by growth. The result is a rough, uneven winter profile and a carpet of dead leaves shed across the lawn rather than retained on the hedge. Cut while the leaves are still green, by mid-September, and the hedge enters winter with a clean, defined shape that persists until April.

In East Kent, the colour change on beech typically begins in the second half of September, slightly later than further inland, because the coastal influence from Sandwich Bay and the broader Thames Estuary keeps temperatures a week or two warmer than the Midlands average. Mid-September is usually a safe target, but watch the hedge: if the leaves begin to yellow before that, cut immediately.

Leylandii and other conifers

Leylandii (× Cupressocyparis leylandii), Thuja plicata and Lawson cypress (Chamaecyparis lawsoniana) all benefit from a September cut. The new growth from the summer flush has hardened by now, the weather is typically still warm enough for cut surfaces to weather in before the first frosts, and you are well clear of the nesting season.

September is widely regarded as the best month for leylandii in particular. Cutting in October or later carries a risk: a hard cut on leylandii followed by October or November frosts can result in brown tips that persist through winter and beyond. September gives the hedge several weeks of mild weather to recover before the cold sets in.

See our guide to renovating an overgrown leylandii hedge if you are considering a larger reduction rather than a routine trim.

Yew

September is an excellent month for yew (Taxus baccata). One cut a year, ideally in late August or September, keeps a yew hedge sharp and allows the cut surface to harden before frost. Yew regenerates reliably from old wood, so it can be cut hard if needed, but most routine maintenance is a tidy of the current year's growth.

In historic East Kent gardens, yew hedges around Sandwich and Deal properties often date back decades. These older hedges benefit from a September cut to maintain their defined architectural profile. Fresh yew clippings are toxic: bag and bin them, or compost in a hot heap away from animals.

Privet

Privet (Ligustrum ovalifolium) is the most common hedge species in East Kent domestic gardens. It typically needs two cuts a year: a first cut in June or July, and a September tidy to keep it compact before the growing season ends. The September cut should be moderately firm, not a heavy renovation, to allow the hedge to harden before winter. A privet hedge left uncut through September will produce a large flush of soft growth that becomes straggly over winter.

Box

Box (Buxus sempervirens) benefits from two cuts a year: the first in May or June (after the main spring flush), and a second lighter tidy in September. The September cut tidies up any late-summer growth and leaves the hedge looking crisp through winter. Do not cut box after October: the new growth produced after a late cut is tender and prone to tip scorch in frost.

If you have been managing box blight (Cylindrocladium buxicola), September is a good time to assess the situation and plan for the winter. See our guide to box blight treatment for the full protocol.

Laurel, holly and other evergreens

Laurel (Prunus laurocerasus) and holly can be lightly trimmed in September, though holly is often better left until November when it can be tidied at the same time as yew. Laurel can be cut again in September if the first cut was in June, using secateurs or loppers on larger-leaved varieties rather than a hedge trimmer, to avoid unsightly brown cut edges on the large leaves.

Deciduous shrub hedges

Hawthorn, blackthorn, field maple, hazel and similar native deciduous species are best left until October or November. By then they will be fully dormant, the structure is clearer with the leaves off, and a renovation cut in the dormant season causes much less stress than one in September while the plant is still carrying foliage.

Planning ahead: September as the booking month

September is when the professional hedge-cutting season kicks off across East Kent. Good contractors book up quickly through September and October. If you have large hedges that need professional attention, now is the time to get a quote rather than waiting until October, when availability shrinks. We are based in Sandwich and work across Deal, Worth, Ash, Woodnesborough, Eastry and Sandwich Bay.

It is also the month to assess gaps, bare patches and sections that need replanting before the bare-root season opens in November. Walk the boundary now, note what needs ordering, and you will have exactly the right plants arriving in November ready to go in.
