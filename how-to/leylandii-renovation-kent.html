Renovating an overgrown leylandii hedge

The most common hedge renovation request we see in East Kent, and the one with the least margin for error. Leylandii renovation is different from every other hedging species because leylandii cannot regenerate from old brown wood. The approach depends entirely on how much green foliage remains.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Nesting season: do not cut between 1 March and 31 August

The UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 protects active bird nests from damage or destruction. The main nesting season runs 1 March to 31 August. Leylandii hedges are popular nesting sites for sparrows, robins, blackbirds and goldfinches, all of which are protected. Renovation work, particularly heavy work on the top and sides, must be completed before March or started after the end of August. Even outside this window, a quick visual check for active nests before any heavy cut is good practice, particularly in early September when late-nesting species (wood pigeon, house sparrow) may still have young.

Before you start: safety with tall hedges

  • Height: for any hedge over 2.5 m, this is contractor territory. Working from a ladder with a powered trimmer, particularly on a top cut, is a significant hazard. If you do work at height, use a proper scaffold plank between two stepladders, have someone footing, and never reach beyond your centre of balance.
  • PPE: safety glasses, ear defenders, gloves and non-slip footwear. Flying debris from leylandii is sharp.
  • RCD: essential for any corded electric trimmer.
  • Brash disposal: leylandii produces large volumes of clippings. Have a plan for disposal before you start. It cannot go in a standard green bin in most Kent councils without being bagged.
  • Neighbour notification: if the hedge sits on a boundary, talk to the neighbour before any significant reduction. Work affecting their side of the hedge needs agreement.

The rule that changes everything: green wood only

Before any other consideration, assess the hedge. Walk its full length and look into the base and sides. How far in does the green foliage extend? Is there green growth throughout the depth of the hedge, or only on the outer 10 to 20 cm?

That assessment determines your options:

  • Green foliage throughout: renovation is possible. You can reduce significantly, keeping cut surfaces within the green zone.
  • Green only on the outer layer, brown interior: you cannot reduce to the target size without hitting brown wood. Progressive reduction over several years is the only option, or removal and replanting.
  • Already brown in patches on the visible surface: that hedge cannot be recovered by cutting. The brown will not regrow. Options are to live with it, patch-plant, or remove and start again.

This is the distinction that separates leylandii from yew (Taxus baccata), which carries dormant buds in old brown wood and will regrow vigorously from a bare trunk if cut hard. It also differs from hornbeam and beech, which can be coppiced to the base and regenerate from the stump. Leylandii cannot do this. It is not a shortcoming to work around; it is a fixed characteristic of the species. Any guide that promises to "cut leylandii back to the trunk and get it green again" is mistaken.

Step 1: Assess the target height and what is achievable

Decide on the target height. This is usually driven by one of three things: a planning dispute, a light complaint from a neighbour, or simply a hedge that has outgrown its space. High hedges legislation in England (Part 8 of the Anti-social Behaviour Act 2003) can require height reductions if a local council issues a remedial notice, though the council will specify a minimum height of two metres.

Now measure from the top down. If the target height means cutting into brown wood, your options are progressive reduction or removal. If green growth extends all the way to the target height, full renovation in one season is possible.

Write the target height down and work to it with a string line or chalk marks on the stems, rather than estimating by eye. Leylandii renovations that go wrong almost always involve cutting unevenly because there was no fixed reference.

Step 2: Tackle the sides first, in September

September is the best month for leylandii work in East Kent. The new season's growth has hardened, the weather is typically warm enough for the cut surface to weather in before frost, and the nesting season is closed.

Start with the sides rather than the top. Working on the sides with a hedge trimmer or petrol saw, cut back to your target width on each face. If green foliage extends to this point throughout the height of the hedge, the full reduction can happen this visit. Keep the sides slightly battered (wider at the base than the top) to allow light into the lower growth.

Do not remove more than you need to in order to reach the target width. The cut face produces new growth from the remaining green shoots, and that new growth fills in over the following season. Cutting further back than necessary removes the growing surface and slows recovery.

Step 3: Address the top

Reducing the top of an overgrown leylandii is the most consequential step. The options are:

Option A: Full reduction to target height (green wood throughout)

If green foliage extends to the target height on all faces, cut the top to that height with a straight horizontal cut. Use a string line between two canes placed at target height, or mark the stem with a chalk line. Work from one end of the hedge to the other with a petrol hedge trimmer or chain saw for thicker stems, cutting to the line.

The resulting flat top will look stark for a season while new growth fills in. This is normal. Within two growing seasons, the top will have a thick cap of new green growth covering the cut stems.

Option B: Progressive reduction (brown wood below target height)

If the target height is below the level of green growth, reduce the top by 30 cm this season, keeping entirely within the green zone. Mark the new top with a cane and string line and cut to it.

In the following September, reduce by another 30 cm. Repeat each September until the target height is reached. This approach is slower but it keeps the hedge in good condition throughout the process and avoids the risk of a large area of bare brown wood that will not recover.

For a hedge that needs to come down from 5 m to 2 m, progressive reduction at 30 cm per year takes ten years. That is a long time, but the hedge stays green and usable throughout. The alternative, cutting to bare brown wood in one go, produces a hedge that cannot recover.

Option C: Removal and replanting

For a hedge that is already extensively brown, too wide to manage, or too tall for the progressive approach to be practical within a reasonable timeframe, removal and replanting is the right answer. This sounds drastic but it is often the most cost-effective option over a ten-year horizon.

Stump grinding after removal is important: leylandii stumps can be large and slow to rot, and they inhibit replanting if left in situ.

Step 4: Annual maintenance from here on

Once the hedge is at target size, the essential change is annual cutting to prevent it from growing away again. Leylandii grows 60 to 90 cm per year on good ground. Left uncut for even two or three years, it quickly becomes unmanageable again. The annual cut should trim back to just inside the previous season's cut line, which keeps the hedge at a consistent size and prevents the progressive build-up of brown interior wood.

September is the ideal month each year. One cut per year is usually sufficient, though a second light cut in April or May can neaten the appearance after the spring flush.

When to call a contractor

Any hedge over 2.5 metres tall. Any hedge where the top reduction involves working from height with a powered saw. Any hedge with a significant dispute dimension. These are situations where the risk of the wrong decision (cutting into permanent brown wood, an accident at height, or an action that provokes a formal complaint) outweighs the cost of professional advice.
