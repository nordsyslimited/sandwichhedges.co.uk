Training a living hedge arch or gateway

A living arch over a gate or path is one of those things that looks effortless once it is done and takes about five years to get there. Here is the honest version.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

A living arch is structurally simple: two hedges, trained to send their upper stems toward each other and eventually meet in the middle. The complexity is in the early training decisions. Get those right and you will have a feature that lasts a lifetime. Miss the first couple of formative cuts and the stems set at the wrong angle and are very hard to redirect later. This guide walks through the questions you need to answer before you start, then covers the method that follows from each answer.

Questions to answer before you start

Question 1: Which species do you have or plan to plant?

This is the most important decision. Not every hedge species trains into an arch.

Yew (Taxus baccata) is the classic choice. Slow to establish but holds a shape indefinitely once trained. Yew is pliable when young and dense enough to form a solid, wind-resistant arch. The coastal conditions around Sandwich and Deal suit yew well: mild winters, rarely hard frosts, and enough shelter in established gardens to allow steady growth.

Hornbeam (Carpinus betulus) is an excellent and faster alternative. Its stems are more flexible than yew when young, it reaches arch height in three to four years from planting, and it keeps its dried winter leaves in a way that gives the arch a warm brown colour through the colder months. Very well suited to East Kent.

Beech (Fagus sylvatica) trains similarly to hornbeam, also keeps winter leaves, and is widely planted in Kent gardens. The difference from hornbeam is subtle in the first few years; beech tends toward a slightly larger final leaf and can be marginally less tolerant of heavy clay soils.

Privet (Ligustrum ovalifolium) is the fastest option but requires cutting three to four times per year once the arch is formed to maintain a clean shape. The stems are flexible and train readily; the downside is the ongoing maintenance commitment.

Laurel and leylandii are not recommended for arches. Laurel stems become very rigid quickly and the large leaves do not close neatly at the join. Leylandii produces new growth only on the outer green fringe; cutting back to the structural stems that form an arch removes all the green and the arch cannot regenerate. If you have an existing leylandii hedge and want an arch over a gate, a timber frame with climbing plants is a better solution.

Question 2: How wide is the gateway?

Under 2 metres: a straightforward arch, with the two opposing stems meeting in the middle to form a smooth curve. The span is short enough that the stems do not need significant external support once they have lignified.

Over 2 metres: the span is wide enough that you need a temporary framework, typically two curved metal or timber hoops fixed to posts on either side, to guide the stems across the opening. Without a framework, wide spans sag or fail to close properly. The framework stays in place until the stems have thickened enough to hold the shape on their own, usually three to four years after the two sides join.

Question 3: Are you starting from new planting or training an existing hedge?

Starting from new planting is significantly easier. You plant the two rows of hedge and, from the first growing season, train one or two upright leader stems per plant on each side to lean toward the centre. At this stage the stems are thin and flexible; redirecting them takes only a cane and a loose tie.

Training an existing hedge is harder. You need to identify the youngest, most flexible upright stems within the top of the hedge, which may be difficult if the hedge has been cut to a fixed height for many years and has no long leaders. If the existing hedge has been clipped hard across the top repeatedly, it may not have leaders long enough to train. In that case, leave the top of the hedge untrimmed for one to two growing seasons to allow long leaders to push up before beginning to train.

Question 4: Rounded arch or flat-topped gateway?

A true rounded arch requires the stems to continue growing over the top in a curve. This is more demanding to train and usually requires a shaped framework. A flat-topped gateway, where the stems from both sides simply meet at the same height and are encouraged to interweave horizontally, is simpler and can be achieved without a framework on narrower openings. Both look good and both are achievable with the same species; it is a question of what fits the garden and how much maintenance time is available.

The basic training method

Plant the two hedge rows with a gap for the gateway. Leave one to two upright leader stems per plant at the arch position untrimmed whilst keeping the rest of the hedge clipped to its intended profile. Tie each leader loosely to a cane leaning toward the centre at roughly 10 to 15 degrees from vertical in year one. As the leader grows through the second and third years, gradually increase the lean by retying to progressively more angled canes. The goal is a gradual curve, not a sharp bend; stems that are bent too sharply too quickly can snap or stop growing at the bend point.

Trim the side shoots off the arch leaders each year to encourage the energy to go into length rather than lateral spread. Once the stems from both sides are within reach of each other (usually year three or four for hornbeam, year four or five for yew), tie them together at the meeting point and allow them to graft together naturally over the following season. Keep the join tied firmly until it has fused.

Maintenance once the arch is established

Trim the arch twice a year on the same schedule as the rest of the hedge. Keep the arch stems tied to their framework or to each other until they have clearly lignified and hold the shape without support, then remove the ties and framework. For a wide arch over 2 metres, leave the supporting hoops in place for a full season after the stems have fused; the extra time makes little visual difference but significantly reduces the chance of the arch splitting under wind load or the weight of the hedge in wet conditions.
