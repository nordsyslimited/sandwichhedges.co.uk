Pittosporum vs Griselinia: which evergreen suits your coastal East Kent garden?

Both are evergreen, both clip well, and both are sold as "good for the coast." Past that, they are quite different plants with different strengths. Here is the straightforward comparison, based on what actually happens to hedges around Sandwich Bay, Deal and the villages behind the shore.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

If you garden within half a mile of the sea in East Kent, you have probably been recommended both pittosporum and griselinia at different points. Nurseries reach for them as default answers to "what can I use for a coastal hedge?" and they are right to, up to a point. But the plants suit different situations, and picking the wrong one is a waste of money and three years' waiting.

This comparison covers the questions that actually matter for this part of Kent: hardiness in a cold snap, performance in salt-laden wind off the North Sea, growth rate on the thin soils inland from Sandwich Bay, and what annual trimming looks like in practice.

The short version

If your boundary is directly exposed to sea wind or within a street of the shore, choose griselinia. If you are in a sheltered town garden in Deal or Sandwich, or a walled garden that gets the worst of the wind taken off it, either will work, and pittosporum gives you more variety in leaf colour. If you are in a borderline spot, the safe choice is griselinia. You can always add pittosporum in a more sheltered corner.

Griselinia in detail

What makes it the go-to coastal hedge

Griselinia littoralis is native to New Zealand's coastal scrub, which means it evolved coping with exactly the kind of salt-laden wind that comes off the North Sea at Sandwich Bay. The thick, waxy leaves shed salt deposits rather than absorbing them, and the stems are flexible enough to move in strong wind without snapping. This is not just marketing: in the gardens directly behind the dunes at Sandwich Bay, griselinia is one of the very few broad-leaved evergreens that holds its foliage through a bad winter without browning significantly.

Growth and form

In a reasonable Kent soil with decent moisture, griselinia puts on 30-60 cm per year once it is established. On the dry, sandy or chalky soils closer to the coast, growth can be slower in the first two or three years until the roots have spread enough to find consistent moisture. One cut per year, in late August or September, keeps it at whatever height you want. The foliage is uniform apple-green with a slight shine: not the most exciting colour but reliably consistent across the whole hedge.

Hardiness

Griselinia is generally Hardy H4 on the RHS hardiness scale (tolerates -10°C in most references), but real-world performance in hard winters is variable. In East Kent, most winters pass without problem. In a prolonged cold snap with sustained temperatures below -8°C (unusual here, but not unheard of), sections of a large griselinia can be killed outright, leaving brown patches that do not recover and need cutting out and replacing. This is rare in the Sandwich/Deal area given the maritime climate, but worth knowing if you are planting a boundary you cannot afford to lose.

What to watch for

Griselinia on waterlogged soil in winter is more susceptible to root problems. On heavy clay, improve drainage before planting. Scale insects can occasionally affect plants in sheltered positions; treat with a horticultural oil spray in autumn if you see white encrustation on the stems. Aside from that, griselinia is a low-maintenance plant once established.

Pittosporum in detail

Why it appeals

Pittosporum tenuifolium and its cultivars are a different proposition: smaller-leaved, more varied in colour, and arguably more versatile within a sheltered garden. 'Silver Queen' has grey-green leaves with cream margins; 'Tom Thumb' is a slow-growing purple-leaved form that suits a low front boundary; 'Irene Paterson' has cream and green marbling that is genuinely ornamental. Against a white wall or beside a pale stone path in a Deal or Sandwich town garden, pittosporum has a visual interest that plain griselinia lacks.

Salt and wind tolerance

Pittosporum copes with moderate coastal exposure but is less comfortable in direct salt spray than griselinia. In a garden a street back from the shore, or sheltered by a building or taller existing hedge on the windward side, pittosporum performs well. Position it on the windward face of an exposed coastal boundary and it will struggle: scorched, desiccated foliage on the exposed side, leaving a thin hedge that never quite recovers each season.

Hardiness and frost risk

This is the main caution with pittosporum in East Kent. The species is rated H3-H4 depending on cultivar (hardy to around -5°C to -10°C in RHS terms), but in practice, some cultivars, particularly the variegated forms, are less cold-tolerant than plain green types. A genuinely cold winter can take out sections or entire plants. East Kent's maritime climate means this happens less often than in inland England, but the risk is real. If you have planted pittosporum and lost plants in previous cold spells, the answer is either to replace with griselinia or to site future pittosporum in the most sheltered microclimate available.

Trimming pittosporum

Pittosporum clips well: the small leaves mean cut edges look clean, and the plant responds to shaping without the browning that you get cutting into old wood on some conifers. August or September is the right time for the main annual cut, avoiding the late-spring flush of growth and staying well clear of spring nesting. An optional light tidy in May or June keeps a formal hedge neater through summer. Do not cut hard into woody stems much older than two or three years: pittosporum will sometimes regenerate from old wood but not always, and the results are unpredictable.

Nesting season applies to both species

Both pittosporum and griselinia are used as nesting sites by dunnocks, sparrows, blackbirds and other garden birds. The UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to damage an active nest. Nesting season runs roughly 1 March to 31 August. Both species should be trimmed between September and February for the main annual cut. If you are trimming in late August, check the hedge carefully before starting.

Which to choose: a practical guide for East Kent positions

Directly behind Sandwich Bay shingle, or on an exposed coastal face

Griselinia. No competition. Plant bare-root stock between November and February, stake firmly, mulch the base, and expect the first couple of years to be slow while it establishes against the wind. Do not trim in the first year; let it build roots and some canopy before you ask it to regenerate. Within three to four seasons, you will have a hedge that genuinely earns its place.

Town garden in Deal or Sandwich, sheltered by buildings

Either will work. If you want an all-green, consistent formal hedge, griselinia is slightly easier and slightly hardier. If you want leaf colour interest or a lower, more ornamental front boundary, try a pittosporum cultivar. 'Tenuifolium' in plain green is the toughest; coloured-leaved forms are a step more susceptible to hard frost.

A garden with both exposed and sheltered sections

Consider griselinia on the windward or most exposed section, and pittosporum for the sheltered end or a garden room inside the main boundary. Running them as a continuous hedge together tends to look patchy once they are established, because the leaf colours, textures and growth rates diverge. Separate sections work better.

Replacing frost-damaged sections

If a cold winter has killed sections of an existing pittosporum hedge, replacing like-for-like is tempting, but the same sections will be vulnerable again. Consider replanting with griselinia where the damage was worst, and filling any remaining gaps with pittosporum only in the more sheltered parts. This way you get a gradually more resilient boundary over time.

Growing them well: shared principles

  • Plant in spring or autumn, avoiding the hottest weeks. Pot-grown stock can go in at any time with sufficient watering; bare-root griselinia is available October to March and works well.
  • Spacing: 45-60 cm apart for a dense hedge by year three or four.
  • First-year watering: both will need watering in dry spells for the first summer. East Kent can be dry from June to August; do not rely on rainfall alone in year one.
  • Mulch: a 7 cm bark mulch ring around each plant in the first two years retains moisture, suppresses grass competition and speeds establishment noticeably.
  • Feeding: a balanced slow-release fertiliser in April each year, applied to established hedges, keeps growth steady without producing excessive soft growth.
  • Cuttings: both species root readily from semi-ripe cuttings in late summer if you want to propagate your own replacements or gap-fill material.
