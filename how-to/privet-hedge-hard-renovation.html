Hard renovation of a privet hedge: one cut or two years?

Privet is the most forgiving of all the common hedging species when it comes to hard cutting. The question is not really whether it will recover, but which approach suits your situation best.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

An overgrown privet hedge is one of the more common jobs we are asked about in East Kent. A privet that has not been cut for a few years grows fast, goes wide and eventually starts encroaching on the pavement or blocking light. Homeowners worry that cutting it back to old wood will kill it.

It almost certainly will not. Common privet (Ligustrum vulgare) and oval-leaved privet (Ligustrum ovalifolium) are both extraordinarily regenerative. The real decision is whether to do the renovation in one go, or spread it over two years. Here is how the two approaches compare.

Nesting season timing

Privet is used for nesting by blackbirds, sparrows and dunnocks in particular. Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, you must not damage an active nest. Nesting season runs roughly 1 March to 31 August. Any hard renovation work on privet should be completed before 1 March or started after 1 September. Always check for active nests first, even in early autumn.

The two approaches at a glance

Factor Single-season renovation Two-year staged approach
Time to complete One winter Two winters
Screening during recovery Lost for 12 to 18 months Partial screen retained throughout
Stress on the plant Higher (all at once) Lower (staggered)
Reliability of recovery Very good for healthy privet Excellent, even for older hedges
Best suited to Younger, healthy hedges; property sales; clearance projects Older hedges; where boundary screening matters; cautious approach

The single-season renovation

You cut all sides and the top in one winter. Both sides are cut back hard, typically to within 15 to 20 cm of the central stem line, and the top is cut to 30 to 45 cm below your eventual target height.

The hedge will look like a row of stumps. In March and April, new shoots will begin to emerge. By late summer of the first year, a healthy privet will have produced 30 to 60 cm of new growth and will start to look like a hedge again, albeit an open one. By the second summer it will be approaching its final form, and by the third summer you are essentially back to normal maintenance.

Choose this approach when the hedge is no more than ten or fifteen years old and is in reasonable health. If you need the work done quickly (a property sale, a boundary clearance or a renovation project), this is the straightforward option.

The two-year staged approach

In year one, you cut one side very hard: back to within 10 to 15 cm of the central line. Leave the other side and the top completely alone. The following winter, you cut the other side equally hard and drop the top to the target height.

This gives the hedge a working foliage system on one side throughout the renovation, which reduces stress and aids recovery. It also means you always have a partial screen: important if the hedge is your main privacy boundary between gardens in Sandwich or Deal, where plots can be close.

It is the better choice for older hedges (twenty years or more), hedges that have already shown signs of stress, and any hedge where you cannot afford to lose all screening for a full season.

The cutting technique

Whichever approach you choose, the cutting technique is the same:

  1. Sides first, top last. Establish the side profile before worrying about the height. This lets you check the width is consistent as you go.
  2. Cut from the bottom up. Starting at the base gives a cleaner taper and stops you cutting unevenly as you fatigue.
  3. Maintain a slight inward taper. The hedge should be a little narrower at the top than the base: 5 degrees is enough. This matters more during the recovery phase when base shoots need light.
  4. Use the right tool. A hedge trimmer handles stems up to 20 to 25 mm. Anything thicker needs loppers or a pruning saw. Do not force the trimmer through thick wood: it damages both the blades and the plant.
  5. Cut the top last. Once both sides are done, step back and check the height is level before making the top cut.

How far back is too far?

For privet, the honest answer is: you can cut to within 15 cm of the ground and it will almost certainly come back. We have done it repeatedly in Kent gardens with strong recovery in the first growing season.

The practical limit is the vigour of the root system. A privet hedge in good soil with adequate moisture will recover from almost any above-ground cut. A privet that has been badly waterlogged, is growing in compacted clay without any improvement, or is already showing signs of root rot, is a different matter.

If you are uncertain, cut to 30 cm rather than 15 cm in the first year. You can cut harder in year two if the hedge has responded well.

Aftercare during the recovery period

Privet does not demand heavy maintenance after renovation, but these steps make a real difference:

  • Mulch the base. A 5 to 8 cm layer of bark mulch or composted material helps retain soil moisture and reduces competition from weeds and grass at the base. Keep it away from the stems themselves.
  • Water during dry spells. East Kent gardens can turn dry by May or June. Newly renovated hedges are more vulnerable to drought than established ones. A thorough watering every ten to fourteen days during dry periods in the first summer is worthwhile.
  • Apply a light feed in spring. A slow-release general fertiliser in March or early April gives the new growth a boost. A single application is enough; do not overfeed.
  • Resist the urge to trim. In the first growing season after renovation, leave all new growth alone. The priority is leaf area, not neatness. Start shaping lightly in the second summer once the hedge has some bulk to work with.

Golden privet: any different?

Golden privet (Ligustrum ovalifolium Aureum) is the yellow-variegated form you see in front gardens throughout Sandwich and Deal. It tolerates hard renovation in the same way as common and oval-leaved privet, but its growth rate is noticeably slower. Expect recovery to take one additional season: it will look at its best in the third summer rather than the second.

Because of this, the two-year approach suits golden privet particularly well. The staged cut reduces the stress on a plant that is already less vigorous than its green-leaved relatives.

If in doubt, cut to 30 cm and assess after one growing season. You can always go harder in year two. You cannot put wood back.
