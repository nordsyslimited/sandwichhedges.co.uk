November hedge care in Kent: the pre-winter checklist

November is quieter than October but far from idle. It is the start of the dormant season, which means renovation cuts, bare-root planting and the last chance to tidy a few evergreens before deep winter closes things down.

Updated September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

November checklist at a glance

  1. Final yew and holly cuts (finish by mid-November)
  2. Begin bare-root planting (November to March)
  3. Hard renovation on deciduous hedges: beech, hornbeam, hawthorn, blackthorn
  4. Mulch the base of all hedges, especially new plantings
  5. Clear clippings from the hedge base to reduce disease carry-over
  6. Check and adjust ties on newly planted hedging
  7. Apply slow-release feed to established deciduous hedges (optional)
  8. Assess what needs professional attention over winter
  9. Order bare-root hedging now before popular varieties sell out

November gets overlooked as a hedge-care month. The main cutting rush is done, the garden is buttoning up, and people assume there is nothing left to do. There is, though, especially if you want to make the most of the dormant season, which is actually the best time for some of the most useful hedge work of the year.

We are out across Sandwich, Deal, Worth, Ash, Woodnesborough, Eastry and Sandwich Bay through November every year, and the jobs below reflect what actually matters this month in East Kent gardens.

Nesting season: fully over in November

The UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 protects active bird nests from disturbance or destruction. The main nesting season runs roughly 1 March to 31 August. By November, all resident bird species have finished breeding for the year. November to February is the clear, legal cutting window, and in fact the preferred season for hard renovation on most deciduous hedges. No nest checks needed: just proceed.

Before you start: safety

  • Eye protection: safety glasses or goggles for any power trimmer work.
  • Ear defenders: petrol and electric trimmers both exceed safe noise levels within a few minutes.
  • Gloves: sturdy gloves; cut-resistant for spiny species (holly, hawthorn, blackthorn).
  • RCD adaptor: essential for corded electric trimmers, no exceptions.
  • Ladder caution: a long-reach pole trimmer is safer than a ladder for tall hedges. If a ladder is unavoidable, have someone footing it and do not overreach sideways.

The detail: what to do and when in November

1. Final yew and holly cuts: do it early

Yew and holly are the two evergreens that can still be cut in November, but only in the first half of the month. The cut surface on yew needs two to three weeks to harden before a hard frost arrives. Do it by mid-November and the hedge will come through looking sharp. Do it in late November and risk browning on the exposed tips if a cold snap follows.

Holly is similarly forgiving but benefits from being cut by mid-November. The bonus: cutting holly in November means you can save the berry-laden trimmings for winter decoration. Female holly with berries is worth keeping in bundles in the shed for weeks.

2. Bare-root planting: the season opens

November is the start of bare-root hedging season and, for most species, the best time to plant. Bare-root plants are lifted from nurseries once fully dormant (late October to November) and dispatched without pots or soil. They are significantly cheaper than pot-grown plants and establish just as well, often better.

In East Kent, November planting works well. The soil is still warm enough for roots to make contact and begin establishing, and the mild coastal climate around Sandwich Bay and Deal means hard freezes in November are unusual rather than guaranteed. For inland positions around Woodnesborough and Eastry, which tend to get harder frosts earlier, plant by mid-November rather than waiting until December.

The best-value bare-root species (privet, hawthorn, beech, hornbeam, blackthorn, field maple, hazel) sell out at reputable suppliers by December. Order in late October and plant in November for the widest choice. See our guide to planting bare-root hedges for the full technique.

3. Hard renovation on deciduous hedges

Beech, hornbeam, hawthorn, blackthorn and field maple can all be reduced hard once fully dormant. November is the start of this window: the leaves have fallen, you can see the structure, and the plant is not under growing stress. A beech hedge that has grown three metres wide over twenty years can be cut back to 60 cm without killing it, if done correctly in the dormant season.

The usual approach is to cut one face hard in year one and the other face in year two, giving the hedge time to recover on each side. A hard renovation that strips both sides and the top in a single season is possible on vigorous species like hawthorn and blackthorn, but works better done gradually on slower-growing species like beech.

November is also the ideal time for hedge-laying, the traditional craft of weaving near-horizontal stems to thicken the base of a hedge. If you have a laid hedge on your boundary or are considering commissioning one, now through February is the season.

4. Mulching the base

A 7 cm layer of bark chip or well-rotted garden compost along the base of the hedge, kept 5 cm clear of the stems, does three things: it insulates the root zone from frost, retains moisture through dry winter spells, and gradually improves the soil as it breaks down. In the free-draining sandy and chalk soils common around Sandwich, Ash and Eastry, a good November mulch makes a visible difference to hedge health the following spring. Skip it only on heavy clay that stays waterlogged through winter.

5. Clear clippings from the base

Clippings left at the base of the hedge over winter create moist, sheltered conditions ideal for fungal disease. Box blight in particular overwinters in dead plant material at the hedge base. Clear all clippings now: compost most species, but hot-compost box clippings or dispose of them in the bin rather than a garden heap.

6. Check ties on newly planted hedging

November winds are the first serious test for newly planted hedging. Any stakes or ties put in at planting time need checking: stakes should be firm, ties should not be cutting into the stems, and plants should not be rocking at the root. A plant rocking in the wind at the root cannot establish properly; firm it back in and resecure the tie if needed.

7. Assess what needs professional attention

November is the planning month for winter hedge work. Walk the boundaries and note what needs doing: large reductions that require a tree surgeon or a long-reach machine, sections that need replanting, or major renovations you would rather a contractor handle. January and February are the best months for professional renovation work on most deciduous hedges, and contractors have more availability then than in the September-October rush. Book now rather than in December.

The BBC Gardeners' World Magazine YouTube channel published a comprehensive guide to winter pruning and the dormant season in February 2025: Late Winter Pruning: The Ultimate Guide You Can't Miss! covers what to tackle during dormancy across the whole garden and is worth watching before planning your November and winter hedge work.
