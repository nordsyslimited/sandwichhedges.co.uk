Weedkiller drift damage to hedges: an 8-step recovery checklist

Accidental weedkiller drift is one of the more avoidable causes of hedge damage, yet it happens every summer. Whether your own spray has drifted or a neighbour's has, knowing what to do in the first few hours makes the difference between a hedge that recovers and one that does not.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Recovery checklist

Work through these steps as soon as you suspect weedkiller drift. The earlier you act, the better the outcome.

  1. Stop any further spraying near the hedge until you know what happened and have changed your method.
  2. Water the root zone thoroughly to dilute any glyphosate that has reached the soil before the roots take it up.
  3. Photograph the damage with the date, especially if a neighbour's spray may be responsible.
  4. Wait at least two to four weeks before removing any material. Some plants look dead and are not.
  5. Scratch-test woody stems to confirm what is alive: green or cream tissue under the bark means alive; dry brown means dead.
  6. Remove confirmed dead material cleanly, cutting back to healthy tissue.
  7. Feed with a balanced slow-release fertiliser once plants are stable and producing new growth, to support recovery.
  8. Decide on replanting where sections are lost, using the same species or a suitable replacement.

How weedkiller drift happens

Drift occurs when spray particles carried on wind, or vapour from evaporating product, land on plants that were not the intended target. A garden sprayer with a fan nozzle on a light breeze is enough to carry glyphosate across a metre or more. Warm dry days increase the risk because the spray atomises into finer droplets that stay airborne longer. The most common cause in an East Kent context is glyphosate applied to paths, driveways or hard standing near a hedge boundary, sometimes by a neighbour rather than the hedge owner.

Three types of weedkiller, three types of damage

Glyphosate (systemic): absorbed through leaves and carried through the plant's vascular system. Damage appears as yellowing from leaf tips inward, progressing to full wilting and die-back over one to three weeks. Because it moves systemically, glyphosate reaching a single branch can eventually kill the whole section. Ground-level contamination is particularly damaging as roots absorb it directly.

Hormone weedkillers (2,4-D and similar): not systemic in the same way, but they disrupt plant hormones and cause new growth to emerge twisted, distorted and fern-like. Affected growth is distinctive and unmistakeable once you know what it looks like. Existing leaves may look fairly normal while new shoots come out grossly misshapen.

Contact weedkillers (diquat and similar): burn whatever they touch but do not move through the plant. Damage looks like sudden severe scorch, usually on one face of the hedge. The plant often recovers from the undamaged interior once the scorched surface is removed.

Working through the checklist in detail

Watering (step 2) matters most for glyphosate, and only works if you do it quickly, before significant root uptake. For a hedge with established roots, thorough soaking means a slow trickle at the base for thirty to sixty minutes, not a quick hose-down.

The waiting period (step 4) is the hardest part. A leylandii section that has turned completely brown can still be alive if the cambium layer (just under the bark) is undamaged. Four weeks is usually long enough to see new growth emerging from living stems, or to confirm that nothing is coming back.

Feeding (step 7) should not happen until plants are clearly recovering and producing new growth. Feeding a stressed plant before it is stable tends to push soft growth that is more vulnerable to setbacks. Wait until you can see green shoots coming from treated sections.

If a neighbour's weedkiller caused the damage

Under common law, accidental damage caused by negligent use of herbicide can form the basis of a compensation claim if the loss is significant. In practice, a polite conversation showing your photographs is often enough to get an agreement that they will use a gel or ready-to-use trigger spray near your boundary in future, and perhaps contribute to replacement plants. For more serious damage to a long established hedge, a solicitor's letter setting out costs may be needed. For context on hedge ownership and boundary issues, see our guide to hedge heights and neighbour law.

Preventing drift in future

A temporary physical barrier between the nozzle and the hedge is the simplest protection: hold a sheet of cardboard between the spray and the hedge, or use a plastic shield designed for the purpose. Low-drift nozzles reduce fine-particle spray. Weed-control gel products apply product exactly where you want it with no drift at all. Never spray on days with any perceptible breeze, and avoid temperatures above 25°C when evaporation creates fine vapour that drifts further than visible droplets.

Garden Ninja, a UK-based garden channel (over 78,000 subscribers), has useful practical videos on safe weedkiller use around garden boundaries if you want to see the barrier-spray technique demonstrated.
