Yew vs leylandii: which tall hedge suits Kent gardens?

Both are used throughout East Kent. Leylandii for speed, yew for permanence. The right choice depends on how fast you need privacy, how much maintenance time you have, and how long you plan to stay.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

When a customer wants a tall formal screen, the choice usually comes down to these two. Leylandii is everywhere along the coast from Sandwich Bay to Deal and beyond, mostly because it provides a usable windbreak in two or three years. Yew takes a decade to do the same job, but the hedge you end up with is a different thing entirely.

Neither plant is wrong. The decision comes down to priorities: timeline, maintenance budget, wildlife value, and what you want the garden to look like in thirty years. This guide lays out the facts side by side so you can decide.

Nesting season applies to both

Trimming either plant must respect the nesting season. The UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting runs roughly 1 March to 31 August. The safe cutting window is September to February, with light summer trims permissible only if the hedge is clearly empty of nests.

Leylandii: fast privacy, high maintenance

Leylandii's appeal is simple: it grows faster than anything else you can plant as a formal screen. A row of leylandii planted at 1.2 metres will give you a 2.5 metre screen in two to three years in most East Kent soils. Along the coast from Sandwich Bay to Deal, where salt wind comes in hard off the sea, leylandii is almost universal in new-build gardens. Nothing gives you shelter that quickly.

The other side of that speed is the maintenance. Leylandii needs cutting two to three times a year once it reaches its intended height. Skip a summer and you are dealing with an extra 75 to 90 centimetres on all sides. Let it go for two or three years and you may find the hedge has grown so far beyond your ability to cut it that you are faced with removal rather than renovation.

This connects to the legal question. Leylandii is the primary target of the Anti-Social Behaviour Act 2003 high hedges provisions. Any evergreen or semi-evergreen hedge over 2 metres that a council determines adversely affects a neighbour's reasonable enjoyment of their property can be the subject of a remedial notice requiring you to reduce and maintain it. The speed of leylandii is what puts most complaints into that territory. See our leylandii height and neighbour law guide for the full picture.

The other significant limitation is renovation. If a leylandii screen has grown too wide, you cannot cut back to bare brown wood. There is no recovery from that. With yew, you can cut a hedge back to the trunk and watch new growth emerge. With leylandii, what you cut is what you keep. This is a real constraint over a thirty or forty year garden.

Yew: slower, but worth the wait

A mature yew hedge is one of the most satisfying things in a formal garden. The growth is dense and very dark green. The texture holds a shape precisely. And unlike leylandii, a yew hedge can be renovated hard and will come back. If you let a yew hedge get away from you, you can cut it back to a metre from the ground and wait for it to regrow. That is simply not an option with leylandii.

The maintenance burden is lower too. An established yew hedge needs cutting once or twice a year. Late August to early September is the main cut in East Kent. Done. You do not have the spring-and-summer anxiety of a leylandii screen that wants to add another 80 centimetres before you can get to it.

The poisonous berries deserve a mention. All parts of yew except the berry flesh contain taxine alkaloids, which are toxic to humans, horses, cattle and dogs. This matters if you have livestock or very young children. In most domestic East Kent gardens it is a practical non-issue: the hedge is trimmed once or twice a year, clippings are bagged and removed, and the berries appear only on female plants. But if you are putting in a hedge adjoining a paddock, yew is not the right choice.

In Eastry and Woodnesborough we see many mature yew hedges maintained over decades. The darkness, the precision and the permanence of a well-kept yew hedge is something leylandii cannot replicate. You will not see a leylandii hedge in the grounds of Canterbury Cathedral. There is a reason for that.

The wildlife value is also significantly higher. The berries (bright red in autumn) are eaten by thrushes, blackbirds, robins and fieldfares. The dense cover provides excellent nesting habitat. For a garden where wildlife matters, yew is the better plant by some distance.

The real question: how long can you wait?

If you need a screening hedge in two or three years, leylandii is the answer. Nothing else gets close in that timeframe. Plant at 1.2 metres, manage the formative cuts carefully in years one to three (see our leylandii first three years guide), and you will have a usable screen faster than any alternative.

If you are planting for the next twenty years and beyond, yew is worth the slower start. The hedge will outlast the leylandii by centuries if properly maintained, and it will look better doing it. The lower maintenance burden, the ability to renovate, the wildlife value, and the sheer permanence of mature yew make it the more considered choice for any long-term garden plan.

Some gardens use both: a leylandii screen planted as a temporary wind break while a parallel yew hedge establishes behind it. The leylandii is then removed once the yew is large enough to do the job. It is a reasonable approach if cost is not a barrier.
