How to trim a conifer hedge: technique, timing and the green rule

One wrong cut into brown wood and a conifer hedge never fully recovers. Here is the decision guide: which species you have, when to cut, how deep to go, and what to do with a hedge that is already too tall.

Updated September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Conifer hedges are everywhere in East Kent: leylandii running along back fences in Sandwich and Deal, Lawson cypress around older properties in Worth and Ash, thuja in newer gardens, and yew as formal boundary hedging around historic properties from Eastry to Woodnesborough. They all look similar when they are well trimmed. They all fail in different ways when the trimming goes wrong.

The guide below works as a decision tree: start with your species, follow the timing, then choose your technique. Get the sequence right and the hedge stays green. Get it wrong and you are looking at a brown patch that will still be there in five years.

Nesting season: check before you cut

Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, disturbing an active bird nest is an offence. Nesting season runs from 1 March to 31 August. September is the opening of the safe window for most conifer hedges, which is one reason a late August or early September trim is the recommended time. Always look into the hedge before starting work: if you find an active nest, leave that section and return once the nest has been vacated.

Safety before you start

  • Eye protection: conifer cuttings are sharp, light and travel fast when a hedge trimmer blade sends them back at you.
  • Ear defenders: petrol hedge trimmers run loud; even cordless ones sustained over an hour cause cumulative hearing damage.
  • Gloves and long sleeves: conifer sap irritates skin and thuja pollen can trigger a reaction in sensitive people.
  • Ladder safety: for hedges over head height, use a proper platform ladder or a hop-up, not a standard stepladder propped against the hedge. Never overreach sideways.
  • RCD and cable: if using a corded electric trimmer, use an RCD adaptor and keep the cable behind you and away from the blade at all times.
  • Wasp nests: conifers are a favoured site. If you disturb one, stop immediately and move away from the area.

Step 1: identify your conifer species

This matters more than anything else, because one species (yew) tolerates hard cuts into old wood, and every other common conifer hedge does not.

Cupressus × leylandii Leylandii No 2–3
Chamaecyparis lawsoniana Lawson cypress No 1–2
Thuja plicata Western red cedar No (limited) 1–2
Taxus baccata Yew Yes 1–2
× Cuprocyparis leylandii 'Castlewellan' Golden leylandii No 2–3

Not sure what you have? The fastest check: pick a small section of foliage and crush it in your hand. Thuja smells strongly of pineapple or fruit. Lawson cypress has a slightly resinous smell. Leylandii has almost no smell. Yew has flat, dark green needles arranged in two rows on the stem, and red berries in autumn.

Step 2: choose the right time of year

The timing calendar for conifer hedges in East Kent:

  • Late May to mid-June: the first trim of the year. By now the main flush of spring growth has extended but not yet hardened off. Cutting at this point keeps the hedge in check without removing too much material.
  • Late August to mid-September: the most important trim of the year. The summer growth is removed before it hardens into thicker stems. September is also the opening of the safe nesting window, which makes it the right moment for a more thorough cut.
  • Avoid November to February for most conifers. Freshly cut surfaces are more vulnerable to frost damage. January and February are fine for yew, which is more frost-hardy at the cut surface.
  • Never trim in very hot, dry conditions: the cut foliage tips scorch and turn brown more easily. An overcast day is better than full summer sun.

For the chalk and flint soils of Sandwich, Deal and the coastal strip, the growing season tends to run slightly hotter and drier than inland at Worth and Ash. Conifer hedges in these coastal areas sometimes make slightly less growth per season, which means a single trim per year may be enough to keep them tidy.

Step 3: stay in the green

This is the rule that everything else depends on. When you are cutting a conifer hedge, you should always be cutting into green, living foliage. The moment you are cutting into brown, dead needles and bare wood, you have gone too deep.

The practical test: pull a branch towards you and look at the interior. Can you see green growth on the inner part of that branch? If yes, you still have a margin of safety. If the inner part is bare and brown, you are at the limit. Do not go further.

For a hedge that is already at the right size, this means taking off only the new season's growth each time. For a hedge that has grown beyond the desired size, you can make one corrective cut per season per side, taking slightly more than just the new growth but never reaching into brown. Over two or three seasons, this gradually brings the hedge back.

Step 4: choose the right tool

For most conifer hedges, a hedge trimmer is the right choice. The points below help you pick the right type:

  • Cordless electric: the right tool for most garden-scale jobs. Quieter, no cable risk, and modern battery platforms give enough run time for typical hedges. A 60 cm or longer bar is more efficient on a conifer hedge than a short bar because it takes wider sweeping passes.
  • Petrol: the right tool for hedges over 20 metres or where multiple charges would slow you down. More powerful through thicker stems. Heavier, so more fatiguing on a tall hedge.
  • Corded electric: fine for shorter hedges where there is a nearby socket. The cable is the limiting factor: always position it behind you.
  • Secateurs or loppers: useful for individual thick stems inside a hedge and for tidying after trimming, but not the main tool for a length of conifer hedge.
  • Long-reach hedge trimmer or pole trimmer: useful for the top of a hedge over 2 metres, reducing the need to work from a ladder.

Step 5: trimming technique

Work in long, smooth sweeping arcs rather than short, jabbing strokes. A smooth sweep moves more material away cleanly and gives a flatter finish. Short strokes leave a corrugated surface that catches the eye.

For the sides of the hedge, work upwards from the base so that cut material falls clear of the section you have not yet trimmed. For the top, work along the length of the hedge, keeping the trimmer bar parallel with the surface and level. Use a taut string line or laser level if you need a precise horizontal line.

A slight inward taper on the sides (the hedge is narrower at the top than at the base) helps light reach the lower foliage and keeps the bottom of the hedge green and healthy. A vertical-sided conifer hedge tends to thin out at the base over time.

Clear cuttings from the base of the hedge as you go. Conifer cuttings left on the soil create a thick mat that can hold moisture against the stems and encourage fungal problems.

Step 6: what to do with a hedge that is already too tall

A conifer hedge that is already significantly taller than you want is one of the harder problems in garden maintenance. The options:

If it is leylandii, Lawson cypress or thuja and already well above the desired height: there is no safe way to take off a large amount and keep a green top. You can take some off the top each season, as long as green growth is still present at the tip. But if the top metre or more is already brown and dead inside, cutting there leaves a brown stub. The realistic choices are either to leave it taller than you wanted, or to remove it and replant with a slower-growing species at the desired height.

If it is yew: yew can be cut back hard, even into old wood, and will regenerate. Take the height reduction in stages over two or three seasons rather than all at once, and feed and water the hedge well during the recovery period. Yew is slower to grow but very reliable once established: the formal yew hedges around historic properties in Eastry and Woodnesborough that have been maintained for decades are evidence of that.

If it is a neighbour dispute situation involving leylandii: the High Hedges Act 2003 provides a mechanism for councils to require a hedge owner to reduce the height of a high hedge (defined as over 2 metres) that is adversely affecting a neighbour's reasonable enjoyment of their property.
