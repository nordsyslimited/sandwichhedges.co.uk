Maintaining established topiary shapes: a year-round care guide

Once the shapes are trained, keeping them is mostly a matter of knowing which plant gets cut when, in which direction, and with which tools. The calendar does the heavy lifting. The cuts themselves are relatively quick.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

This guide is about maintenance, not training. It assumes the shapes are already established: the box balls, yew columns, hornbeam cubes, or clipped privet forms are in place and the job is keeping them looking right through each season without letting them drift or bulk up beyond their intended size.

The Royal Horticultural Society has covered topiary technique in depth, including tool maintenance, species selection and clipping methods at RHS Garden Wisley: The Art of Topiary (RHS, YouTube). It is a good companion to the calendar below.

The year in brief: cutting windows by species

Before the month-by-month detail, a summary of when each species gets cut:

  • Box (Buxus sempervirens): twice a year, May and August–September
  • Yew (Taxus baccata): once a year, August or September
  • Hornbeam (Carpinus betulus): once a year, late July or August
  • Holly (Ilex aquifolium): once a year, August (or late July with a nest check)
  • Privet (Ligustrum ovalifolium): two to three times, April–May, June–July (optional), and August

Nesting season: the constraint that shapes the whole calendar

Topiary plants, particularly dense evergreens, are attractive nesting sites. Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, disturbing or damaging an active nest is an offence. The nesting season runs from roughly 1 March to 31 August. Any trimming during this period requires a thorough visual check of the whole plant before you start. The September to February window is when most topiary work is safest, which conveniently aligns with the timing most species benefit from anyway.

January and February: preparation, not cutting

There is nothing to cut in January or February. Evergreen topiary is dormant or close to it, and cutting in cold weather risks browning on the cut faces. Use these months to:

  • Sharpen hand shears and replace any blades that have become too worn to take an edge
  • Oil pivot points and moving parts on electric or battery trimmers
  • Check guide canes and string lines are still in place and properly aligned
  • Look for frost damage on box and yew: brown or bronze patches on exposed faces are worth noting but usually recover in spring without intervention

Box blight (Cylindrocladium) is easier to spot in winter when the plant is not growing. Brown patches with dark stems warrant a closer look; if it is blight rather than frost damage, remove and bin (not compost) the affected material when dry weather returns.

March and April: patience for most, first cut for privet

March brings the start of the nesting season and the first real growth push, but most topiary species do not need cutting until May at the earliest. Resist the urge to tidy up early. The exception is privet, which starts growing fast in March and by late April can already look untidy around a clipped shape.

For privet shapes, a light first cut in late April is reasonable if you check for nests first. Inspect the whole plant before you start and look for active cups or eggs. If anything is present, leave it and wait. Most privet nesting is done by June so delaying a few weeks rarely causes problems.

April is also a good time to apply a slow-release balanced fertiliser around the base of box and yew if the plants look pale or have thinned over winter. Do not overfeed: yew and box in particular grow more cleanly without excessive feeding.

May: box first cut, privet main cut

The first cut of the year for box falls in May. By this point the new growth from spring is well underway, the fresh shoots are still soft and easy to cut cleanly, and clipping now encourages a second flush that fills in the surface by summer. Box cuts cleanly in May and the wounds callus quickly in warm spring conditions.

For box balls and simple geometric shapes, hand shears give the most control. Work from one side to the other in a single sweeping motion where the scale allows, using a curved motion to follow the surface of a sphere. For flat planes and cube shapes, a taut string line or a long straight cane held against the face helps keep the lines true.

Privet in May can take a more substantial cut if the April tidy was light. This is the main spring pruning for privet topiary: take it back to the intended shape and remove any shoots that are trying to break the outline.

PPE reminder: wear close-fitting eye protection when cutting topiary with electric or battery trimmers. Box clippings in particular are flung at speed and can cause eye irritation. Gloves protect against the stems of box and yew, which can cause skin reactions in some people.

June and July: mostly leave it alone

June and July are the peak of the nesting season and also the period of fastest growth. The temptation is to cut back the lush new growth that is softening your shapes, but the risk of disturbing active nests is highest now and most cuts in June result in another flush of growth almost immediately, so the effort is not always worthwhile.

If privet has grown substantially and is clearly losing its shape, a light tidy in late June or early July is possible but requires a careful nest check. Do not cut back to bare wood and do not cut more than necessary. For box, yew and holly, the standard advice is to leave June and July alone entirely.

The main exception is hornbeam. Late July is within the acceptable cutting window for hornbeam (nesting is largely complete by late July in most years), and many gardeners prefer to do the annual hornbeam cut at the end of July rather than August. If you choose late July, still check for active nests first.

August and September: the main maintenance window

August is when most topiary work gets done, and rightly so. The nesting season ends on 31 August, which means August cuts need a nest check, but September cuts do not. Both months are fine; the choice often comes down to convenience and weather.

August priorities:

  • Box second cut: this is the most important cut of the year for box topiary. It defines the shape going into winter and the result stays visible for six months. Take time with it
  • Yew annual cut: August is the classic timing for yew. The cut hardens before winter and any bronzing on cut faces is temporary, gone by the following spring
  • Holly annual cut: late August is the safest time for holly if you want to leave berries on the plant through winter, as cutting before this removes the developing fruit
  • Hornbeam if not done in late July

September is often a better option for yew specifically: the growth has fully stopped, the cuts are clean, and there is no need to check for nests. Many professional topiarists prefer September for their yew work for exactly this reason. It is also the best time to address any shapes that have drifted over the summer and need more attention than a routine clip.

October: last chance before winter

Any topiary that was missed in August or September can still be cut in October. Growth has largely stopped by mid-October, so an October cut will hold through to spring. Avoid cutting after the first hard frosts, which in East Kent typically arrive in November, though in sheltered coastal gardens in Sandwich and Deal, frost may not come until December.

October is also useful for a final check of shapes before winter: make sure guide canes are still in place for anything that needs them, and collect the clippings (box clippings in particular should be collected rather than left to rot under the plant, as they can harbour fungal spores).

Keeping edges crisp over time

Shapes drift for two main reasons: the cuts are not taken back to the same line each time, or the guide canes have been removed or shifted. The remedy is simple: always cut to a physical reference, not by eye alone. A straight vertical face needs a cane or spirit level. A flat top needs a taut string. Spheres are harder to guide mechanically, but working from the same viewpoint at the same distance each time helps maintain consistency.

When a shape has drifted beyond what a single corrective cut can fix, restore it over two seasons rather than one. Taking too much off in a single cut removes the growing points that the plant needs to recover. Box and yew both respond well to gradual correction; hornbeam is reliable for hedged shapes. On all three, cut back to where there is still live green foliage rather than into completely bare wood.
