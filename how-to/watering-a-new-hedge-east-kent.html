How to water a new hedge in East Kent

Most new hedges that die, die of thirst in their first or second summer. A few good soakings do more than a little water every day.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

The first two summers decide whether a new hedge thrives or struggles. Roots go in at planting but take 12 to 24 months to spread beyond the planting hole. Until they do, the plant relies on you. East Kent is one of the drier parts of the country, and the free-draining soils around Sandwich Bay, Worth and the chalk above Deal let water run through quickly.

Checklist: watering a new hedge

  • Soak at planting time, even in winter, so soil settles round the roots.
  • Mulch straight after planting to hold moisture in.
  • From spring, check the soil 10 cm down every week in dry weather.
  • Water slowly and thoroughly when it is dry at that depth.
  • Water in the early morning or evening.
  • Keep doing it through the second summer, tapering as the plants establish.

1. Soak at planting

Whether you plant bare-root in winter or pot-grown plants in spring or autumn, give each plant a full watering once the soil is back round it. This removes air pockets and brings soil into contact with the roots. Pot-grown plants should be well watered in their pots before they go in the ground. For the planting method, see planting bare-root hedges and trench planting a formal hedge.

2. Mulch to hold the water in

A layer of mulch over bare soil cuts evaporation and suppresses weeds that would compete for water. Keep it off the stems. Our mulching guide covers materials and depth.

3. Check, do not guess

The surface can look dry while the roots are still fine, and a damp-looking surface can sit on bone-dry soil. Push a trowel in beside a plant, or dig down about 10 cm. If it is damp and crumbly, leave it a few more days. If it is dry and dusty, water.

4. Water deeply, not often

Frequent light watering keeps roots near the surface, where they dry out first. A slow, heavy soak sends water deeper and encourages roots to follow it. Let the water soak in rather than run off, which is easy to do with a watering can poured around the base of each plant, or a soaker hose laid along the row.

5. Adjust to your soil

Soil What to expect Guide
Sandy and shingle Drains very fast. Needs watering soonest and most often. Mulch matters a great deal. Sandy soil planting
Chalk Often thin and free-draining over the chalk, so it dries quickly in summer. Chalk soil planting
Clay Holds water, so overwatering is the bigger risk. Water less often, but check it cracks in dry spells. Clay soil planting

6. Keep going into year two

A hedge that came through its first summer is not yet self-sufficient. In the second summer, keep checking in dry spells, especially on exposed sites where wind dries the ground as well as the leaves. By the third summer most hedges can cope with normal weather, though a long drought will still stress them. See hedge care in summer drought.

Signs a new hedge is short of water

  • Leaves wilting, drooping or curling in the afternoon and not recovering by evening.
  • Evergreen foliage dulling or browning at the tips.
  • Deciduous leaves dropping early in summer.
  • Small plants failing to make any new shoots by midsummer.

If you also see yellowing with waterlogged soil, you may be overwatering; check drainage before adding more.
