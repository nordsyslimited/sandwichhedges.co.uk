Cherry plum hedge care: pruning and managing Prunus cerasifera

Purple versus green, formal versus informal, annual trim versus renovation: what actually matters for a cherry plum hedge in Kent.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Cherry plum (Prunus cerasifera) is one of the most common hedging plants in East Kent suburban streets, and one that rarely gets much dedicated advice. It gets lumped in with "all the Prunus types" or described vaguely as "trim after flowering." Both things are true as far as they go, but they leave out enough that many people end up cutting at the wrong time or handling the purple and green varieties as if they were the same job.

This guide covers the main decisions: purple versus green varieties, how to trim a formal hedge versus managing a loose screen, annual maintenance versus renovation, and when you really do need a professional. Where two approaches are genuinely both fine, it says so.

Before you start: safety and nesting season

Nesting season: 1 March to 31 August

Cherry plum flowers from late February and is in full leaf by mid-spring, which makes it attractive nesting cover from March onwards. Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is a criminal offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Check the hedge thoroughly before cutting: look into the interior, work around the outside slowly, look for movement or alarm calls. If you find an active nest with eggs or young, postpone cutting until the young have fledged. Mid to late summer trimming largely sidesteps this: most first broods are fledged by late July, though a second brood may still be present in August.

Before cutting

  • Wear eye protection and gloves: cherry plum is not thorny but cut stems and debris will find your face.
  • Sharp tools matter more here than with most hedges. Prunus species seal pruning wounds with callus tissue, and a clean cut heals faster than a ragged one.
  • Avoid cutting in wet conditions if you can. Silver leaf disease, to which all Prunus are susceptible, spreads through pruning wounds and the spores are most active in wet autumn and winter weather.
  • If using a petrol trimmer, wear ear defenders and a face visor. Thick cherry plum stems can kick back or deflect.

Purple versus green: does it matter for pruning?

In terms of care, less than many people think. But there are real differences in how they behave and what they suit.

Purple-leaved forms
'Nigra', 'Pissardii', 'Thundercloud'

  • Deep purple to near-black foliage all season.
  • Pale pink or white flowers in late February to March against bare stems, before leaves open.
  • Slower growing than the wild form, to about 3-4m if left. Easier to keep at 1.5-2.5m.
  • Most commonly seen as a formal garden or street hedge in Sandwich, Deal and Ash.
  • Responds well to shearing and regular trimming. The dense leaf colour holds interest through summer and autumn.
  • Leaves are smaller and the canopy slightly denser than the wild type.

Green-leaved wild type
Species form, also called myrobalan

  • Pale to mid-green leaves, more open habit than the purple forms.
  • White flowers February to March, followed by small red or yellow cherry-plum fruits.
  • Faster growing, can reach 4-5m+ if left completely unmanaged.
  • More often seen as a countryside boundary hedge or windbreak in East Kent farm boundaries.
  • Tolerates a wider range of soil conditions including heavier clay around Eastry and Woodnesborough.
  • Better for wildlife: more fruit, slightly looser structure suited to nesting birds.

The key difference in practice: the wild green form grows faster and needs more frequent attention to keep within bounds. The purple cultivars are somewhat more self-limiting and better if you want a predictable formal appearance.

Formal hedge versus informal screen

This choice affects almost everything that follows.

Formal trimmed hedge

Cherry plum, particularly the purple forms, takes formal trimming well. A hedge trimmer handles the leaf mass easily, the plant regenerates from cut wood quickly, and it can be maintained at any height from about 1m upwards. The density of the purple-leaved forms looks good as a formal boundary in narrower garden settings in Sandwich town or the houses around Deal seafront where space is limited and a crisp line matters.

For a formal hedge, trim twice a year: once in mid-July to mid-August (main shape cut) and a light tidy in late January to mid-February if needed. This keeps the flowering shoots but also controls the growth. You sacrifice some of the spring flower display by cutting after the new flowering shoots develop, but a formal hedge that has flowered and not been trimmed looks untidy by June regardless.

Informal screen or boundary

The wild green form is often left much looser, maintained as a soft screen rather than a hard line. In this case, pruning moves away from shearing altogether: use loppers and secateurs to remove the oldest, most congested stems and shorten the longest outward-reaching branches. This retains more natural shape, more flower display, more fruit for wildlife, and is generally less frequent work (every two to three years rather than every summer).

An informal cherry plum screen at 2.5-3m is a good windbreak in coastal East Kent gardens. The mild winters around Sandwich Bay and Walmer mean the plant often comes into growth, and even into early flower, by mid-February. It establishes quickly, tolerates salt-laden wind reasonably well, and fills a gap faster than most alternatives.

When to trim: the key dates

Time of year What to do Why
Late Jan to mid-Feb Hard structural cuts, renovation work Before sap rise, before flowering, nesting season not yet begun
Late Feb to end of Aug Avoid cutting or inspect very carefully first Flowering then nesting season; active nest risk
Mid-July to mid-Aug Annual shape trim (formal hedges) Most first broods fledged; growth has slowed; results hold until late season
Sept to Oct Light tidy if needed; inspect first Nesting season ended; avoid wet conditions (silver leaf risk)
Nov to Jan Minor maintenance only; avoid major cuts in very wet periods Silver leaf spores most active in wet autumn/winter; wounds heal slowly in cold

Renovation: how hard can you cut?

Cherry plum responds well to hard renovation. An old, wide hedge that has spread well beyond its original footprint can be cut back to within 30-40cm of target size in late January to mid-February. The stumps will push new growth from both the cut ends and from the base. Within two seasons a well-fed, watered plant is close to looking established again.

A common mistake is cutting both sides and the top hard in the same year. This is a lot of stress on the plant. If you need to do a major renovation, cut one side hard in year one, the other side in year two. The top can follow in year two or three once the sides have regenerated and the plant has built some reserve back up.

After a hard renovation cut, mulch heavily around the base and water through any dry spells for the first summer. The plant is devoting a lot of energy to regeneration and will appreciate the help.

Silver leaf disease: the main risk for Prunus hedges

Silver leaf (Chondrostereum purpureum) can infect any Prunus species through pruning wounds. The characteristic sign is a silvery sheen on the leaves followed by the wood showing a brown or purple stain in cross-section. Infected wood eventually dies back.

The practical precautions are straightforward:

  • Prune in dry conditions. The spores spread in wet weather.
  • Use sharp, clean tools. A ragged cut is much more vulnerable than a clean one.
  • Prune in late winter when the plant is dormant, not in wet autumn or early winter.
  • If you see the silver sheen on leaves, cut back to well below the stained zone in the wood and burn or bag the cuttings. Don't compost them.

DIY versus calling a professional

For a routine annual trim of a hedge up to about 1.5-1.8m, a hedge trimmer and a bit of care is entirely manageable as a DIY job. Cherry plum is not thorny, it doesn't fight back, and it doesn't require specialist timing knowledge beyond what's on this page.

It is worth calling someone if:

  • The hedge is above about 2m and you're working from a ladder. A long-reach trimmer or a professional with a proper platform is safer.
  • You need a major renovation cut, particularly if the hedge overhangs a road or pavement. That kind of work involves larger debris, possibly a skip or a chipper, and planning the cuts to avoid creating a structural problem.
  • You see signs of silver leaf disease in a hedge that is more than a few years old. An infected hedge is still worth saving, but it requires careful work: identifying and removing all infected wood, disposing of cuttings correctly, and judging whether the plant is worth the effort.
  • The hedge sits on a boundary you share with a neighbour and the renovation involves cutting to a significantly different shape. Getting that conversation right before the chainsaw arrives is worth it.

Not sure what you've got?

Cherry plum is sometimes confused with blackthorn (thorny, darker bark, later flowers) or ornamental plums more generally. If you're working on a plant you inherited and aren't sure which Prunus you're dealing with, send us a photo in late winter when the flowers are out.
