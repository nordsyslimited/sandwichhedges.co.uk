September hedge cutting checklist: making the most of the main cutting season

Nesting season ends 31 August. September is when the main work begins across Sandwich, Deal and the villages of East Kent. Here is the checklist we run through before we start.

Updated August 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

The September cutting checklist at a glance

  1. Confirm nesting season has ended (31 August)
  2. Do a quick visual inspection for any late nests
  3. Check and prepare your trimmer (oil, blade condition, RCD lead)
  4. Put on full PPE: eye protection, ear defenders, gloves
  5. Inspect the hedge for damage, disease or gaps
  6. Conifers first: finish by end of September
  7. Cut sides before top; use a string line for formal hedges
  8. Clear all clippings from the base, lawn and borders
  9. Check heights if the hedge is near a neighbouring property
  10. Mulch the base and note any renovation work for winter

September is the pivot point of the hedge-cutting year. Nesting season ends on 31 August, the growing season is winding down, and hedges have built up a full summer's growth that needs taking back before winter. If you only cut once a year, September is when to do it, particularly in East Kent, where the mild coastal climate means hedges often carry more growth than equivalent plants further inland.

Below is what each checklist item actually means in practice.

The legal position: nesting season

The Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to damage or destroy a bird's nest that is in use or being built. Nesting season runs from 1 March to 31 August. From 1 September, cutting is generally permitted without the nesting constraint. However: late-nesting species such as pigeons and collared doves can sometimes extend into early September. A brief check before starting is always good practice, and if you find eggs or unfledged young at any time of year, leave the section of hedge undisturbed until they have fledged. See our wildlife-friendly trimming guide for the full detail.

1. Do a final nest check

Walk slowly along the hedge before you pick up the trimmer. Look for any movement at the base or in the body of the hedge. A bird repeatedly entering and leaving the same spot is a clear sign of an active nest. Look from underneath and through any gaps. Most years, by 1 September all chicks have fledged; but it takes 30 seconds to check and could save a brood.

2. Prepare your trimmer

A blunt or poorly maintained blade leaves shredded stems that are slow to heal and look untidy. Before each session:

  • Check the blade for damage, bent teeth or obvious bluntness.
  • Oil the blade lightly along both cutting edges before starting and every 20 to 30 minutes of use.
  • For electric or cordless trimmers: check the power cable for cuts or damage, and always use an RCD (residual current device) adaptor between the cable and the mains socket. If you cut through the cable, the RCD will cut power in milliseconds.
  • For petrol trimmers: check the fuel level, air filter and spark plug. Do not run the trimmer on old fuel left over from last season.

If your trimmer blade is too blunt to sharpen at home, see our hedge trimmer maintenance guide.

Before you start: safety

  • Eye protection: flying debris from a hedge trimmer is the most common cause of hedge-cutting injuries. Goggles or a face shield, not just glasses.
  • Ear defenders: petrol and many electric trimmers exceed safe noise levels for prolonged use.
  • Gloves: thornproof for hawthorn, blackthorn and pyracantha; heavy-duty for powered trimmer work on any species.
  • Ladders: most garden ladder accidents happen during hedge trimming. Use a long-reach or pole hedge trimmer wherever possible. If a ladder is needed, always have someone footing it and never overreach sideways. A stepladder on uneven ground without someone holding it is not safe.
  • Wasp nests: late summer (August and September) is peak season for wasp nests in hedges. A nest inside a dense privet or laurel may not be visible until the trimmer hits it. Go slowly along the first pass and watch for wasps flying in and out of the base of the hedge before committing to a full cut.
  • Brambles: often growing up through the hedge from the base. Gloves and long sleeves before any cutting near the base.

3. Inspect the hedge before cutting

September is a good time to notice things you missed in the growing season:

  • Brown patches or dieback: in conifers, this often indicates root damage, drought stress or brown rot on cut surfaces from previous years. See our conifer hedge going brown guide.
  • Bare patches at the base: common in privet and laurel that has been cut too hard or shaded out at the base. If bare at the bottom, cutting is not the solution; this needs to be addressed differently.
  • Signs of box blight or box tree moth: a September cut spreads blight spores. If your box hedge shows signs of blight, disinfect blades between hedges and dispose of clippings away from the garden.
  • Honey fungus: visible as white mycelium at the base or as clusters of honey-coloured toadstools in autumn. See our honey fungus guide.

4. Conifers first

If you have leylandii, thuja plicata or lawson cypress, cut these before anything else and finish by the end of September. Once October arrives and night temperatures drop in East Kent, a conifer cut is more likely to result in persistent browning on the cut face. The colder, wetter conditions prevent the cut surface from hardening before the first frosts. September is still warm enough for the hedge to recover.

The golden rule for conifers: stay inside the green. Do not cut into brown wood. Conifers do not regenerate from bare stems (yew is the exception). See our leylandii rules guide for the detail on cutting depth and height.

5. Cut sides before top; use a string line

On a formal hedge, the sides come first: work from the bottom up on each face, letting the clippings fall away as you go. Do not start at the top, because clippings falling onto untrimmed lower sections will stick and make it harder to see the line.

For a clean, straight top, stretch a string line at the desired height between two canes before you start. It takes two minutes and the difference is visible. See our how to trim a hedge straight guide for the full method.

6. Clear all clippings promptly

Clippings left on the lawn or in borders will kill grass and promote mould under the hedge. Clear as you go or at the end of each section. For composting options, see our composting hedge trimmings guide.

7. Check heights near boundaries

If your hedge runs along a boundary with a neighbour, September is the time to assess height. The High Hedges Act (Anti-social Behaviour Act 2003, Part 8) allows a council to issue a remedial notice on an evergreen or semi-evergreen hedge over 2 metres that is blocking a neighbour's light or access. A pre-emptive September cut that brings the height back is far preferable to a council notice. See our hedge heights and neighbour law guide.

8. Mulch after cutting

Once the main trim is done, run a 5 to 7 cm layer of bark chip along the base of the hedge (kept away from the stems). Mulching in September locks in soil moisture ahead of the autumn dry spells that follow the first rains in East Kent, and suppresses weeds that would otherwise compete with hedge roots through winter.

9. Note what needs professional attention

September is also the planning window for winter work. A hedge that is significantly too wide, too tall or showing signs of real structural decline is better addressed in dormancy (November to February for deciduous species, or early spring for evergreens), not in September when cutting stress is already high. Make a note now, book in winter.
