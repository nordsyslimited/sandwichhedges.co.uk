Overgrown and gappy hedge renovation

When an overgrown hedge also has bare or dead sections: how to assess the damage, decide between cutting back and replanting, and sequence the work correctly.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

A hedge that is both oversized and gappy is a more complicated problem than either of those faults separately. The usual impulse is to cut it back hard and hope the gaps fill. That sometimes works, and sometimes turns a gappy but functional screen into an open structure with no privacy at all.

The right approach depends on what is causing the gaps. The sequence below works through the question systematically. It is based on what we see in Sandwich, Deal and East Kent gardens: hedges that grew well for a decade or two, lost sections to a dry summer, a harsh frost, or box blight, and are now both oversized and patchy.

Step 1: diagnose the gaps before you cut anything

Before deciding on a plan, understand what is causing the bare sections. The cause affects the solution.

  • Dead stems from frost or drought. The affected sections have no live growth; the stems are brittle and snap cleanly. The surrounding hedge is otherwise growing well. This is the most straightforward: the dead sections can be cleared and replanted into the existing hedge once the size reduction is done.
  • Die-back from disease (box blight, honey fungus). If the gaps are in a box hedge and the dead sections have white mycelium at the soil line, or black bootlace-like structures in the soil (honey fungus), the situation is different. Replanting the same species into infected soil will fail. See our box blight guide for the specific approach.
  • Bare base from drawn-up growth. The lower part of the hedge has no leaves but the stems are otherwise live. This happens when hedges are cut on the top and sides but not given a slight inward taper, starving the base of light. The stems are alive and will respond to cutting, but it takes two to three seasons to refill.
  • Mixed picture. Parts dead, parts leggy and drawn-up, parts simply overgrown. This is the most common scenario and is dealt with in Step 4 below.

Step 2: assess whether the living sections are worth keeping

Before investing in renovation, make a cold-eyed assessment. If more than a third of the hedge is dead or dying and the remaining live sections are weak and drawn-up, complete removal and replanting on a prepared site will produce a better hedge in five years than a patched renovation. This is not a failure; it is a practical judgement.

The cases where renovation is worthwhile: at least two-thirds of the hedge is structurally sound and growing actively; the gaps are confined to specific sections rather than distributed throughout; and the hedge is of a species that regenerates well from old wood (privet, hawthorn, hornbeam, beech, laurel, holly).

Step 3: the decision

Gaps are dead sections in an otherwise sound, overgrown hedge (most common)
Proceed to Step 4: the two-phase renovation. Reduce first, then replant into the cleared gaps.
Gaps are caused by a spreading disease (box blight, honey fungus)
Clear and replant using a different species. Do not prune and try to retain the remaining live material in an infected run. See the relevant disease guide before proceeding.
More than one-third of the hedge is dead or the remaining growth is weak throughout
Remove and replant. We cover the process in our how-to plant a hedge guide.

Step 4: the two-phase renovation for sound overgrown hedges with dead sections

Phase 1 (September to February): reduce the size

Carry out the size reduction first, before dealing with the gaps. This is counterintuitive but important: cutting the hedge back to its target dimensions first establishes the new surface that the gap-filling plants will need to match. Planting into an oversized hedge and then cutting back a year later will force the new plants through an unnecessary second major cut.

For the size reduction itself, follow the phased approach: reduce the top in year one, then the sides in year two for most species. For deciduous and evergreen broadleaf hedges such as privet, hawthorn, laurel and beech, this is straightforward. For conifers, see the leylandii-specific guidance in our overgrown hedge rescue guide.

Nesting season: September to February is the safe window

It is an offence under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. The nesting season runs from 1 March to 31 August. Overgrown, gappy hedges are used intensively as nesting sites. Plan both the size reduction and any replanting work for September to February where possible. During the nesting season, only proceed if you have checked for active nests and found none.

Phase 2 (following autumn to spring, October to March): fill the gaps

Once the size reduction is underway and the hedge is at or approaching its target height, plant replacements into the gaps. Use bare-root plants for cost and ease of establishment: they go in between October and March.

Match the species. If the hedge is privet, replace with privet. If it is hawthorn, replace with hawthorn. Unless the existing species has been identified as the problem, matching species will close the gaps most effectively.

Spacing in gaps: for a hedge-height gap (a section that is completely missing), plant at the same centres as the existing hedge, usually 30 to 45cm for privet and hawthorn, 50 to 60cm for hornbeam and beech. For a base-gap where the upper structure is present but the lower section is bare, plant 20 to 30cm from the existing stems and train the new growth upward.

New plants in gaps will be several years behind the existing hedge in size. Manage the existing hedge a little less hard for the first two to three seasons to allow the new plants to catch up. Once the gap plants reach the height of the established hedge, resume normal maintenance cutting. For more on the planting and establishment process, see our guide to filling gaps in hedges.

What if the whole hedge needs replanting?

If the assessment in Step 2 leads to a decision to remove and replant, the gap-filling approach becomes a full-clearance project. This is worth doing properly: clear all the old material and roots, improve the soil with organic matter, and plant a new hedge on a good base. A hedge planted well on a prepared site establishes faster than one planted into tired, root-bound ground.

If only sections of the hedge are beyond saving, the replanted sections will need several years to catch up in height. Manage the established sections more lightly during this period to close the visual gap.
