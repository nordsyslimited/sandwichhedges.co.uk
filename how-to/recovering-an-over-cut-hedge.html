How to recover an over-cut hedge

Someone went too hard, too late, or into the brown wood, and now the hedge looks terrible. Here is what you can actually do about it, in the order you should do it.

Updated September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

The eight-point recovery checklist

  1. Confirm the hedge is alive (the scratch test)
  2. Stop all cutting immediately
  3. Remove genuinely dead stems only
  4. Water deeply if the soil is dry
  5. Apply a slow-release feed in spring
  6. Mulch the base to lock in moisture
  7. Wait a full growing season before judging the result
  8. Know when it is time to accept a replant

We see over-cut hedges fairly regularly, usually after a new owner has moved in and gone at the boundary with enthusiasm, or after a contractor has cut a conifer hedge back much harder than the customer asked for. The thing is, not all over-cut hedges are as bad as they look, and recovery is more straightforward than most people expect, provided the plant is still alive and the roots are intact.

The approach depends heavily on species. Broadleaved hedges (privet, laurel, beech, hornbeam, hawthorn, hazel) will push new growth from old wood. Conifers mostly will not. That single fact determines whether recovery is possible or whether a replant is the honest answer.

Before you do anything: nesting season

Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs roughly from 1 March to 31 August. If your hedge is producing new growth during that period, birds may be using it. Check thoroughly before removing any stems. If you are in any doubt, wait until September.

The checklist in detail

1. Confirm the hedge is alive: the scratch test

Before anything else, find out what you are actually dealing with. Take a fingernail or a penknife and scrape the bark on a stem you are worried about. Green or white-green underneath means it is alive. Dry, brown and papery means it is dead. Do this in several spots across the affected area. A hedge can have a patchwork of live and dead stems, which changes your approach considerably.

If the roots are healthy and at least some stems pass the scratch test, you have something to work with. If every scratch reveals dead, dry wood, the plant is gone and the discussion moves to replanting.

2. Stop all cutting immediately

This is the one most people resist. The hedge looks thin or lopsided and the instinct is to tidy it up. Do not. Every cut you make right now removes potential growing points and stresses a plant that is already struggling. Leave it alone, shape and all, for the rest of this growing season.

3. Remove genuinely dead stems only

Once you have completed the scratch test across the whole hedge, you can remove stems that are definitively dead. Cut them cleanly to a junction with live wood, or back to ground level if the whole stem is gone. Dead wood does not contribute to recovery and can harbour disease. Live stems, however brown they look on the surface, stay.

4. Water deeply if the soil is dry

An over-cut hedge is a stressed hedge. Stress is compounded by drought. In a dry Kent summer (and we do get them, particularly on the chalk soils around Sandwich and Ash), a hedge that has been hard-cut will struggle to push new growth without adequate moisture. Give it a long, slow soak rather than a quick splash. The aim is to wet the soil down to root depth, roughly 30 to 45 cm. Do this weekly if there is no meaningful rain.

5. Apply a slow-release feed in spring

Wait until the following spring for this step. Feeding a stressed plant in late summer or autumn encourages soft new growth that can get frost-damaged. In March or April, apply a balanced slow-release granular fertiliser (something like 7-7-7 NPK, or a proprietary hedging fertiliser) along the base of the hedge, watered in well. This gives the plant the nitrogen it needs to produce new foliage without forcing it too fast.

6. Mulch the base

A 5 to 8 cm layer of wood chip or garden compost along the root zone does three useful things: it retains moisture, suppresses weeds that compete for nutrients, and moderates soil temperature. Keep the mulch a few centimetres away from the stems themselves to avoid collar rot. This is a cheap intervention that makes a real difference over a recovery period of two to three seasons.

7. Wait a full growing season before judging

This is the hardest part. A broadleaved hedge that has been over-cut in late summer may look dreadful all winter and then push vigorous new growth the following April. Do not declare it dead in November. Give it until the following summer before forming a view, and another year beyond that before it looks anything like normal again. Rosy Hardy, RHS Vice President and plantsperson with 24 Chelsea Gold Medals, makes exactly this point in her video on rejuvenating mistreated hedges: patience is the primary ingredient.

8. Know when it is time to accept a replant

Some hedges do not come back. The most common candidates for a replant rather than a recovery are:

  • Leylandii and most conifers cut back into bare brown wood. There are no dormant buds in the dead zone; these species cannot regenerate from bare wood.
  • Any hedge where the scratch test finds dead wood throughout. If the roots have gone, nothing above ground will recover.
  • Box hedges severely affected by box blight or box tree moth alongside hard cutting. The cumulative stress is often too much.
  • Hedges cut repeatedly at the wrong time over several years, where the root system has been gradually depleted.

Accepting a replant is not a failure. A new bare-root hedge of privet or hornbeam planted in autumn will establish quickly and look presentable within two to three seasons, which is roughly the same timescale as nursing a badly damaged hedge back to health. See our guide to planting bare-root hedges if you reach that decision.

Species notes for Kent gardens

Privet

Very forgiving. Even hard renovation cuts tend to produce vigorous regrowth provided some live wood remains. One of the most resilient hedging plants for East Kent gardens.

Laurel (cherry and Portuguese)

Responds well to hard cuts if made at the right time (late spring or late summer). An over-cut made during dry weather may need extra watering support. Use secateurs rather than shears on new stems to avoid shredded-leaf edges.

Beech and hornbeam

Will regenerate from old wood. They are slower than privet but very reliable. Renovation cuts are usually best made in late winter when you can see the structure clearly.

Yew

Surprisingly good at recovering from hard cuts, provided the wood is genuinely live. The scratch test is essential with yew as the surface of old wood looks dead even when the plant is fine. Slow to push new growth; allow two full seasons.

Leylandii and cypress species

Once the cut goes past the green zone into bare brown wood, there is no recovery from that section. The surrounding green growth may partially disguise the gap over time, but the brown zone will not resprout. For leylandii that have been cut too hard, see our leylandii guide for what options remain.
