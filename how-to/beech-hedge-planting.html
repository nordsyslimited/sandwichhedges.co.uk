How to plant a beech hedge in Kent

Beech makes one of the best formal hedges in East Kent: it establishes well on chalk downland, keeps its russet leaves through winter, and responds to regular trimming with tight, dense growth. Here is a seven-step planting guide for bare-root stock.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Beech (Fagus sylvatica) has been grown as a garden hedge in Kent for centuries. The formal beech hedges in the gardens around Sandwich, Worth and Eastry are some of the most striking in the county: dense, clean-topped, and honey-brown through the winter months. Planting one is straightforward if you get the basics right.

November to March is the bare-root window. Bare-root beech whips are inexpensive and available from UK hedging nurseries throughout this period. Early planting, in November or December, gives roots the longest possible time to establish before the following summer's dry spells.

Before you start: a few checks

Beech dislikes permanently waterlogged soil. If the planting area sits wet through winter, improve drainage before you plant: dig a sump at one end of the trench, or incorporate coarse grit into the soil. On thin chalk, the opposite applies: beech is well adapted, but the first summer can be hard if the weather turns very dry. Have water available. If there is any existing vegetation that might harbour nesting birds, check before clearing it in spring and summer.

Step 1: Choose and order your plants

Bare-root beech whips are sold in several sizes: 40–60 cm, 60–90 cm and 90–120 cm are the most common. Smaller whips are cheaper and often establish more reliably because they put less energy into supporting existing top growth and more into root development. For a new garden boundary, 60–90 cm whips are a practical middle ground.

Common beech (Fagus sylvatica) is the standard choice. Copper beech (F. sylvatica 'Purpurea') can also be grown as a hedge and gives a striking dark-purple tone through summer, fading to russet in winter. Some gardens mix the two for a variegated effect, though keeping a consistent trim height is easier with a single variety.

When the plants arrive, check that the roots are moist and the stems are not dried out. Plant within a day or two of delivery. If you cannot plant immediately, heel the plants into a shaded corner with roots covered in damp soil.

Step 2: Prepare the ground

Mark the planting line with a string line or canes. Strip the turf along a 60 cm-wide strip and remove any perennial weeds, particularly bindweed, couch grass and thistles. These will compete hard with young plants in year one and are far more difficult to remove once the hedge is established.

Fork over the soil to 25–30 cm depth. On chalk, work some garden compost or leaf mould into the trench bottom to improve moisture retention. Beech is naturally chalk-adapted and does not need rich soil; it just needs not to dry out completely in its first summer.

On heavier clay soil, common in the lower-lying areas around Ash and Woodnesborough, fork in coarse grit to open up the structure. Clay retains plenty of moisture but can become compacted, which restricts root development in the first year.

Step 3: Dig the planting trench

For a row of plants, a continuous trench is quicker than individual holes and ensures consistent depth along the whole line. Dig 25–30 cm wide and 20–25 cm deep. Keep the excavated soil to one side, mixed with any compost or grit you are incorporating.

If you are planting just a few plants, individual holes are fine: dig each to about 1.5 times the root spread. The key is consistent depth, with the root collar at soil level and not buried below it.

Step 4: Set the spacing

Plant three beech whips per metre, spaced roughly 30–35 cm apart in a single row. This is slightly wider than hawthorn or blackthorn because beech spreads more at the base and benefits from a little more room to develop without competition from its neighbours in the first few years. At this spacing, the plants begin to form a recognisable hedge within two to three years.

A double staggered row (two rows offset, 30 cm apart) fills in faster but significantly increases plant cost and complicates access for future trimming. A single well-spaced row is the standard approach for most garden hedges.

Step 5: Plant at the correct depth

The root collar (the point where the stem meets the roots, often visible as a slight change in colour on the stem) should sit at soil level, neither above it nor buried below it. Fan the roots out in the trench bottom, making sure they are not crumpled or coiled. Backfill with the excavated soil, firming it in with your boot as you go in stages to remove air pockets.

Once each plant is firmed in, give it a gentle tug: it should resist. If it pulls out too easily, firm it in more thoroughly.

Step 6: Formative cut immediately after planting

This is the step that most people are reluctant to do, and it is the most important one. Immediately after planting, cut each whip back by roughly one-third to one-half. This seems counterintuitive but it is well-established practice for good reason.

Cutting back forces energy into the base of the plant rather than the tip, producing dense growth from the bottom up. Without this formative cut, beech whips tend to grow straight up, leaving a gap at the base of the hedge that is very hard to close once the plants mature. The spare stems can be laid over the trench as a temporary windbreak while the new hedge establishes.

Use clean, sharp secateurs. The cut should be diagonal, just above an outward-facing bud.

Step 7: Water in and mulch

Water each plant in well after planting, even in November. Then apply a 10 cm layer of bark mulch or composted wood chip along the full planting strip, keeping it 5 cm clear of the stems to prevent collar rot. The mulch holds moisture, suppresses grass and weeds, and moderates soil temperature through the winter and the following summer.

If rabbits are a problem in the area, fit a spiral guard or mesh sleeve around each plant. Rabbits will strip the bark from bare-root whips in winter, particularly on chalk downland where grass is short and cover is limited. Fit the guards before you walk away from the site.

Beech and the nesting season

Planting is fine year-round. All trimming and shaping, however, must respect the bird nesting season. Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to damage or destroy an active nest. Nesting season runs approximately 1 March to 31 August. The safe trimming window is September to February.

What to expect in years one to three

Year one is not dramatic. Beech puts energy into roots rather than top growth in its first season. Some plants may shed remaining leaves and sit looking dormant through spring; this is normal. Keep the mulch topped up and water during any dry spell from April to September. Do not trim in year one.

Year two brings noticeably more growth. The plants start to look like a hedge. A light trim in late August or September of year two helps thicken the structure. Keep this trim light: you are encouraging bushy side growth, not trying to reach the final height yet.

By year three, a properly planted beech hedge in East Kent begins to function as a real boundary. The plants start to knit together, and the characteristic russet winter leaves begin to appear on the lower branches. From this point, an annual trim in late summer is usually sufficient to maintain good shape.
