Building a living willow fedge in East Kent

A fedge (fence meets hedge) built from willow whips takes root, grows into a living screen, and gets stronger every year. Here is how to install one in an East Kent garden, from marking out the line to the annual winter weave.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

A conventional fence rots. A conventional hedge takes years to fill in. A living willow fedge gives you a full-height structure on day one and improves with every growing season. The willow rods root into the ground, leaf out in spring, and the diamond lattice that would be gaps in year one becomes a dense tangle of living stems by year three.

East Kent suits willow exceptionally well. The damp low-lying ground around Sandwich, the drainage channels running across the levels towards Eastry and Worth, and the heavy clay soils that hold moisture: all of these are conditions where willow thrives. If your garden sits on or near damp ground, a fedge is worth serious consideration.

The living willow techniques shown in Musgrove Willows' YouTube channel (7,100 subscribers; UK's leading willow nursery and supplier) demonstrate how living willow structures are woven and maintained in practice, and are a useful complement to the steps below.

Before you start: safety checks

  • Wear thick gloves: willow splinters are sharp and the cut ends are pointed.
  • Check for underground services before driving stakes: call Dial Before You Dig if in any doubt.
  • Nesting season (1 March to 31 August): under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, do not disturb an active nest. A fedge should be planted in the dormant season (November to March) in any case, so this rarely applies, but check the ground area for ground-nesting birds before driving stakes in spring.
  • Work on damp but not waterlogged ground: if the ground is saturated, wait for it to drain slightly or the rods will heave out of the ground as the soil settles.

What you will need

  • Living willow whips (rods), 1.8 to 2.5 m long: order from a specialist willow nursery such as Musgrove Willows. For a 5 m fedge you will need approximately 50 to 60 rods (20 to 25 per metre).
  • A mallet or post-driver for making pilot holes in clay soils.
  • Biodegradable raffia or similar natural tie for securing crossings.
  • Weed-suppressing membrane (optional) along the base, to keep grass competition away in year one.
  • A bucket of water to keep the rods moist while you work.
  • Loppers and a folding saw for the annual maintenance trim.

Step-by-step installation

Step 1: Mark out the line

Use canes and string to mark the exact line of the fedge. A living fedge is usually 30 to 40 centimetres wide at the base. Mark both edges of the footprint. For a straight fedge in a long East Kent garden, string pulled taut between two stakes gives you a true line even on slightly uneven ground.

Step 2: Prepare the ground

Remove grass and weeds from the planting strip to a width of at least 40 centimetres. On clay soil, loosen the top 20 centimetres with a fork. You do not need to add compost or fertiliser: willow is not demanding. If the ground is very compacted, a single pass with a fork is enough to allow the rods to go in easily.

Step 3: Source and condition your whips

Order bare rods from a UK willow specialist in November or December for a winter planting. The rods should be freshly cut: anything stored for more than a week or two needs 24 to 48 hours standing in a bucket of water before planting to rehydrate the cut ends. Keep them out of direct sun and wind until you are ready to plant. Salix viminalis (common osier) is the standard choice for East Kent conditions: it is reliable, fast-growing and undemanding.

Step 4: Plant the diagonal rods

Push the rods into the ground at a 45-degree angle, alternating direction to create a diamond lattice. Space them 20 to 25 centimetres apart. The fat end of the rod (the base end, which was closest to the root) goes into the ground: willow roots from the cambium layer just under the bark, and it does this more readily from the base end than the tip. Push each rod to a depth of 20 to 30 centimetres. In East Kent clay, a piece of dowel or an old broom handle driven in first makes the job much easier.

Step 5: Tie the crossings

Where two rods cross, tie them loosely with biodegradable raffia. Do not use wire or plastic ties: as the rods thicken over the years, a tight tie will strangle the stem. Raffia breaks down within two or three years, by which point the rods have often fused together at the crossing points naturally, a process called inosculation, which adds strength to the structure.

Step 6: First-year care

Water every seven to ten days if there is no useful rain for the first six weeks after planting, particularly in a dry March. Keep the base weed-free: grass competition is the main risk to establishment, not drought in East Kent's normally damp spring. Resist the urge to trim in the first summer: the new shoots are building root mass, and cutting them back early reduces the plant's energy reserves going into the first winter.

Step 7: Annual winter maintenance weave

Each winter, from December to February, spend an hour or two weaving the new long shoots back into the lattice. Bring the longest new growth down at an angle and weave it under and over existing rods. Tie crossings loosely if needed. This is how the fedge grows denser and how you extend it in height if you want it taller. Cut back any growth that is beyond what you want to incorporate. This session is done by hand with loppers and a saw, no powered tools required.

Step 8: Summer trim

In July or August, cut back the outward-pointing side shoots to keep the fedge tidy and to stop it spreading sideways. This is a light trim, like a conventional hedge tidy, but done with loppers rather than a hedge trimmer (the willow is too thick and the lattice too irregular for a straight machine cut). Check for active nests before cutting (see nesting season note above), particularly on the north-facing side of the fedge where birds sometimes nest in the denser growth.

How wide, how high and how long does it last?

A fedge can be built to any height between 1 and 3 metres. Above 3 metres it becomes difficult to weave without a ladder, and the structure can become unstable without additional support posts at intervals. Width at the base is typically 30 to 40 centimetres, widening slightly as the structure matures. A well-maintained fedge is effectively permanent: willow lives for decades, and the fused crossings become woody and very strong. Fedges installed in the 1990s in the permaculture gardens and smallholdings of rural Kent are still in excellent condition today.

Wildlife value

Willow flowers (catkins) appear in March and April and are an important early nectar source for bees emerging from winter. The dense lattice provides good cover and nesting opportunities for small birds. In the drainage-channel landscape between Sandwich and Eastry, a willow fedge also makes visual and ecological sense as a complement to the waterway willows already in the landscape.

A note on boundary disputes

A living fedge eventually takes on a width of 40 to 60 centimetres. Plant it far enough inside your boundary that this width does not encroach on a neighbour's land. For guidance on what the law says about boundary hedges and overhanging growth, see our separate guide to hedge heights and neighbour law.
