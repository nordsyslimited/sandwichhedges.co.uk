Getting a crisp finish on a formal yew hedge: trimmer vs hand shears

Yew rewards precision more than almost any other hedge species. Here is how professionals approach the annual cut, and where the difference between a near-miss and a truly sharp finish comes down to tool choice.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Yew (Taxus baccata) is a forgiving plant in almost every way: it tolerates deep shade, hard soils, annual clipping for centuries, and cuts back to old wood that most other conifers will not regrow from. The one thing it does not forgive is a sloppy finish. The dark, dense foliage holds its edge so well that any waviness in the top line, any hollow in the face, or any missed section is visible from twenty metres away. That visibility is what makes yew formal hedging so satisfying when it is done well, and so disappointing when it is not.

Before you start: yew is toxic, and nesting applies

Yew toxicity. All parts of yew are poisonous except the red flesh of the berry, and even that is harmful to horses and cattle. Wear gloves throughout: the sap on freshly cut foliage transfers to skin and is absorbed slowly. Wash hands before eating or touching your face. Keep children and pets off the area while cutting and until clippings are cleared. Never burn yew: the smoke carries the alkaloids. Bag clippings or put them on a compost heap away from grazing animals.

Eye and ear protection. Yew throws small stiff fragments when trimmed. Wear eye protection. For petrol or electric trimmers, add ear defenders.

Nesting season. Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to damage an active bird's nest. Yew is particularly attractive to nesting birds because the dense cover persists year-round. The nesting season runs roughly 1 March to 31 August. Check the hedge before cutting in this window. The main formal cut belongs in late August to September, when most nesting has ended. The safe cutting window is September to February.

When to do the main formal cut

The standard advice for yew in the UK is late August to early September. By then the new growth that appeared in spring has hardened enough to hold a clean edge, the nesting season is effectively over, and there is enough of the growing season left for the cut surface to toughen slightly before the first hard frost arrives. In coastal East Kent, where winters are milder than inland, there is usually a further week or two of leeway: cuts taken in mid-September still tighten up before December.

A second light pass in late February or early March, just before new growth flushes, can refresh the winter silhouette and remove anything that looks ragged after frost. This is a tidy pass of a few centimetres, not a shaping cut.

The core question: trimmer or hand shears?

This is where most DIY formal yew cuts fall short. The honest answer is: both, in that order.

Factor Powered hedge trimmer Hand shears / secateurs
Speed on straight faces Fast: covers 1 m² in under a minute Slow: suited to short runs and detail
Finish quality on long faces Good with a second pass; slightly feathery after one pass Excellent on short sections; fatiguing on long runs
Corners and edges Difficult to get a crisp right-angle or arris Best tool for clean corners, arrises and top edges
Topiary details Not suitable for curves or domed finials Essential: single-handed shears or bypass secateurs
Following a string line Good: long blade is easy to run parallel to string Fine on the top; more difficult to maintain a flat side face
Physical demand Low fatigue on long hedges; heavier overhead High fatigue on long runs; lighter overhead
Best use Main bulk removal on all faces Finishing pass on corners, top arrises and topiary

The practical method is: do the main bulk cut with a powered trimmer (battery-powered is quieter and easier to manoeuvre than corded for this type of work), then go back over the whole hedge by hand for the finishing pass. The hand pass takes about a third of the time of the trimmer pass. It is the step most DIY cutters skip, and it is the step that produces the formal finish.

The string line: more important on yew than on privet

Because yew holds such a sharp edge, any deviation in the top line is clearly visible from a distance. A string guide matters more here than on a privet or leylandii hedge, where slightly uneven growth softens the outline.

Set the string at the finished height at both ends (see our guide on correcting an uneven hedge top for the full setup method). On a formal yew, also consider running a second string along the base of each face, 30 to 40 centimetres from the ground, to help maintain the batter angle. The batter is the slight lean inward from bottom to top: the base should be a few centimetres wider than the top. Without it, the lower growth is shaded by the overhanging top and gradually dies back, leaving a thin and bare lower section.

The two-pass technique in detail

Pass one: the trimmer pass

Start at one end of the top face. Hold the trimmer blade flat and parallel to the string, not angled into the hedge. Move steadily along the length in one continuous pass, keeping the blade just under the string without touching it. Repeat for each side face: start at the bottom of each side and work upward, so clippings fall away from the part you are still cutting. After the trimmer pass, the hedge should be close to the target shape but the cut surface will have a slightly ragged, frosted look where the blade has cut through to the tips of the foliage.

Pass two: the hand-shears pass

The second pass uses single-handed garden shears (not long-handled loppers). Working in the opposite direction to the first pass, take off the remaining loose tips with short, flat cuts. On the top face, keep the blades horizontal and work along the top, pausing every metre to sight back along the line. On the side faces, hold the blades parallel to the face and sweep in short horizontal strokes from the bottom upward. Particular attention goes to the arris: the upper corner where the top face meets the side face. This is the edge most visible from a distance. A clean right-angle here is the single biggest visual improvement you can make to a formal yew hedge.

Corners

External corners (the outside of a turn) are cut by working each face in turn and using a single sweep of the shears to remove any projection at the arris. Internal corners (where two faces meet inside a return wall or gateway) are easier to tidy with a small pair of bypass secateurs or topiary scissors rather than full-size shears.

The batter angle

The rule of thumb is five centimetres of batter per metre of height. A two-metre yew hedge should be approximately 10 centimetres wider at the base than at the top on each face. You can check this with a batter board: two pieces of timber hinged at the top and set to the correct angle, which you hold against the face as you cut. Or you can simply develop an eye for it by checking the face profile from the end of the hedge every few passes.

DIY or professional?

A formal yew hedge up to about 1.5 metres tall and 10 metres long is a manageable DIY project for someone who is comfortable with a powered trimmer and has a few hours to give to the finishing pass. Above 1.5 metres, access becomes the main issue: the top face needs to be reached safely, and for a two-metre or taller hedge that usually means a scaffold board between stepladders, or a pole trimmer and separate finishing tools. Above 2.5 metres, or for any hedge with topiary features such as domed finials, buttresses or crenellations, the time and access requirements usually make professional trimming genuinely cost-effective.
