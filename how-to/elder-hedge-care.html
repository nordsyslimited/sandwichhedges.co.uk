Elder hedge care: Sambucus nigra in Kent

Elder vs hawthorn vs blackthorn compared as native hedges for East Kent gardens, with pruning timing, wildlife value and the one thing most people get wrong when cutting elder in summer.

Updated September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Elder (Sambucus nigra) is one of the most common plants in the East Kent countryside. Drive along any lane between Sandwich and Eastry in June and you will see its flat cream flower heads catching the light from the tops of field boundaries. It has been used as a hedgerow plant in Kent for centuries, tolerating the chalk and sandy loam soils of this corner of England without complaint.

In a garden context, elder is often overlooked in favour of more predictable hedge species. That is understandable: it grows fast and loosely, it does not make a formal edge, and if you leave it alone for a few years you end up with a sprawling 4-metre bush rather than a tidy boundary. But handled with a simple annual prune, it is one of the most wildlife-productive hedging plants you can grow, and it suits a garden in Ash, Worth or Woodnesborough that wants a natural, country feel to the boundary rather than a clipped formal line.

Nesting season and elder: a hedge full of birds

Elder is heavily used as a nesting site by blackbirds, song thrushes, sparrows and wrens. Under the UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 it is an offence to damage or destroy a nest in active use. Nesting season runs roughly from 1 March to 31 August. The safe pruning window for elder is late autumn through February. September is the first safe month. If you need to do a light tidy before that, check the hedge very carefully from the outside before touching it.

Elder vs hawthorn vs blackthorn: which native hedge for Kent?

These are the three most common native hedgerow species in East Kent. Each has a distinct character, and choosing the right one depends on what you want from the hedge.

The short version: if you want a formal-looking boundary that is also wildlife-friendly, hawthorn is probably the better choice. If you want wildlife value and are happy with an informal, naturalistic boundary that changes through the seasons, elder is excellent. If you want a stock-proof or people-proof barrier, blackthorn is the most formidable of the three but the hardest to maintain safely.

A mixed hedge using all three is also a very traditional East Kent solution. Many of the old field boundaries around Worth, Ash and Eastry use exactly this combination, with field maple (Acer campestre) sometimes added for autumn colour.

Before you start pruning

Safety before you cut

  • Eye protection: elder stems are soft but fast-growing, and debris from cutting is unpredictable. Wrap-around safety glasses or goggles.
  • Sturdy gloves: elder sap can cause mild skin irritation in some people. Heavy work gloves are sensible.
  • Ear defenders for any petrol or prolonged electric trimmer use.
  • Prefer a long-reach trimmer over a ladder where height allows. Most elder hedges can be topped with a pole trimmer from ground level. If you must use a ladder, have someone footing it and do not overreach sideways.
  • RCD protection for any corded electric tool.
  • Check for wasp nests at the base and within the hedge before cutting. Elder's dense growth and hollow stems can shelter wasps in summer.

How to prune elder

The annual prune (late winter, February in Kent)

Cut the previous year's growth back by roughly half to two-thirds. Aim to keep the plant at the height and width you want, rather than letting it gain a metre of new growth each year. Use loppers or a pruning saw for thicker stems, and remove any crossing, dead or diseased wood as you go. The cut surface will look bare immediately after but new growth is rapid: by late April the hedge will already be filling out again.

Rejuvenation pruning

If the elder has been neglected and become a 3 or 4-metre multi-stemmed thicket, cut the whole thing back to 15 to 30 centimetres from the ground in February. This sounds drastic but elder is almost impossible to kill this way. Within a few weeks of mild weather, new growth breaks strongly from the base. A rejuvenated elder will be growing vigorously again within a single season and can be shaped from that point onward.

After pruning

A light mulch of bark or compost around the base after the winter prune is appreciated but not essential. Elder is not a plant that needs much feeding on Kent's reasonably fertile soils.

Ornamental elder: the 'Black Lace' question

Sambucus nigra 'Black Lace' and the similar 'Eva' are ornamental elderberries with dark purple, almost black, finely cut foliage. They have the same pruning requirements as the native elder: hard annual cut in late winter, with the added benefit of more dramatic foliage colour on the fresh growth. They produce pink flowers rather than the native cream. These ornamental forms are often used as specimen shrubs rather than hedges, but they can be combined with native elder in an informal boundary if you want the colour contrast.

Wildlife value in detail

If wildlife benefit is a priority, elder is genuinely one of the best choices available. The flat-topped flower heads in June and July (called corymbs) attract hoverflies, beetles and bees in large numbers. The berries from August to October are a food source for blackbirds, song thrushes, starlings and wood pigeons, along with small mammals. The dense, leafy growth provides nesting cover from March onwards for a range of garden birds, and the hollow elder stems are sometimes used by solitary bees for nesting.

See our hedges as wildlife corridors guide and wildlife-friendly trimming guide for more on timing cuts to protect the wildlife value of your boundary.

An elder hedge is not tidy in the way a box hedge is tidy. But it is genuinely useful: flowers in summer, berries in autumn, dense cover all year. On a country-edge plot in Worth or Ash, that is often exactly what you want.
