Planting a hawthorn hedge: a step-by-step checklist

Hawthorn is the most forgiving and wildlife-rich hedging plant you can put in. Get these basics right and it largely looks after itself.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Quick checklist: planting a hawthorn hedge

  1. Choose bare-root whips (40-60cm) for economy, or pot-grown for year-round planting
  2. Plant late October to late March (November-December ideal)
  3. Single row: 30-40cm spacing. Double staggered row: 30-40cm spacing, rows 30cm apart
  4. Dig a trench or individual holes: 30cm deep, roots spread naturally at nursery depth
  5. Improve the soil with well-rotted compost or manure at 1:2 ratio
  6. Firm in well, water thoroughly even in wet weather
  7. Mulch 7-10cm deep, kept clear of stems
  8. Cut back by a third after planting or in the first winter to encourage bushy base growth
  9. Water in the first dry spring: hawthorn establishes well but benefits from water in April-May of year one
  10. Protect from rabbits or deer if they are present on the site

Hawthorn (Crataegus monogyna) is the backbone of the traditional East Kent hedgerow. It's fast, thorny enough to be genuinely stock-proof within three or four years, produces spectacular blossom in May and dense berry crops in autumn, and supports more insect species than almost any other UK native plant. In November 2024, Monty Don planted a new hawthorn hedge around the orchard at Longmeadow, demonstrating just how simple the process is when the conditions are right.

Gardeners' World 2024 Episode 32 (BBC), featuring hawthorn hedge planting in the orchard at Longmeadow.

Plant outside nesting season

Hawthorn hedges are prime nesting habitat for thrushes, robins, wrens and whitethroats. Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, disturbing an active nest is an offence. The nesting season runs roughly from 1 March to 31 August. The ideal planting window, late October to late March, falls within the safe period and coincides with hawthorn's dormant season. If you must do any ground preparation close to an existing hedge in spring, check for nesting activity first.

1. When to plant

The window for bare-root hawthorn is late October to late March. November and December are the optimum months in East Kent: the soil is still holding some warmth from summer, winter rains do most of the establishment watering, and the plant can put roots down before needing to push leaves in spring. January and February work well if the ground is not frozen or sitting in standing water.

Avoid planting in a frozen or waterlogged spell. If your bare-root plants arrive and the ground is unusable, heel them in temporarily: dig a shallow hole or trench in a sheltered corner, cover the roots with damp soil and they'll keep for several weeks.

2. Bare-root or pot-grown?

For a long hedge run, bare-root whips are considerably cheaper and establish just as well. They're available from October through to March, usually in bundles of 25 or 100. A bare-root whip at 40-60cm costs a fraction of the equivalent pot-grown plant and, given the density hawthorn needs to be planted at, the saving is significant.

Pot-grown plants can go in year-round, which matters if you're planting in spring or summer. They establish a little more slowly because the root ball is already formed and needs time to spread into the surrounding soil. Both work.

3. Spacing

Single row: 30-40cm apart, measured centre to centre. This is the standard spacing for an informal garden hedge or a garden boundary. At this density, a hawthorn hedge becomes shapeable within two or three growing seasons.

Double staggered row: for a security or wildlife hedge that needs to be thick from the base, plant two rows 30cm apart with plants in the back row sitting between two in the front, so the rows stagger. This uses more plants but produces a hedge that fills in faster at the base and has genuine stock-proofing capacity in three to four years rather than five to six.

4. Planting

For a short run (under 4-5 metres) or small plants (40-60cm bare-root whips), individual planting holes work fine. For a longer run or larger stock, a continuous trench is faster and gives more even planting depth. See our guide to trench planting for the full method.

Regardless of whether you dig holes or a trench:

  • Add well-rotted compost or manure to the backfill at a 1:2 ratio (one part compost to two parts soil)
  • Plant at the same depth the plant grew in the nursery: the soil mark on the stem should be at ground level
  • Spread the roots naturally in the hole, do not ball them up or double them under
  • Firm in well with your boot, working around the plant in a circle
  • Water thoroughly, even if the ground is already damp

5. Mulching

Apply a 7-10cm layer of bark mulch or well-rotted compost along the planting line, kept a hand's width clear of each stem. Mulching is the single most effective thing you can do for establishment in East Kent's variable soils. On the sandy soils of the coastal strip around Sandwich Bay, mulch dramatically reduces water loss in the first growing season. On clay soils around Worth and Ash, it moderates temperature and prevents capping of the soil surface.

6. Rabbit and deer protection

If rabbits or deer are present, protect each plant with a spiral guard or mesh tree shelter, or run a temporary rabbit-proof fence along the whole hedge line while the plants establish. Hawthorn's thorns are no deterrent at all until the hedge is several years old and dense enough. A rabbit can kill a bare-root whip in one sitting.

7. First-year pruning: the cut people skip

This is the step that makes the biggest difference to how quickly you get a proper hedge. Cut each plant back by roughly a third, either immediately after planting or in the first winter. The cut signals to the plant to put energy into lateral branching from the base rather than straight upward growth. A hedge planted and left unchecked for two years will typically have a sparse, leggy base and a bushy top. A hedge cut back at planting will be denser at ground level by year two, which is where it counts.

Repeat the same cut in the second winter if basal branching is still thin. After that, the hedge should be growing strongly enough for proper annual trimming.

8. Watering in year one

Hawthorn is one of the more drought-tolerant native hedging species, but new plants still benefit from attention in their first April and May. If March and April are dry (not unusual in coastal East Kent, which has a lower average rainfall than much of the UK), water the hedge thoroughly once a week during dry spells. After the first summer, an established hawthorn hedge rarely needs supplementary watering.

Hawthorn takes everything Kent throws at it. Salty coastal wind, poor drainage in winter, chalk in summer. The only thing that defeats it is a bad planting job and no aftercare in year one.

Hawthorn for wildlife

A hawthorn hedge supports over 300 insect species. The blossom (May to early June) is one of the most important early nectar sources in the calendar for bees. The berries (haws) are crucial for fieldfares, redwings and other winter thrushes passing through East Kent on migration. Dense hawthorn is the preferred nesting site for whitethroats, yellowhammers and long-tailed tits in the Kent countryside, and many of those birds will also use a garden hedge once it is established and thick.
