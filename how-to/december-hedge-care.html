December hedge care in Kent: dormant season tasks

December is quieter than the autumn rush, but it is not idle. The dormant season is the best time of year to renovate deciduous hedges hard, plant new ones cheaply, and sort out jobs you could not tackle while birds were nesting. Here is what we actually do this month across Sandwich, Deal and the villages of East Kent.

Updated September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Most people assume December is the month when hedge work stops. The main cutting rush finished in October, the garden is closing down, and it feels like a time to leave everything alone. In fact December is one of the most productive hedge-care months of the year, if you pick the right jobs.

Kent's mild coastal climate helps here. The Sandwich Bay and Deal coastline rarely sees the hard ground frosts that inland areas get in December, which extends the practical working window by several weeks compared with, say, the Vale of Kent further north. Inland villages like Woodnesborough and Eastry tend to freeze a bit earlier, but even there, December is usually workable for most of the month.

December is in the safe legal cutting window

The UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. The nesting season runs roughly 1 March to 31 August. By December, all UK resident birds have finished breeding for the year. There is no legal restriction on cutting in December, and no need to check for nests before you start.

Before you start: safety

  • Eye protection: safety glasses or goggles when using a powered trimmer, even for short sessions.
  • Ear defenders: petrol and electric trimmers both exceed safe noise levels within minutes.
  • Gloves: sturdy, close-fitting gloves. Cut-resistant for spiny species such as hawthorn, blackthorn and holly.
  • RCD adaptor: essential for any corded electric trimmer. No exceptions.
  • Ladder awareness: ground is often wet and soft in December. A long-reach pole trimmer is far safer than a ladder for anything above head height. If a ladder is unavoidable, have someone footing it and never overreach sideways.
  • Check for wasps and brambles: less relevant in December than summer, but always check before reaching into a hedge.

What to do in December: task by task

1. Holly: the last ornamental cut, and the harvest

Holly can be cut in December without any lasting harm. For formal holly hedges around Sandwich, Deal and Ash, a light tidy in early December gives a sharp finish for the winter and lets you salvage the berry-laden cuttings. Female holly with red berries will hold for three to four weeks in a cool shed, making the December cut doubly productive.

Avoid cutting holly very hard in late December if a frost is forecast within a week. The exposed cut surfaces benefit from a little time to harden, and holly cut during a hard freeze can brown at the tips. Early December, or a mild spell later in the month, is fine.

2. Yew: early December only, then stop

Yew tolerates cold better than almost any other hedging species, but it does need a few weeks after cutting for the exposed surfaces to harden before a hard frost. Cut yew in the first ten days of December and you should be fine in most Kent winters. Cut it in late December and a cold snap could leave brown patches on the new cut faces. If you missed the November window for yew, cut it early December rather than waiting until March.

3. Deciduous hedge renovation: the best time of year

Beech, hornbeam, hawthorn, blackthorn, hazel and field maple are all best renovated in the dormant season, and December is firmly within that window. With the leaves off beech and hornbeam, you can see exactly what you are doing, which matters when reducing a hedge that has grown two metres wider than it should be.

A beech hedge that has spread three metres wide over the years can be cut back to 40 cm without killing it, provided the cut is made cleanly and the root system is healthy. The usual approach on an established hedge is to cut one face hard in year one, allow it to regenerate, and cut the other face in year two. Fast-growing deciduous species such as hawthorn and blackthorn can take both faces and the top in a single winter season without drama.

December cuts on beech and hornbeam also have a secondary benefit: the dead leaves, which normally hold on the branch all winter, fall away after cutting, leaving a cleaner-looking hedge through January and February.

4. Bare-root planting: the window is open

Bare-root hedging plants are lifted from nurseries once fully dormant, generally from November onwards. December is still a good planting month in Kent as long as the ground is not frozen or waterlogged. In the sandy and free-draining soils around Sandwich and Worth, December planting works particularly well; roots can establish through the winter months and the hedge will be ahead of April-planted pot-grown stock by the time summer arrives.

The best-value native species (privet, hawthorn, beech, hornbeam, blackthorn, field maple, hazel) tend to sell out at reputable suppliers by mid-December. If you need to plant in the New Year, order before Christmas. See our guide to planting bare-root hedges for technique.

5. What not to cut in December

A few species are best left alone until spring:

  • Leylandii and most conifers: avoid any significant cut in December. Leylandii will not produce new growth to cover cut surfaces until late spring, leaving bare brown patches for months. Light tidying of stray shoots is acceptable; a proper cut is not. See our leylandii guide for the full detail.
  • Tender evergreens (pittosporum, choisya, escallonia, griselinia, phormium): leave until March at the earliest. These species can suffer tip dieback after any cut in cold weather. In East Kent, an exposed pittosporum hedge along a coastal boundary near Sandwich Bay or Deal can be particularly vulnerable to winter wind damage after cutting. Wait for settled, mild weather in late March before tidying these.
  • Laurel: best left until March. While laurel is generally tough, hard cuts in cold, wet weather increase the risk of bacterial canker entering the cut surfaces. If your laurel is in desperate need of attention, wait for a dry mild spell in late winter rather than cutting in December.
  • Rosemary and lavender hedges: leave until April. Both are frost-sensitive at the cut surfaces and should only be trimmed once growth resumes in spring.

6. Frost protection for tender hedges

If your garden has a recently planted or particularly exposed tender hedge, December is when frost protection earns its keep. A 7 to 10 cm mulch of bark chip or composted wood along the base, kept 5 cm clear of the stems, insulates the root zone and retains moisture through dry winter spells. This is particularly worth doing in the free-draining chalk and sand soils around Eastry and Ash, where the root zone can dry out and cool quickly in cold snaps.

For the top growth of particularly tender species in an exposed site, a double layer of horticultural fleece over forecast hard-frost nights (below -3°C) prevents tip scorch. Weigh the fleece down or pin it to prevent it acting as a sail in December wind.

7. Plan January and February work now

January and February are the peak months for large renovation work, and professional hedge contractors have more availability then than during the busy September-October season. If your hedge needs a major reduction, structural renovation or complete replanting, December is the time to arrange a quote and schedule the work. Leaving it until the spring rush in March will mean longer waiting times and fewer available dates.

The RHS Horticulturist Alesandra Sana covers correct cutting technique, including the importance of clean cuts and the right tool angle for different hedge types, in the RHS YouTube video What is the best way to cut a hedge? (The RHS channel, published September 2025). Worth watching before making renovation cuts on a hedge you have not cut hard before.

The dormant season is not a pause. It is the best time to do the heavy work on deciduous hedges, when the plants are resting and you can actually see what you are doing.
