Yew hedge: first three years

A year-by-year guide to establishing a young yew hedge in East Kent: why you do not hard-cut it at planting, when the first clip happens, and what realistic progress looks like.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Yew is the most rewarding hedge you can plant in East Kent. It tolerates the free-draining chalk downland around Sandwich and Deal, it cuts to a razor-sharp formal finish, and a well-established yew hedge will outlast the house. The catch is that it is slow. Getting the first three years right does not turn a slow grower into a fast one, but it does mean the hedge you eventually get is dense, well-shaped and low-maintenance. Getting those years wrong means thin, straggly growth and a hedge that takes the best part of a decade to recover.

What follows is the programme we use on the yew hedges we look after across the villages between Sandwich, Worth and Eastry. It applies to Taxus baccata planted as small root-balled specimens or bare-root whips.

Nesting season: 1 March to 31 August

Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 it is an offence to damage or destroy a nest that is in active use. Yew is a popular nesting shrub. Check carefully from underneath before any cut between March and August. If a nest is occupied, leave the hedge alone until the birds have gone. The safe cutting windows are late August through September and any dry day in winter.

Year 1: establishing the roots, not the shape

The single most common mistake with a newly planted yew hedge is treating it like a hornbeam or hawthorn: cutting it back hard at planting to encourage a bushy base. For deciduous species, that works. For yew, it does not. Yew establishes slowly, and hard cutting in the first year diverts the plant's resources away from root development into regrowth at a time when it needs to put down roots above everything else. Leave the leaders alone.

At planting, remove any physically damaged or very untidy shoots with secateurs. That is all. Then plant at the spacing appropriate to the eventual height you want: for a 1.5-metre formal hedge, one plant every 45 to 60 cm in a single row is right. For taller or quicker density, go to 30 to 40 cm spacing.

Watering in the first summer is the job that matters. The chalk downland around Sandwich Bay and Deal dries out quickly in June and July, and a newly planted yew in its first summer has a small root ball and very little drought tolerance. Water once a week in dry spells, aiming for a good soak at the base rather than a surface sprinkle. Apply a 7 to 10 cm mulch of composted bark or wood chip around the base, keeping it clear of the stems, to hold moisture and suppress weeds. Do not cut the hedge at all during year one. Leave it completely alone between March and late August. If you want to tidy a stray shoot in autumn, use secateurs, not shears.

Year 2: the first clip

By late August of year two, a well-watered yew hedge will have put on a full season of new growth on the sides. This is the moment for the first proper clip. Use hedge shears or a hedge trimmer set to a light pass, taking off no more than 5 to 8 cm from the sides. Do not touch the top. The goal at this stage is to stimulate lateral branching on the lower half of the hedge, not to impose a shape. One pass in late August is enough for year two.

If the weather has been particularly dry and the hedge looks stressed, yellowing at the tips or showing very limited new growth, hold off. A yew hedge under drought stress does not benefit from a clip. Water it, mulch it again, and wait until it is visibly growing before you cut it.

By the end of year two, the hedge should be visibly fuller than it was at planting, with new side growth beginning to thicken. It will not look like a hedge yet. That is fine.

Year 3: beginning to impose a shape

In year three you can start training the profile. Yew cuts best with a slight batter, wider at the base than the top. This matters for yew more than for many other species because the dense canopy of a mature yew shades the lower branches heavily, and once the lower face starts to thin, it is very difficult to recover. Building the batter from year three onwards prevents this problem before it starts.

Two cuts in year three: a light side clip in late August, working the slight batter angle in with hedge shears or a trimmer angled slightly inward. Then, if the top leaders have reached the target height or close to it, you can tip them in autumn. If they have not reached the target height yet, leave the top alone for another season. Do not rush the height. A yew hedge that reaches its target height in year five with a dense, well-trained base is better than one that reaches it in year three with a bare lower section.

Kent's coastal winters are mild enough that late cuts in October and November do no harm to established yew, so there is a reasonable window to get the second year-three cut done before Christmas if August timing does not work out.

Common questions

Should I cut a yew hedge back hard when I plant it?

No. Unlike hawthorn or hornbeam, yew should not be cut back hard at planting. Remove any damaged or very straggly shoots, but leave the main leaders alone. Yew establishes slowly and cutting it back severely at planting delays rather than encourages dense growth.

When should I give a young yew hedge its first clip?

Wait until late August or early September of the second growing season after planting. By then the roots will have had over a year to establish. Take a light pass over the sides only, removing no more than 5 to 8 cm. Leave the top alone until year three.

How much will a young yew hedge grow each year?

Expect 10 to 20 cm of new growth per year on well-watered, established plants. On the chalk downland around Sandwich and Deal, growth tends toward the lower end until the roots reach below the dry chalk horizon. With a good mulch and regular watering in the first two summers, 15 cm per year is realistic.

How long before a yew hedge looks properly established?

Most yew hedges planted as small root-balled plants or bare-root whips look presentable by the end of year four or five, and dense and formal by year seven to ten. This sounds discouraging, but the hedge you get after a decade of steady training is worth the wait. Getting the first three years right matters.
