Bat-friendly hedges in Kent: species and management compared

Bats use hedges as navigation routes, hunting grounds and in some cases roosting habitat. Whether your hedge is a mixed native boundary or a formal yew row, there are practical things you can do to make it more useful for the bats flying through your garden each night.

Updated September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Around 14 bat species breed in the UK. In East Kent, the common pipistrelle is by far the most widespread, but noctules, brown long-eared bats and soprano pipistrelles are all recorded in the Sandwich, Deal and Worth area. All of them depend on linear landscape features, including hedges, to navigate and find food in the dark.

Echolocation lets bats detect insects, but it does not give them a map of the landscape. They learn routes by flying them repeatedly, using linear features such as hedgerows, tree lines, road verges and garden boundaries as guides. Removing or fragmenting a hedge does not just destroy one hedge; it can remove a navigation route used by local bats every night for years.

What bats actually use a hedge for

Understanding what bats want from a hedge makes the management choices clearer. There are three distinct uses:

  • Navigation: bats fly along hedge lines to travel between roosts and feeding areas. A continuous, unbroken hedge is more useful than a gappy one. Height matters too: bats generally fly just above or within 1 to 2 metres of the hedge top when using it as a navigation line. A hedge reduced to 60 cm stumps provides far less guidance than a 2-metre hedge with a defined top.
  • Foraging: moths, flies, beetles and other insects are abundant in the zone immediately above and around a hedge, especially where the canopy overhangs long grass or wildflowers at the base. Pipistrelles hunt in the hedge shadow zone at dusk, picking off moths and midges flying in the sheltered, insect-rich air. The insects depend on the plants in the hedge, so a diverse native hedge produces more prey than a monoculture of laurel.
  • Roosting: most garden bats roost in buildings, but mature hedges, especially those with ivy cover, can provide day-roosts for some species. Brown long-eared bats and soprano pipistrelles occasionally roost in dense ivy growing on a hedge or wall. Stripping ivy from hedges removes this potential.

Comparing hedge types for bat value

The three main hedge categories in East Kent gardens differ markedly in their value to bats.

Native mixed hedge (hawthorn, blackthorn, hazel, dog rose, elder, field maple)

Navigation value: high. A mixed native hedge maintained at 1.5 to 2+ metres provides a clear, consistent flight line for bats. The uneven profile, varying slightly in height between species, creates a natural-looking feature that bats can follow reliably.

Foraging value: very high. The range of plants in a mixed native hedge supports a far greater insect community than any single-species hedge. Hawthorn alone supports around 150 invertebrate species. Elder and dog rose add further insects and moths. The result is an insect-rich aerial zone above the hedge from dusk onwards that concentrates bat foraging.

Roosting value: moderate. Mature, dense sections of a native mixed hedge, particularly where ivy has established, offer occasional roosting opportunities for long-eared bats and pipistrelles.

Management impact: cutting the hedge during the day in late August to October does not directly disturb bats (who are roosting elsewhere during daylight). Evening cutting sessions should be avoided during the active bat season (April to October), particularly with noisy petrol equipment. Maintaining the hedge at a consistent height of 1.5 metres or more preserves the navigation line.

Formal evergreen hedge (yew, beech, hornbeam, privet, laurel)

Navigation value: high. A well-maintained formal hedge provides a particularly clear and reliable navigation line because the height and profile are consistent. Yew and beech hedges clipped to a formal shape are among the best navigation aids for bats in terms of linear clarity.

Foraging value: low to moderate. Monoculture evergreen hedges support fewer insects than mixed native hedges, so the hunting above them is less productive. Privet is the exception: it produces heavily scented flowers in June and July that attract moths and other insects, making a privet hedge significantly more bat-friendly than laurel or leylandii.

Roosting value: low for most species (unless ivy is present). A formally clipped yew or beech hedge offers little roosting habitat unless sections are left uncut and develop internal density.

Management impact: the navigation line is what matters most here. Maintain height. Avoid reducing a formal hedge to a low box; if it must be reduced, do it gradually over several years rather than in one hard cut that temporarily removes the flight line.

Ornamental hedge (photinia, griselinia, escallonia, pittosporum)

Navigation value: moderate. Ornamental hedges provide a navigable line, but their less structured profile and often lower height (particularly young plantings in new-build gardens around Deal, Worth and Ash) make them less reliable as bat navigation routes than mature native or formal hedges.

Foraging value: variable. Escallonia is notably good, producing insect-attracting flowers through the summer. Griselinia and pittosporum provide limited forage directly but add to the linear feature. Photinia has modest flowering value.

Roosting value: low. Most ornamental species are cut too regularly and grown too formally to provide roosting opportunities.

Management impact: maintaining height is the single most useful thing. A 1.2-metre photinia hedge is more useful to bats than a 60-cm hedge of the same species.

Practical changes that help bats

These apply to any hedge type and require no additional planting or major changes:

  • Maintain height: a hedge of 1.5 metres or more provides a much better navigation line than a low box. If the hedge must be reduced, take it in stages over two or three years rather than all at once.
  • Leave the base uncut: a strip of long grass and wildflowers along the hedge base (30 to 60 cm wide, cut once a year in late autumn) generates the insect abundance that makes the hedge a productive hunting ground. Without it, even a good native hedge produces far fewer foraging opportunities.
  • Avoid evening cutting in summer: bats begin foraging from around sunset. Running a petrol hedge trimmer at 9 pm in July disturbs foraging bats and, more practically, produces poor visibility for safe working. Daytime cutting is both safer and less disruptive.
  • Do not strip ivy from mature sections: ivy is one of the most valuable wildlife plants in a garden, offering roosting sites for bats and insects, late-season nectar for ivy bees, and winter berries for birds. A wall or hedge section covered in mature ivy in the Sandwich Bay and Deal coast area may be in use as a bat day-roost; if in doubt, check with the Bat Conservation Trust before cutting it back.
  • Keep gaps to a minimum: where a hedge has died back or been removed, bats lose that section of their navigation route. Replanting gaps promptly, or filling them with a native species, restores the linear continuity that matters.
  • Connect hedges where possible: a garden hedge that connects to a neighbour's hedge, a road verge planting or a field boundary extends the navigation route available to local bats. Even a small linking strip of hedge or tall planting between two existing hedges makes a difference.

HedgeLink, the UK's hedgerow conservation partnership, has produced guidance on managing hedgerows for wildlife including bats. The talk Life in the hedge: how to manage hedgerows for wildlife by conservation ecologists Rob Wolton and Nigel Adams covers the ecology of hedgerows and practical management principles in detail.

Are bats legally protected?

Yes. All UK bat species are protected under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 and the Conservation of Habitats and Species Regulations 2017. It is an offence to deliberately disturb, injure or kill a bat, or to damage, destroy or obstruct access to a bat roost, whether or not bats are present at the time. If you think a hedge or wall you are planning to cut may contain a bat roost, contact the Bat Conservation Trust helpline before proceeding. In practice, most garden hedges are not roost sites, but mature ivy-covered sections, old hollow stems and dense tangled growth are worth checking.
