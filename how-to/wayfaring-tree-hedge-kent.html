Wayfaring tree in a Kent hedge: how Viburnum lantana compares

An underused native that has been growing in Kent chalk-land hedgerows for centuries. Here is how it stacks up against the more commonly planted alternatives.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

If you drive the lanes around Worth, Ash, Woodnesborough, or Eastry in May, you will see it without necessarily knowing its name: creamy flat-topped flower clusters, thick woolly leaves, growing in the scrubby hedgerows on the chalk banks. That is the wayfaring tree, Viburnum lantana, and it has been there longer than any of us.

It is native to chalk downland across south-east England. Kent hedgerows are one of its heartlands. The berries go from green to red to near-black through summer and autumn, and while they are not edible for people, blackbirds and other thrushes take them. The thick woolly leaves are good at catching dust on lane-side hedges. And crucially for East Kent: it tolerates dry chalk conditions better than almost any competing native shrub.

The question is usually whether to include it in a mixed native hedge, and how it compares to the other species you might choose for the same slot.

How four native chalk-soil hedge species compare

The table below covers four species commonly used in native hedge mixes on the chalk and loam soils of East Kent. The conditions around Sandwich, Deal, and Worth are relevant here: reasonably mild coastal winters, free-draining chalk in many spots, and occasional summer drought.

Species Height Growth rate Drought tolerance Wildlife value Pruning Best for
Viburnum lantana (wayfaring tree) 3-5m Medium Very good Medium-high Tolerant Dry chalk banks, exposed sites
Viburnum opulus (guelder rose) 2-4m Medium Poor High Moderate Moist clay/loam, NOT chalk
Acer campestre (field maple) 4-7m Medium-fast Good High Very tolerant Most Kent soils
Rosa canina (dog rose) 2-3m Fast Good Very high Tolerant (wear gloves) Mixed native hedge filler

When to choose each

The two viburnums are regularly confused, and for good reason: both are native, both have white flower clusters in spring, and both produce berries that birds eat. The difference is in the soil. Guelder rose (Viburnum opulus) is a plant of river margins and damp woodland edges. Put it on free-draining chalk and it will sulk. The wayfaring tree is the opposite: chalk and limestone downland is its natural habitat. If you have the kind of well-drained soil typical of the lanes between Ash and Worth, the wayfaring tree will thrive where guelder rose would fail.

Field maple is an easy choice for most Kent soils: it grows well almost everywhere, tolerates hard cuts, turns a good yellow in autumn, and has high insect value. If you want a workhorse species to mix with hawthorn and blackthorn, field maple is reliable. Dog rose is the best all-round wildlife plant of the four: caterpillars, aphids that feed other insects, hips for birds through winter, and dense thorny growth that makes the hedge impenetrable. Its one drawback is that it is prickly to manage. Use it and accept that.

Wayfaring tree fills a specific niche: dry chalk banks, exposed lane-side positions, and places where you want height without the weight of a full field maple. For a mixed hedge on the downland soils east of Sandwich, mixing wayfaring tree with hawthorn, blackthorn, and field maple gives you a genuinely diverse hedge that reflects what was there before.

Planting notes

Plant wayfaring tree as part of a mixed native hedge rather than as a monoculture. It is an infill species rather than a primary hedging plant. Pair it with hawthorn (Crataegus monogyna), blackthorn (Prunus spinosa), and field maple (Acer campestre) as the structural backbone, and add wayfaring tree, dog rose, and other species as infill every few metres.

Plant October to March as bare-root whips, spaced 30-45cm in a staggered double row. No special soil preparation is needed on chalk: wayfaring tree does not want fertiliser or compost, it wants the lean soil it evolved in. Water in after planting and mulch to suppress weeds in year one.

Pruning

Viburnum lantana tolerates hard cuts and will re-shoot from old wood. Trim as part of the wider native hedge mix in September to February, outside the bird nesting season. One cut a year keeps it at hedge height. If it gets away and reaches the top of the hedge, a hard cut back in winter recovers it without lasting damage.

Nesting season: check before you cut

Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs roughly from 1 March to 31 August. Native mixed hedges are actively used as nesting sites by blackbirds, robins, wrens and others. The safe cutting window is September to February. A light summer trim is only acceptable once you have checked the hedge is demonstrably empty.
