June hedge care in Kent: the summer trim window opens

June is the first month in the year when a section of the hedge trimming population in East Kent can legitimately pick up the shears. Not all hedges, and not without checking first. Here is the checklist.

Updated September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

June hedge care: your checklist

  1. Check for nests first on every hedge you plan to touch. The nesting season runs to 31 August.
  2. Light trim on fast growers (privet, box, photinia, lonicera nitida) once confirmed empty, from mid-June.
  3. Leylandii and conifers: tidy up new growth if a first trim was done in April or May.
  4. Leave alone: hawthorn, laurel, blackthorn, holly, yew, beech, hornbeam. September is their month.
  5. Water new hedges weekly if June is dry: East Kent can go two to three weeks without useful rain in a typical June.
  6. Do not go hard on shape: a light tidy now, the main cut in September or October.
  7. Have an RCD adaptor for any corded electric trimmer. Non-negotiable.

Still nesting season in June

Under the UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, damaging or destroying an active bird's nest is a criminal offence. The season runs roughly 1 March to 31 August. In June, second-brood nesting is common across East Kent: blackbirds, pigeons and house sparrows all regularly raise two or more broods. Do not assume a hedge is clear without checking. A hedge you trimmed in May may have been reoccupied by June.

Privet: the June trim in detail

Privet is the most common hedge in East Kent domestic gardens and the species most likely to benefit from a June tidy. After a full winter and spring of growth, privet can put on 30 to 40 cm of new shoots between March and June. A light trim in mid-June keeps the hedge compact, encourages branching, and prevents the leggy, open look that develops if it is left untouched until September.

Check the hedge first. Privet provides good nesting cover and is commonly used by blackbirds and house sparrows. Stand back and watch for two minutes: if birds are actively flying in and out of the same area, leave that section for another few weeks. In a mild East Kent year, many first-brood privet nesters have fledged by mid-June, but second broods are possible and the obligation to check is unchanged.

Before you start trimming in June: safety

  • Check for nests: two to three minutes of observation before you touch the hedge. Check from both sides and from underneath.
  • Eye protection: safety glasses or goggles. Hedge clippings at trimmer speed can cause serious eye injuries.
  • Ear defenders: petrol and electric trimmers both exceed safe noise levels within minutes.
  • Gloves: cut-resistant, close-fitting. Privet thorns are small but sharp, and a trimmer can flick debris at the hands.
  • RCD adaptor: for corded electric trimmers. The garden is damp in June: no exceptions.
  • Pole trimmer over a ladder: if the hedge is taller than you can safely reach from the ground, a long-reach pole trimmer is safer. If a ladder is necessary, have someone footing it and do not overreach sideways.
  • Check for wasp nests: June is when wasp nests are established in hedge bases and inside dense growth. Look before you cut, and if you see a nest, leave that section alone.

For the cut itself: a light trim means taking off the new season's growth at the tips, leaving the shape broadly as it was. You are not reducing the height or changing the profile, that is September's job. Use a hedge trimmer working from the bottom up on the faces and from front to back on the top to keep the cut even. A practical video showing a large privet reduction using hand tools, published in May 2022, is available on YouTube: How to Prune a large Privet Hedge Reduction with Hand Tools. While a full reduction is a different operation to a light June tidy, the video illustrates the correct approach to the cut face and the attention to straight lines that separates a clean hedge from a ragged one.

Box: first cut of the year

Late May or June is the standard first cut for box hedging and formal box topiary. Box grows slowly relative to privet and one or two cuts a year is usually enough. A June trim tidies up the new growth that appeared in spring and sets the shape for the rest of the season. If you have not fed your box yet this year, do it after the cut rather than before: freshly trimmed box takes up a liquid feed particularly well. Watch for box blight when you cut: do not trim in wet weather, as water on the shears spreads the fungal spores. Wipe the shears with a disinfectant solution between any box plants showing symptoms.

Leylandii and conifers in June

If you gave your leylandii a first trim in April or May, June is the point to assess whether a light tidy is needed. Fast-growing leylandii can put on 30 to 60 cm in a single season. A June tidy-up of any sections that look uneven is fine, subject to the usual nest check. Avoid going harder than the light tidy at this point: the proper formal cut is still late summer. Our conifer trimming guide has the full detail on staying inside the green and managing conifer growth across the season.

What to leave alone in June

Hawthorn, blackthorn, laurel, cherry laurel, holly, yew, beech and hornbeam should all be left alone in June. They are either actively nesting (hawthorn, blackthorn, laurel) or simply not ready for their once-a-year cut, which belongs in August or September. Cutting them in June gains nothing and risks a great deal. The mild winters and warm early springs of coastal East Kent mean these species are growing strongly in June and will benefit more from the late-summer cut than from an early intervention.

If the hedge is overhanging a path and is genuinely causing a problem, a minimal tidy of the offending section is not an offence if you have confirmed it is empty. But confirm it first.

Watering: important in June

East Kent can be genuinely dry in June. The soils around Deal, Worth and the coastal strip drain quickly and recently planted hedges can suffer real moisture stress in a two-week dry spell. Water deeply and slowly at the base, twice a week if there has been no useful rain. Established hedges in healthy soil do not need irrigation, but anything planted in the last two years does. The mulch you laid in March or April should be earning its keep now by retaining whatever moisture is available.
