Field maple hedge care in Kent

Britain's only native maple is one of the most overlooked hedging plants. It tolerates chalk, clay and shade, blazes orange in autumn and supports more wildlife than most people realise. Here is how to grow and manage it well.

Updated September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Field maple (Acer campestre) is the only maple native to Britain. Drive any lane between Sandwich and Worth in October and you will spot it immediately: golden-yellow leaves catching the low autumn sun, the roadsides lit up where the old field boundaries still stand. As a hedging plant it is genuinely excellent, yet it rarely appears in garden centre advice because it is less showy than photinia or griselinia and less fashionable than beech.

That is a shame. It is tolerant of almost any soil, grows densely when trimmed correctly, produces a modest but reliable flower for early pollinators, and its winged seeds (samaras) are devoured by birds through winter. On our local chalk downland behind Sandwich and Deal it thrives. On the heavy clay around Ash, Woodnesborough and Worth it does just as well. It even handles partial shade better than most deciduous hedging plants.

If you have one, or you are thinking of planting one, here is what you need to know.

Before you start: safety and the law

PPE: Eye protection against flying debris is essential when using any powered trimmer. Wear ear defenders with petrol or electric hedge trimmers, and sturdy gloves. Check for wasp nests by slowly walking the hedge beforehand, particularly at the base and in any dense internal growth.

Electric trimmers: Always use an RCD (residual current device) on the circuit and keep the cable behind you at all times. If possible, prefer a battery or petrol trimmer to eliminate the cable hazard. For hedges over chest height, a pole/long-reach trimmer is safer than a ladder. If a ladder is unavoidable, have someone footing it and never overreach sideways.

Nesting season (Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981): It is an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs roughly from 1 March to 31 August. The safe trimming window for most hedges is September to February. A summer trim is only acceptable if the hedge is demonstrably free of active nests, checked visually before work begins.

Step 1: know your tree

Field maple in a hedge grows quite differently from a field maple left as a standard tree. Hedged plants branch low and densely. The lobed, five-pointed leaves are smaller than in the hedge than in an open-grown tree. In spring the leaves emerge with a reddish tinge before settling to mid-green through summer. Come October they shift to warm yellow, gold and occasionally orange, depending on the season and the individual plant. On chalk soils the autumn colour tends to be richer.

Left completely uncut, field maple will eventually reach four to six metres as a hedging plant. A well-managed hedge can be kept at any height from one to three metres without stress to the plant.

Step 2: soil and site

Field maple is not fussy. It grows on chalk, clay, sandy loam and most soils in between. It handles slightly acidic to quite alkaline conditions. The one thing it dislikes is waterlogged ground. If your plot stays wet after heavy rain, consider improving drainage before planting or choose a different species for that section.

It tolerates partial shade better than most deciduous hedging plants, which makes it a practical choice for boundaries with a northerly aspect or sections shaded by a building. Full sun is still preferred for the best autumn colour and the densest growth.

In East Kent specifically, field maple performs particularly well on the chalk downland that lies behind Sandwich and Deal, and on the clay soils in the farming villages of Ash, Woodnesborough and Eastry. It is already a component of many old hedgerows in this area, often mixed with hawthorn, blackthorn and hazel.

Step 3: planting

Bare-root field maple is available from October to March and is the most economical way to plant. Use pot-grown stock if you need to plant outside that window. Space plants at 2 to 3 per metre for a single row, or 4 to 5 per metre for a double-staggered row. A double row gives a denser result but takes more plants and needs a wider strip.

After planting, cut the whips back by a third immediately. This feels brutal but encourages basal branching from the outset, which is how you get a dense hedge from the bottom rather than one that is all legs.

Water well in the first spring and summer, particularly in dry spells. Apply a 5 to 8cm layer of bark mulch along the base, kept clear of the stems, to hold moisture and suppress weeds. Bare-root plants are cheaper but do need that first-season irrigation to establish properly.

Step 4: the first two years

Year one is about establishment, not shape. Let it grow. The only cut needed in the first dormant season (January to February) is to tip the leading shoots back by a quarter, which encourages lateral branching. Do not attempt to create a flat-topped formal hedge in year one.

In year two, the side shoots will have started to branch. A light trim in July or August, taking only the current season's growth, starts to define the shape. Keep the sides slightly angled, wider at the base than the top. That taper allows light to reach the lower growth and prevents the classic problem of a hedge that is dense at the top and bare-legged at the bottom.

Step 5: annual management of an established hedge

An established field maple hedge needs one main cut per year, in July or August. You can add a second, lighter tidy in October if you want a very neat finish through winter.

Do not cut in spring (March to May) if you can avoid it. Acer species bleed sap from fresh cuts when the sap is rising. It is not fatal to the plant, but it is unsightly, and the sap can attract secondary problems. Summer and autumn cuts, when sap flow has slowed, are much cleaner.

Use a hedge trimmer for long straight sections. For formal shapes with very large leaves (though field maple has modest-sized leaves compared to laurel), a final tidy with hand shears gives a cleaner finish than a trimmer alone. For any section above comfortable arm height, a pole/long-reach hedge trimmer eliminates the need for a ladder.

After cutting, take the clippings away rather than leaving them in situ, particularly if tar spot fungus has been present on the foliage (see below).

Step 6: renovation

An old, overgrown field maple hedge can be cut back hard. Unlike most conifers, field maple regenerates reliably from old wood. If the hedge has become very wide and the centre is all dead wood, cut one face back to within 20 to 30cm of the main stems in winter (January to February, while fully dormant). Leave the other face for one to two years before cutting it back similarly. This staged approach reduces the stress and maintains some screening through the renovation period.

After any hard cut, feed the base with a balanced slow-release fertiliser in spring, water well through the first summer, and give the new growth a year before you start trimming it back to the desired shape again. Most renovated hedges are back to a presentable shape within two to three seasons.

For significant reductions (over a metre off the height or width), a qualified contractor is the safer option. If the hedge is over two metres tall, see our hedge height and neighbour law guide for what the High Hedges legislation says.

Wildlife value

Field maple punches well above its weight for wildlife. The spring flowers, small and yellowish-green, open in April and May along with the young leaves. They are an excellent early pollen and nectar source for bees and hoverflies before other hedging plants flower. The foliage supports more than 50 species of invertebrate, including several moth species whose caterpillars feed specifically on maple leaves.

The paired winged seeds (samaras) ripen in October and are eagerly taken by finches, particularly chaffinches, as well as wood mice, voles and grey squirrels through winter. Dense twiggy growth throughout the year provides good nesting cover for warblers, finches and dunnocks. In rural East Kent, field maple hedgerows are known feeding territory for yellowhammer and bullfinch.

Common problems

Tar spot fungus

Black, irregularly shaped spots on the upper leaf surface from mid-summer are tar spot fungus (Rhytisma acerinum). It looks alarming but is purely cosmetic and does not harm the plant. The fungus overwinters on fallen leaves, so raking up and composting (or removing) fallen leaves reduces the load the following year. There is no need for treatment.

Powdery mildew

A white powdery coating on young leaves in late summer, particularly in dry conditions, is powdery mildew. Again, cosmetic rather than serious. Improving air circulation around the base of the hedge and watering the roots during dry spells reduces the incidence.

Slow growth in the first season

Bare-root plants can take a full growing season to show significant top growth while their roots establish. If the plant looks alive (the buds break and leaves emerge, even if slowly) but is not putting on much extension, patience and a mulch are the right response. Feed lightly with a slow-release balanced fertiliser in spring of year two.
