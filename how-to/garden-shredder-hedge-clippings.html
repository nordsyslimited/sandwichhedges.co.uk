Garden shredder for hedge clippings: drum, impeller or straight to the compost heap?

Three ways to deal with the pile of clippings after a hedge cut, compared honestly: what each handles well, what each costs in money and time, and which makes sense for different garden sizes in East Kent.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

After the hedge is cut, there is a pile. What happens to that pile is where most people's hedge-care planning runs out. The clippings from a 20-metre privet hedge fill three wheelbarrows; from a leylandii boundary, double that. You can compost them, shred and mulch them, or get them collected, and the right choice depends less on what the gardening magazines say and more on how much hedge you have and whether you have anywhere to put the result.

Here is a straightforward comparison of the three main approaches, based on what we see in gardens across Sandwich, Deal, Worth and Woodnesborough.

Shredder safety

A garden shredder runs a fast-moving blade or drum. Always wear safety glasses (flying chips travel 2-3 metres), ear defenders (even electric models exceed 90dB), and thick gloves. Feed material from above into the hopper and never push clippings down with your hands: use the supplied tamping stick. Keep children and bystanders 5 metres back. Unplug or kill the engine before clearing a jam, and wait for the mechanism to stop completely before reaching into the hopper. Work in dry conditions: wet clippings block both drum and impeller shredders and dramatically increase the risk of a jam.

Electric drum shredder: the quiet workhorse

A drum shredder passes stems between a fixed blade and a rotating drum. It works best on the kind of growth you get from an annual privet, beech or box cut: bendy, relatively thin, with nothing more than 30-35mm across. Feed it steadily and it ticks through clippings quietly enough that neighbours a garden away can hold a conversation.

The limitations are real. A drum shredder jams on thick, tough stems: the thick main runners of an old leylandii, a thick laurel bough, anything much over 35mm. It also slows dramatically with wet, sappy material. If you have a large leylandii boundary or run into an old yew or hawthorn stem, the drum shredder is not the right tool for those bits, set them aside for a different disposal route.

For a typical Sandwich, Deal or Ash garden with a 15-25 metre privet or beech hedge cut once or twice a year, a mid-range electric drum shredder is probably all you need. The output, dry shredded material, can go into the compost heap or spread lightly around shrubs.

Petrol impeller shredder: fast and thorough, but loud

An impeller shredder uses high-speed rotating hammers or blades to break up material. It handles woody stems that would jam a drum shredder: leylandii side growth, thick laurel stems, even substantial hawthorn and blackthorn branches up to 40-50mm. It works much faster than a drum shredder on the same volume of material.

The trade-off is noise, cost and size. A petrol impeller shredder produces 95-105 decibels: that is above the level at which hearing damage begins with prolonged exposure, and it is not something most suburban gardens in Woodnesborough or Eastry can run for an extended Saturday afternoon without neighbourly friction. It is also large and heavy to store, and requires petrol, oil and periodic blade maintenance.

If you manage a long leylandii or laurel boundary of 50 metres or more, the impeller shredder's speed and capacity will save hours compared with bagging everything for green waste. For most East Kent gardens, it is more machine than the job requires.

Straight composting and green waste: the no-kit option

Most households in East Kent receive a brown-lidded garden waste bin collection from the council. Hedge clippings go in without shredding, collection is weekly from spring through autumn and fortnightly in winter. For small hedges, this is the most sensible option: no equipment to buy, no noise, no jam to clear.

The compost heap is the other route, but raw hedge clippings compost slowly without shredding. A layer of privet clippings 10cm thick will still be identifiable two years later. If you want unshredded clippings to compost, chop them roughly with hand shears, mix them with green material (grass clippings, kitchen waste) and be patient. Alternatively, run a hired shredder for a morning once a year: tool hire companies in the Deal and Canterbury areas typically charge £40-£60 per day for a petrol impeller shredder, which covers any volume a domestic hedge produces.

The yew exception

Yew clippings are toxic to horses, dogs and humans. Do not put them in a home compost heap, do not shred them and spread the output as mulch, and do not leave them where livestock or pets can reach them. Yew clippings go into the council garden waste bin (the council's commercial composting process handles toxic material safely) or to a household waste recycling centre. This applies regardless of which shredder approach you use for everything else.

What to do with shredded output

Shredded privet, beech, box and hornbeam clippings make reasonable mulch: spread 3-5cm around shrubs and trees to suppress weeds and retain moisture. Leave shredded leylandii clippings to dry for 2-3 weeks before using them as mulch, as fresh leylandii oils can inhibit neighbouring plants. Mix woody shredded material with green compostable material in the compost heap to avoid a solid woody mass that will not break down.
