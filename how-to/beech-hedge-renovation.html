Renovating an overgrown beech hedge

A beech hedge that's doubled in width and lost its shape can be brought back. It won't look right immediately, but beech is one of the most renovation-tolerant hedging plants there is. Here's the checklist and the two-year plan.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Beech (Fagus sylvatica) is one of the great formal hedging plants of the British countryside. It's also one of the most forgiving when neglected. Cut it hard during dormancy and it pushes new growth from the old wood, denser and more vigorous than the spindly growth it had before. We see this in East Kent gardens every February: a hedge that looked embarrassingly wide and gappy by September, tight and presentable the following summer.

The key is timing, staging the work correctly, and knowing what to expect. Here's the full checklist first, then the detail.

Renovation checklist

Before you do anything: check the nesting calendar

Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, damaging or destroying an active bird's nest is an offence. Nesting season runs 1 March to 31 August. Beech hedge renovation must be completed before the end of February, or held over to September at the absolute earliest, only after checking the hedge is empty. For renovation work (not light trimming), the dormant January-to-February window is strongly preferred.

  1. Confirm the hedge is dormant and leafless. January to late February is the window.
  2. Cut one side only in year one. Never both sides in the same season.
  3. Reduce to 15–20cm of live growth on the face being renovated. Leave enough shoots to photosynthesise.
  4. Leave the height alone in year one unless it's genuinely unmanageable. Tackle height in year two once one face has recovered.
  5. Remove debris thoroughly. Beech leaf litter left against the base can harbour fungal problems.
  6. Apply mulch along the base after renovation to retain moisture during the summer recovery push.
  7. Return to normal annual trimming from year three. The hedge will be denser than before.

Before you start: PPE and tools

Renovation work produces a large volume of woody material. You'll want a petrol or battery hedging trimmer, loppers for stems over 2.5cm, and a bow saw for anything thicker than a thumb. Eye protection is essential: renovation cuts produce more flying debris than a light trim. Ear defenders if you're using a petrol machine. Sturdy leather gloves throughout.

Have a groundsheet or tarpaulin to catch clippings on the other side of the hedge. Beech renovation debris is bulky and difficult to clear off a lawn.

Year one: one side hard

Stand back from the hedge and look at it. The worst side, usually the one that's encroached into a path or lawn, is what you tackle first. Cut this face back hard, leaving no more than 15–20cm of soft growth on the main woody framework. For a hedge that's grown 60cm too wide, this is a significant reduction. It will look severe. That is correct.

Beech will push new buds from dormant growth on the old wood, provided there is some live growth remaining on the cut face. The side you haven't touched carries on photosynthesising normally and feeds the root system through the growing season. This is why you don't cut both sides at once.

The untouched side gets its normal annual cut in late July or August as usual.

Year two: the other side and optionally the height

By year two, the renovated face should be showing dense new growth. It won't look fully recovered yet, but the new shoots will be there. Now you tackle the second face with the same approach: cut hard during dormancy, leaving 15–20cm of live growth.

Height reduction can also be done in year two if necessary. Cut the top down to slightly below the target finished height. Beech regrows at the top fairly quickly once the sides are under control. For very tall hedges in the Deal and Eastry area, we sometimes stage the height reduction into year three to avoid stressing the plant too much at once.

What to expect during recovery

In the first summer after a hard side cut, the renovated face will produce a mass of thin, whippy new shoots. It looks untidy. Leave them. By the second summer these shoots are thickening up and you can begin lightly trimming them into shape. By year three the hedge face is typically dense enough to trim normally.

The winter skeleton-leaf effect that makes beech hedges distinctive, the coppery retention of dead leaves through winter, is not affected by renovation. It resumes as soon as the hedge returns to its regular trim cycle.
