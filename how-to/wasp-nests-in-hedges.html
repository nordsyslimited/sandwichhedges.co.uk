Wasp nests in hedges: what to do and when it is safe to trim

Finding a wasp nest after the trimmer has already started is not a great afternoon. Here is how to check before you cut, what to do if you find one, and when the nest will die off on its own.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Wasps nest in hedges more often than most people realise, particularly in dense, established evergreens like privet, leylandii and laurel. The nest is hidden inside the canopy, invisible from the outside, and perfectly quiet until you run a trimmer two inches from it.

We have found nests in Sandwich gardens, in Worth, in Eastry, and in hedges all across East Kent. They are not dangerous if you leave them alone. They are very unpleasant if you do not.

Do not trim a hedge with a live wasp nest in it

Vibration from a hedge trimmer triggers an immediate defensive response from the whole colony. You will be stung multiple times before you can get away. Always check before you cut, and if you find a live nest, stop. Come back after October or call a pest controller.

Step 1: Check before you cut

This takes two minutes and should be done before any hedge trim between May and October. Stand a few metres back from the hedge and watch. You are looking for steady, purposeful wasp traffic: wasps departing from a single point in short bursts and returning to the same spot. This is different from individual wasps foraging around the hedge, which is harmless.

Do this in the morning when it is cooler and the wasps are less active. Walk slowly along the base of the hedge looking into it from underneath. A nest inside a hedge is often visible as a grey papery ball, 20-40cm across, attached to a branch or built around a gap in the canopy. It looks a little like scrunched newspaper.

Also listen. A large nest produces a low, continuous hum.

Step 2: If you find a live nest, your options

Option A: Leave it until late October or November

This is the easiest and cheapest option. Wasp colonies die off naturally as temperatures drop in autumn. The queen stops laying in late summer, the workers dwindle and by late October to November in Kent, most nests are inactive. You can then trim safely. The nest itself breaks down over winter and is never reused by wasps the following year, so there is nothing to remove.

Option B: Call a pest controller

If the nest is causing a genuine hazard (near a children's play area, blocking access, next to an entrance), a professional pest controller can treat it with insecticide powder. They apply it at the nest entrance; the workers carry it in and the colony dies within 24-48 hours. The cost is typically £50-£90 depending on accessibility and location. After treatment, wait two to three days before trimming to be sure the activity has stopped.

Option C: Treat it yourself

Proprietary wasp nest treatments are sold at garden centres and hardware shops. They work the same way as professional treatment: powder or foam applied at the entrance, ideally at dusk when most workers are inside. The risks are obvious: getting too close to a live nest without professional training or protective clothing is dangerous. If the nest is accessible from a distance and you can reach the entrance without getting close, this can work. If there is any doubt, call a professional.

What to do with a dead nest

Nothing. A dead wasp nest in October or November is harmless. You can cut through it with the trimmer. Wasps do not reuse old nests, and the papery material breaks down over winter. If you want to remove it for tidiness, wait until a full week after the last wasp activity and then pull it out with a gloved hand. There is no need to treat it with anything.

How to check a hedge quickly before each job

These are the steps we take before every hedge trim, adapted from the standard pre-job safety check used by working hedge cutters:

  1. Walk the full length of the hedge and look at the base from below
  2. Stand back and watch for 60 seconds for traffic in and out of any single point
  3. Listen for humming from dense sections
  4. Prod any suspicious area gently with a stick before putting hands or equipment near it
  5. If in doubt, do not start. Come back the following day and check again, or rearrange for late October onwards

This check adds very little time to the job and is genuinely worth doing every single time between May and October.

A word on timing

East Kent's mild coastal climate, particularly along the Sandwich Bay and Pegwell Bay coast, means wasp colonies here sometimes remain active a week or two longer than the UK average. Do not assume the nest is dead because it is mid-October. If there is any visible flight activity, it is still live.

The safest period for trimming a hedge that had a wasp nest is mid-November onwards, well after the first cold nights have finished the colony.
