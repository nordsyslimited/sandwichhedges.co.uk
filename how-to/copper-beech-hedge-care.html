Copper beech hedge care: growing purple beech in Kent

Copper beech is the same species as common green beech, just with more colour. The care is almost identical. But there are a few decisions, around timing, sun and soil, where the two part ways. Here is the comparison, from people who trim both across East Kent.

Updated September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Copper beech (Fagus sylvatica purpurea, also sold as Fagus sylvatica 'Purpurea' or simply as purple beech) is one of the most striking hedging options for a formal garden in East Kent. The new spring leaves open a vivid reddish-purple, deepen to bronze-copper through summer, then turn the characteristic bronze-tan of all beeches in autumn, holding on the branches through winter. You get something different to look at in every month of the year.

The species is the same as common green beech. The hedges we trim around Sandwich, Deal and Worth respond to the same cutting schedule, the same renovation techniques and the same pruning approach. The differences are subtle, but worth understanding before you plant or make your first cut.

Copper beech vs standard green beech: the key differences

Spring leaf colour Vivid reddish-purple Bright fresh green
Summer leaf colour Deep bronze-copper Mid to dark green
Autumn and winter leaf Bronze-tan, retained through winter Bronze-tan, retained through winter
Annual growth rate 30 to 40 cm 40 to 60 cm
Colour in shade Fades towards green; needs sun for best colour Tolerates partial shade reasonably well

Choosing between copper and green beech for your garden

Go for copper beech if:

  • The hedge will be in full or near-full sun (south or west-facing, not overshadowed by buildings or trees). The purple-red colour is at its best in direct sun.
  • You want a formal hedge with year-round visual interest. The colour cycle from spring purple through bronze summer to winter tan gives something different every season.
  • Your garden needs a bold focal feature. A copper beech hedge used as a backdrop to a mixed border or alongside stonework or pale gravel is genuinely striking.
  • You want to mix it with standard green beech for a tapestry effect. See our tapestry hedge guide for how to mix species effectively.

Go for standard green beech if:

  • The hedge is in partial or full shade. Green beech holds its colour better in lower light. Copper beech in shade tends to look a muddy brownish-green rather than the sharp purple you are aiming for.
  • Budget is a consideration. Bare-root copper beech plants are noticeably more expensive than standard green beech, particularly at hedging quantities. Green beech gives similar structure and the same winter leaf retention for less money.
  • You need a hedge established quickly. Green beech's faster growth rate, particularly in the first five years, means the hedge reaches its target height one to two seasons sooner.

When to trim a copper beech hedge

Nesting season: 1 March to 31 August

The UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to damage an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs roughly 1 March to 31 August. The safe cutting window for beech hedges is September to February. A late-July cut is acceptable if the hedge is checked and demonstrably empty first, and can trigger an attractive second flush of new growth. September and October are the practical main cutting months for most Kent gardens.

Timing the main annual cut correctly is slightly more important with copper beech than with green beech, because the timing affects how much of the year you see the best colour.

Late July: the colour-maximising option

A cut in late July, provided the hedge is checked and empty of active nests, triggers a second flush of the vivid reddish-purple new growth. The new tips emerging after the cut are more intensely coloured than the mature bronze-copper leaves they replace, so a July cut extends the period of good colour into late summer and early autumn. This is the approach we use on copper beech hedges in formal gardens around Sandwich and Deal where colour is the point.

August to September: the practical standard cut

For most gardens, cutting in August or September is sufficient and avoids any ambiguity around nesting checks. The hedge holds its summer colour until the cut, then the new growth following the cut provides autumn colour before the leaves drop or turn bronze-tan for winter. This is the lower-maintenance option and produces a neat, well-shaped hedge without the need for an earlier nest check.

Dormant season renovation

Like standard green beech, copper beech takes hard renovation cuts well in the dormant season (November to February). If your copper beech hedge has grown too wide or too tall, cut one face hard in year one and the other in year two. The hedge will respond with vigorous new growth from the cut points in spring. Renovation cuts in the dormant season will not affect the colour of the following season's growth.

Before you start: safety

  • Eye protection: safety glasses or goggles whenever using a powered trimmer.
  • Ear defenders: petrol and electric trimmers both exceed safe noise levels quickly.
  • Gloves: sturdy gloves to protect against flying debris and the edge of a sharp trimmer blade.
  • RCD adaptor: essential for all corded electric trimmers.
  • Ladder caution: a long-reach pole trimmer is far safer than a ladder for the top of a tall beech hedge. Beech hedges in formal gardens are often maintained at 2 to 2.5 metres, at which height a well-extended pole trimmer works well without any ladder.

Technique: how to trim copper beech

The trimming technique is the same as for standard green beech. Use a powered hedge trimmer with sharp, clean blades. Beech leaves are papery enough that a sharp trimmer gives a clean cut without shredding. Blunt blades will tear the leaves and give the hedge a brown, ragged appearance at the cut surface that persists for weeks.

On the top of the hedge, keep the trimmer bar level to maintain a consistent flat line. On the sides, angle the trimmer bar very slightly inward at the top (so the base of the hedge is marginally wider than the top). This classic A-profile allows light to reach the lower parts of the hedge, keeping the base from going bare over time.

Martin Fish of the Pots & Trowels gardening channel, who has been gardening for over 40 years and judges for the RHS, demonstrates clean hedge trimming technique in his video Hedge Cutting Explained and Demonstrated (Pots & Trowels, youtube.com/watch?v=pbLr1l7uNrs). The principles apply directly to copper and standard beech alike.

Soil and feeding

Copper beech performs best in well-drained, slightly acidic to neutral soil. It tolerates the free-draining sandy and chalk soils common around Sandwich, Worth and Eastry reasonably well, but benefits from an annual application of a balanced slow-release fertiliser in early spring, particularly in chalk soils where nutrient availability can be limited.

Avoid waterlogged conditions. Beech, both forms, is more susceptible to root disease in consistently wet soil than most hedging species. In the heavier clay soils around the inland villages of Kent, raised planting in a slight ridge above the surrounding soil level helps drainage around the roots.

Common problems

Colour fading to green

The most common issue with copper beech hedges is the colour fading towards green. This is almost always caused by insufficient sunlight. The purple pigmentation (anthocyanin) in copper beech is produced in response to UV light: less sunlight means less pigment. There is no treatment other than improving the light conditions. A hedge planted near a fence, wall or tall tree that shades it during the day will never achieve the deep purple colour of a hedge in full sun.

Thin or bare patches

Beech hedges can develop bare patches at the base if the profile has become too vertical and the upper growth shades out the lower. The solution is to taper the sides more aggressively in future cuts (wider base, narrower top) and, in severe cases, to cut the hedge hard in the dormant season to allow light to reach the base. See our guide to bare patches in hedges for the full approach.

Beech bark disease

Beech bark disease (caused by the scale insect Cryptococcus fagisuga followed by fungal infection) occasionally affects garden beech hedges, usually showing as oozing patches on the main stems. It is more common in very tall, mature hedges than in regularly trimmed ones. Remove and dispose of any obviously infected material; do not compost it.
