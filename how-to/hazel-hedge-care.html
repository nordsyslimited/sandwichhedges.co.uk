Hazel hedge care in Kent

A month-by-month guide to trimming, coppicing and looking after Corylus avellana in East Kent gardens and rural boundaries.

Updated August 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Hazel (Corylus avellana) is one of the oldest hedgerow plants in Britain, and in East Kent it shows up everywhere: along farm boundaries near Ash and Woodnesborough, in cottage gardens in Worth and Eastry, and as the backbone of the mixed native hedgerows that divide this part of the county into its characteristic patchwork. It is forgiving, genuinely wildlife-rich, and one of the very few hedge plants you can cut almost to the ground and watch grow back within a few seasons.

Managing it as a garden hedge is a different proposition from a field boundary, but the same underlying biology applies. Here is what we do and what we would tell a customer to do.

Nesting season: the rule that runs above everything else

Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 it is an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Hazel is a popular nesting site, particularly for robins, blackbirds and wrens. Nesting season runs roughly 1 March to 31 August. The safe cutting window is September to February. A light summer tidy of the very outermost growth is acceptable only if you have checked the hedge carefully and it is demonstrably empty.

Before you start: safety

  • Eye protection: hazel rods and cut stems spring back; flying chips are common.
  • Gloves: sturdy leather or cut-resistant gloves, especially for the coppicing cut and when removing cut material.
  • Ear defenders: for petrol or electric hedge trimmers.
  • Ladder safety: most hazel hedges can be reached with a long-reach pole trimmer, which is much safer than a ladder. If a ladder is needed, always have someone footing it and never overreach sideways.
  • Electric trimmers: use an RCD (residual current device) on any corded electric tool and keep the cable behind you.
  • Check for wasps: hazel thickets are a favourite site for wasps and other stinging insects, especially in summer. Check before you start.

The hazel hedge calendar

January and February: the coppicing window

This is the best time for heavy renovation work on hazel. The plant is dormant, the structure is visible without leaves, and coppicing or hard renovation now gives the hedge the whole growing season to recover. Cut back to the height you want, or for a full coppice, cut the main stems to 15–30 cm above ground level. Hazel responds to this vigorously, throwing up multiple new stems from the cut stump (the stool). The yellow catkins (lamb's tails) are usually just beginning in late February, which is pleasant timing: you can enjoy them before the cut if you are quick about it.

March: stop and look

Nesting gets underway from 1 March. Finish any major cuts before the end of February if you can. From March onwards, walk along the hedge and look in before touching anything. If a robin is flying in and out of the same spot repeatedly, there is almost certainly a nest there. Put the tools away.

April to late July: hands off

Peak nesting. Hazel is dense, multi-stemmed and provides excellent nesting cover, so the chances of finding an active nest during this period are high. There is nothing constructive to do in the hedge itself during these months. Mulch the base if you have not already done so; feed the roots rather than cutting the tops.

August: light trim if needed

By mid-to-late August most birds have fledged and a careful check usually confirms the hedge is empty. A light trim of this season's new growth only, using a hedge trimmer or hand shears, is fine. Keep it modest: just taking back the long whippy shoots keeps the hedge tidy and encourages denser growth. Do not do any renovation cutting in August; save that for winter.

September and October: harvest and tidy

Hazelnuts ripen in September and October, and if you want them, or want the birds and squirrels to have them, wait until the nuts are brown and beginning to fall before trimming the hedge further. October is a perfectly good time to tidy the outline of a hazel hedge while the leaves are still on. November and December are also fine for heavier cuts as the plant becomes fully dormant.

Coppicing a hazel hedge

Coppicing is the practice of cutting the main stems almost to the ground to stimulate vigorous regrowth from the base. Hazel is one of the traditional coppice species of the Kentish countryside, alongside sweet chestnut, and it responds to the technique extremely well.

For a garden hedge, a full coppice is rarely done all in one go. Instead, consider a rotational approach: take out the oldest, most congested one-third of the stems each winter over three consecutive years. This keeps the hedge in reasonable shape throughout while steadily rejuvenating it. Full coppice to the base is more dramatic but more effective for a badly neglected hedge: it looks alarming for a season, then grows back with real purpose.

The traditional rotation for hazel coppice in Kent is every 7–15 years for hedge management, or every 3–7 years if you want to harvest straight hazel rods for hedge laying, hurdle-making or pea sticks. Freshly coppiced hazel is still used by hedge layers across East Kent for the binding stakes (heethers) woven along the top of a laid hedge.

The Royal Horticultural Society's video "Why you need a hazel in your garden" covers the wildlife value and year-round interest of Corylus avellana in some detail; it is well worth a watch before you decide how to manage yours.

Keeping a hazel hedge at the right size

Hazel grows 30–60 cm a year in good conditions, which means a hedge kept to 1.5 m needs a trim at least once a year, and ideally in late winter and again in August. Without management it will reach 4–6 m and begin to look more like a row of small trees.

A good working height for a hazel hedge in a garden is 1.2–2 m. At this height you can reach the top without a ladder using a long-reach trimmer, and the hedge stays dense enough to provide good screening and wildlife habitat. Much below 1 m and hazel becomes difficult to keep formal; it is not really a low-edging plant.

Wildlife value in East Kent

Hazel is one of the most wildlife-valuable hedge plants you can grow in this part of Kent. The catkins in January and February provide critical early pollen for queen bumblebees, honeybees and early solitary species emerging on mild days. The dense twiggy structure through spring and summer is a first choice for robins, wrens and blackbirds for nesting. In autumn, hazelnuts feed dormice (Kent has reasonable populations in well-managed rural hedgerows), woodpeckers, jays, squirrels and many small mammals. Even the leaves are useful: the hazel leaf feeds dozens of moth species that do not have many other options in a garden hedge.

If you are near Ash, Woodnesborough or the rural lanes north of Eastry, there is a very good chance any established hazel hedge on your boundary is part of a wider ecological corridor. Managing it sympathetically, leaving some nuts to fall and avoiding cuts in nesting season, makes a meaningful difference to local wildlife, not just a symbolic one.
