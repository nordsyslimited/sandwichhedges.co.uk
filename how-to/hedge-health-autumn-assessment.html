Autumn hedge health check: diagnosing problems in October

Once the cutting season winds down, it is worth spending ten minutes walking your hedge and looking properly. October is the ideal time: growth has slowed, problems are visible, and there is still time to act before winter.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Most hedge problems are not sudden. They develop gradually over a season or two: a section gets progressively thinner, a fungal disease takes hold in one corner, a patch at the base goes bare. The trouble is that people often notice these things only in late winter, when the hedge is leafless, cold and the problems look worse than they are.

October is genuinely the best month for a health assessment. Evergreen and semi-evergreen hedges (privet, laurel, box, leylandii, yew, photinia) still have their leaves and show symptoms clearly. Deciduous hedges (hawthorn, beech, hornbeam) are beginning to thin, which makes structural problems visible for the first time since spring. And if you find something that needs acting on, there is still time to treat or plan the work before the worst of winter.

Nesting season: late October still applies

The Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 covers nesting season from 1 March to 31 August, but in mild Kent autumns occasional late-brooding birds can still be active into early September. By October this is very unlikely, but always do a quick visual check before cutting or disturbing any section of hedge. The check takes thirty seconds and is worth the habit.

The five diagnostic questions

Work through these in order, starting at one end of the hedge and walking its full length before making any judgements.

1. Has the hedge grown noticeably less this year?

Compare this year's growth to previous years. If a hedge that normally needs cutting twice is looking the same after one cut, or a vigorous privet has put on half the usual growth, something has changed.

If yes: check the roots and soil. Dig a small test hole 20 to 30 cm from the base on both sides. Look for waterlogging (standing water or very wet clay in a hollow), compaction (rock-hard soil with no earthworm activity), or unusual root colour (healthy roots are white or pale; black, slimy or reddish roots suggest rot or disease). Also check whether the hedge has been cut into brown on conifers: this stops regrowth permanently in that section.

If no: continue to question 2.

2. Are there bare or brown patches?

Walk close to the hedge and look from different angles, including from below. Bare sections that you could not see from the garden path are often visible when you crouch and look upward through the canopy.

If yes: identify where the bare patch sits:

  • Bare at the base, full at the top (the classic "leggy" hedge): usually a long-term trimming profile problem. The hedge has been cut narrower at the base than at the top, shading out the lower growth. Fix by gradually cutting a wider-at-the-base profile over two or three seasons.
  • A section dead from the ground up, mid-hedge or top to bottom: more likely to be disease, root rot or physical damage (a vehicle, a chemical spill, herbicide drift).
  • Random brown patches in a box hedge: almost certainly box blight. See the box blight section below.
  • Brown interior on leylandii only: normal, not a problem. See the leylandii section below.

If no: continue to question 3.

3. Are there unusual leaf markings, spots or coatings?

Look at the leaves closely, not just the overall colour. A yellowing hedge seen from across the garden can look like autumn colour, but close inspection reveals whether it is natural senescence, nutrient deficiency or disease.

Signs to look for by species:

  • Box: small tan or brown spots on leaves that have spread into larger brown patches; white cottony or powdery coating on affected stems in damp conditions. Box blight (Cylindrocladium buxicola). Remove affected material, improve air circulation by thinning, avoid cutting in wet weather.
  • Privet: yellow mottled leaves, sticky honeydew on lower surfaces or on the ground beneath. Privet aphid or whitefly. Treat with a horticultural soap spray or systemic insecticide before temperatures drop too far in October.
  • Laurel (cherry laurel): brown or tan shothole spots on leaves, as if the leaf has been peppered. Shot-hole disease (Stigmina carpophila). Usually cosmetic and self-limiting; remove the worst affected material and ensure the hedge has good drainage.
  • Yew: yellowing from the base upward, combined with slow, spreading dieback. Phytophthora root rot. Check drainage; this disease thrives in waterlogged conditions. There is no cure for heavily infected plants, but improving drainage can slow progression.
  • Leylandii/thuja: orange-brown blistering on the bark and stems in wet areas. Seridium canker. Cut out affected sections well below the visible symptoms and dispose of them; do not compost.
  • Photinia (Red Robin): large orange or red powdery spots on older leaves. Entomosporium leaf spot. Worse in wet years and on closely trimmed hedges in humid positions. Remove affected leaves; avoid overhead watering.

If no unusual markings: continue to question 4.

4. Is the base of the hedge significantly thinner than the top?

Stand back and look at the hedge in silhouette. A well-maintained hedge should be as full at the base as at the top, with a slight inward taper on the sides. If the base is noticeably sparse, open or bare compared to the top, the hedge has developed a mushroom or wedge profile.

If yes: this is correctable but takes time. The fix is to gradually cut the top and sides back to a narrower profile over two or three seasons, restoring light to the base. In the meantime, removing competing grass and weeds from the base and applying a mulch helps. Do not cut the base back hard in one go in an attempt to stimulate new growth: the base growth needs light first.

If no: continue to question 5.

5. Is any section leaning, shifting or losing structural integrity?

Push gently against the hedge at intervals. A hedge should be solid. If sections flex, tilt or feel unstable, the root system may be compromised, or the stems have split at the base and the hedge is held together by the canopy rather than the roots.

If yes: investigate at the base. Common causes include waterlogging softening the soil, vandal or vehicle damage, or honey fungus (Armillaria). Honey fungus produces honey-coloured toadstools clustered at the base in autumn and leaves distinctive flat black bootlace-like strands (rhizomorphs) under the bark just above soil level. There is no chemical cure for Armillaria; affected sections need to be removed and the stumps ground out before replanting.

If no: the hedge has passed the five checks. Make a note of any minor issues and plan for the routine winter maintenance.

What to do with your findings

A straightforward October assessment gives you three possible outcomes:

  1. All clear: the hedge is healthy, well-shaped and needs only its routine winter maintenance. Make a note of the date and any minor observations, and carry on.
  2. Minor issues that need attention: profile problems, some base thinning, a small patch of leaf spot. Plan the corrective work for winter. Most of these are slow to develop and a planned winter intervention is the right response.
  3. Significant problems: confirmed disease, root rot, structural failure or large dead sections. These need a proper assessment, possibly by a professional, before you decide on the right approach. Some problems (Armillaria, advanced Phytophthora) require full removal and cannot be fixed in place.
Ten minutes in October saves you the surprise of a dead hedge in February. Write down what you find and when. Your future self will be glad of it.
