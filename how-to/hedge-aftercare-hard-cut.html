Hedge aftercare: what to do after a hard renovation cut

The work does not stop when the trimmer goes back in the van. The first few weeks after a hard renovation cut are when a hedge is most vulnerable, and a bit of aftercare makes a real difference to how quickly it comes back.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

A hard renovation cut, where you take a hedge back significantly from its previous size, is not something most species mind as much as people fear. Beech, hornbeam, hawthorn, privet, laurel and yew all regrow reliably from hard cuts, often coming back denser than before. What they do need is a reasonable start in the weeks after the work, particularly in the drier parts of East Kent where soils drain quickly and drought stress is a genuine risk in summer.

This guide covers what to do in the hours, weeks and months after a hard renovation cut, including what normal recovery looks like and when to start worrying.

Nesting season: renovation cuts should be done September to February

Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 it is an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs roughly from 1 March to 31 August. Hard renovation cuts should always be carried out between September and February, when hedges are outside the nesting window. If you are reading this after a renovation cut carried out in that safe period, you are in the right place. If the cut was done in spring or summer, the hedge itself may need a nest check before any further work.

Before you start: a brief safety note

Clearing cut material from beneath a recently renovated hedge means handling woody debris at ground level. Wear sturdy gloves to protect against sharp cut ends and any bramble stems that may have been growing through the hedge. If you are using a powered shredder on the cuttings, wear eye protection and ear defenders. Rake out from the sides rather than from beneath the hedge to avoid standing under unstable cut material.

Step 1: clear the cuttings the same day, or within a couple of days

Cut material left piled against or beneath a renovated hedge shades out any new growth trying to emerge at the base, and in wet weather it creates the conditions for fungal problems, particularly on privet and laurel. Rake out all the cuttings from underneath and along the base of the hedge as soon as the work is done, or within a day or two of the cut.

Smaller material, shredded hedge trimmings especially, can go straight into a compost heap or be used as a mulch path. Thicker cut stems from a renovation cut are best shredded if you have access to a shredder, or stacked and allowed to rot down over a year or two. Leylandii and other conifer material composts very slowly and is better put out for green waste collection unless you have a hot compost system.

Step 2: expose the base and mulch the soil

Once the cuttings are cleared, have a look at the soil at the base of the hedge. After a hard cut the base is suddenly exposed to light it has not seen for years, which is exactly what you want to encourage new growth from the base. If the soil is bare, now is a good time to apply a mulch layer of composted bark or wood chip, 7 to 10 cm deep, along the base of the hedge. Keep a 5 cm gap around any stems to prevent collar rot.

Mulching retains moisture, suppresses weeds and adds organic matter to what may well be very depleted soil. A hedge that has been left to grow for years often sits over compacted, nutrient-poor ground. The mulch starts to address that gradually.

In the chalk-based soils around Sandwich, Ash and Eastry, where drainage is fast and moisture retention is poor, the mulch layer is particularly valuable in the first summer after a renovation cut.

Step 3: water in dry spells in the first growing season

Most renovation cuts on deciduous hedges are done between October and February, which means the hedge spends the winter recovering and starts producing new growth in spring. The critical period for moisture is May to September of the following year, when the hedge is producing the new growth that will fill in the gaps from the cut.

If you get a dry spell of two weeks or more in that first growing season, it is worth giving the base of the hedge a good soaking, ideally with a soaker hose or by running a garden hose along the base slowly rather than using a sprinkler overhead. The aim is to get water into the root zone, not just wet the surface.

Kent's coastal conditions mean the area around Sandwich, Deal and Worth can have drier summers than inland areas, and hedges renovated in winter that then face a dry first summer sometimes stall in recovery. Watering in the first year after a hard cut is not dramatic gardening, but it makes a real difference on fast-draining soils.

Step 4: apply a balanced feed in spring

In the spring following a renovation cut, once you can see new growth starting (typically March to April), apply a balanced general fertiliser along the base of the hedge at the manufacturer's recommended rate. A slow-release granular fertiliser works well: scatter it along the base, rake it in gently and water it in if the weather is dry.

Avoid high-nitrogen fertilisers (such as lawn feed) on a hedge that has just been hard cut. High nitrogen pushes rapid, soft growth, which is vulnerable to frost damage if the hedge puts on a lot of new growth late in the season. A balanced feed, one with roughly equal NPK ratios or one slightly weighted towards potassium (which builds stronger cell walls), gives steadier, sturdier regrowth.

If the hedge was mulched in autumn or early winter, you may find the mulch layer has broken down partially by spring and is already releasing nutrients into the soil. In that case a light feed is still worthwhile but a heavy dose is not needed.

Step 5: monitor regrowth and spot dead wood early

During the first growing season after the renovation cut, watch the hedge for even regrowth. On most species you would expect to see new buds breaking and new growth appearing all along the cut stems within a few weeks of the spring growth flush. If some sections are noticeably slower than others, or stay brown while everything else is greening up, it is worth investigating.

To check a stem, scratch the bark gently with a fingernail or a penknife. Live bark is green or cream beneath the surface. Dead bark is brown, dry and pulls away easily. If a section of stem is dead, trace it back towards the base until you find live wood and cut it off there, using clean secateurs. Fresh live growth below the cut will fill the gap.

Some die-back after a hard cut is entirely normal, particularly on old woody stems that were already in poor health before the renovation. What matters is that the majority of the hedge is showing live regrowth. A hedge with patches of die-back interspersed with vigorous new growth is recovering normally.

Step 6: resist the urge to tidy up in the first year

The new growth that follows a renovation cut often looks a bit unruly: uneven lengths, some stems shooting faster than others, a generally rough appearance. This is exactly what you want. The hedge is investing energy in recovery. Leave it alone for a full growing season after the renovation cut before you do any tidying trim.

Once the hedge has had a year to recover and the new growth is clearly established, a light tidy cut in the following autumn is perfectly fine, and from that point onwards you manage it as you would normally.
