Privet vs laurel: the two most common garden hedges compared

Ask any hedge cutter in East Kent what fills most garden boundaries and the answer is privet and laurel. Both are fast, both are tough, but they behave very differently. Here is the honest comparison.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Privet and cherry laurel between them account for a large proportion of the garden hedges we cut across Sandwich, Deal, Woodnesborough, Worth and Ash. They are popular for the same reason: both grow fast, tolerate most soils, and take hard cutting without collapsing. But they are not interchangeable, and choosing the wrong one for your situation leads to more work than it needed to be.

Side-by-side comparison

Feature Privet (Ligustrum ovalifolium) Cherry laurel (Prunus laurocerasus)
Growth rate 30–45 cm/year, fastest in summer 45–60 cm/year, can exceed this in good soil
Evergreen? Semi-evergreen; may drop leaves in harsh winters Fully evergreen, holds leaves all year
Cuts per year 2–3 (June, August, autumn tidy) 1–2 (late summer main cut, possible autumn tidy)
Best tool Hedge trimmer (electric, battery or petrol) Secateurs for main cut; trimmer for light tidies only
Winter privacy Fair to good in mild Kent winters; thin in hard frost Excellent, solid year-round screen
Tolerance of hard cut Very good; recovers well from renovation Good; recovers from hard renovation but slower
Wildlife value High: berries for birds, flowers for pollinators Moderate: nesting cover, some berries; flowers less accessible to UK pollinators
Soil preference Most soils; tolerates chalk and dry conditions Most soils; dislikes waterlogged ground
Cost to buy Usually cheaper per plant; often available bare-root Slightly more per plant, often sold pot-grown

Nesting season applies to both

Whether you are cutting privet or laurel, the rule is the same. Under the UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs roughly 1 March to 31 August. The safe cutting window is September to February, with a light summer trim allowed only if the hedge has been checked and found to be empty. Both species offer good nesting cover, so do check carefully before picking up a trimmer in summer.

When privet is the right choice

Privet is the better pick if you want a tidy, relatively formal hedge that can be kept to a modest height without much fuss. It responds well to frequent light cuts, which means two or three sessions with a hedge trimmer per year can keep it sharp all season. In East Kent's mild coastal winters, privet holds its leaves reliably from Sandwich Bay through to Woodnesborough and Ash, though in a severe January frost it can look sparse for a few weeks.

The flowers, which appear in early summer on growth you did not cut the previous year, attract a surprising number of pollinators: bees, hoverflies and butterflies all use privet blossom. If you let one cut fall late, you get the flowers; if you want a tight formal hedge all year, you cut earlier and sacrifice them. Both are reasonable choices.

Privet also tolerates the chalky and sandy soils common in parts of East Kent far better than many species. If your garden soil dries out quickly in summer, privet is unlikely to complain.

When laurel is the right choice

Cherry laurel wins if year-round privacy is the priority. Its large, glossy, dark green leaves are almost opaque even in winter, which privet cannot quite match in a normal UK winter. If your hedge faces a road or a neighbour and you want solid screening 12 months a year, laurel delivers it consistently.

Laurel also suits gardeners who do not want to cut very often. One main cut a year in late summer, done properly with secateurs to avoid the brown shredded-leaf effect, is usually enough to keep it manageable. The trade-off is that each cut takes longer per metre than privet, because cutting through individual large leaves with hand tools is slower than running a hedge trimmer along a privet hedge.

On heavier soils, particularly the clay-rich ground around Worth and parts of Ash, laurel often performs slightly better than privet during wet winters, partly because its root system tolerates temporarily waterlogged soil more reliably.

Before you start cutting: safety

Before you start

For privet, a hedge trimmer is appropriate; for laurel's main cut, use secateurs or loppers to avoid shredded brown edges. Either way: wear eye protection against flying debris, ear defenders for petrol and loud electric trimmers, and sturdy gloves. If you are using a corded electric trimmer, plug it into a residual current device (RCD) or use an RCD adapter. Prefer a long-reach pole trimmer over a ladder for tall sections. Check for wasp nests and brambles before starting.

The trimming calendar for each

Privet

First light trim in June, if the hedge is clearly empty of nesting birds. Main cut in late August, once nesting season is reliably over. A final tidy in October if growth continues. On very fast-growing privet in a good summer, a fourth pass in July is sometimes worth it for a formal hedge beside a front path.

Cherry laurel

One main cut in late summer, ideally late August to mid-September. Use secateurs rather than a powered trimmer to leave clean cuts on the large leaves. A second light tidy in October is sometimes worthwhile on vigorous plants. Avoid cutting in wet weather: laurel is susceptible to bacterial and fungal diseases, and cutting in rain increases the risk of infection entering through cut stems.

Can you mix them?

You can, but it is worth thinking through carefully. Privet and laurel have very different leaf sizes and textures, and they look mismatched clipped side by side in a formal hedge. Mixing them in an informal or wildlife hedge is more forgiving: if the hedge is allowed to grow a little loosely, different foliage textures read as variety rather than inconsistency. For a formal garden boundary, stick to one or the other.
