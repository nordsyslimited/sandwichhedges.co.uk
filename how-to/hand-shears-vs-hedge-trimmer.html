Hand shears vs hedge trimmer: which to use when

A powered trimmer is faster. Hand shears give a cleaner cut on the right plants. Knowing which to reach for, and when to use both on the same hedge, is what separates a tidy finish from a ragged one. From the team that cuts hedges across Sandwich, Deal and East Kent.

Updated September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

The question comes up often: should I buy a hedge trimmer, or are shears good enough? The honest answer is that it depends on what you are cutting. There are hedges where shears are the better tool, and jobs where no pair of hand shears would get through the work in a reasonable time. Most gardens benefit from having both available and knowing which one to pick up.

Nesting season: check before you cut anything

Under the UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs roughly 1 March to 31 August. The safe cutting window for most hedges is September to February. If you need to trim during nesting months, check the hedge carefully first.

The quick summary: when each tool wins

Situation Better tool
Box, yew or privet topiary (balls, cones, precise shapes) Hand shears
Large-leaved species (laurel, griselinia, photinia) Hand shears or secateurs
Small formal hedge under 5 metres (box edging, low privet) Hand shears
Long straight run of privet, beech or leylandii Powered trimmer
Hedge over 1.5 metres tall Powered trimmer (long-reach for the top)
Dense or overgrown hedge with thick stems Petrol trimmer, plus loppers
Delicate, fine-textured hedge (lavender, rosemary) Hand shears
Final tidy after a powered trim Hand shears

The case for hand shears

Large-leaved species

This is the clearest argument for shears. Laurel (both cherry laurel and Portuguese laurel) has large, glossy leaves. When you cut across them with a powered trimmer, the blades slice through individual leaves and leave ragged, brown, half-cut leaf remnants scattered across the surface of the hedge. From a distance it looks as if the hedge is dying. From close up, it looks like someone took kitchen scissors to it.

Shears or secateurs cut entire stems rather than slicing across leaves. The hedge looks clean immediately after cutting and stays clean as the cut surfaces dry. In East Kent gardens, where laurel is probably the most commonly planted screening hedge after privet and leylandii, this difference matters to a lot of people.

Griselinia, photinia (Red Robin) and fatsia are in the same category: large leaves, better cut with hand tools.

Topiary and precise shaping

A powered trimmer is a surface tool. It is excellent at maintaining a flat plane. It is not good at cutting a precise curve, a tight corner, or the underside of an overhang. For box balls, yew cones, privet spirals, and the sort of architectural shapes that are common in formal East Kent gardens and at properties around Sandwich Bay, hand shears or even secateurs give far more control.

The professional approach: power trimmer for the main faces, then hand shears for detail work and tightening up. The two tools complement each other.

Small hedges and edging

A row of low box edging around a border, a small privet hedge of two or three metres, the lavender border along a path: setting up a powered trimmer for these jobs is more trouble than it is worth. Hand shears are quick to pick up, need no lead or battery, and give precise control in tight spaces. For Kent gardens in Sandwich or Deal where the borders are small and the spaces between features are narrow, shears are often simply the more practical tool.

Quiet, and kinder to wildlife

A powered trimmer at close range is genuinely loud. For neighbouring properties and for your own peace of mind on a September morning, shears are a quieter option. There is also a meaningful difference in how disturbing the tool is to any wildlife that might be in or near the hedge: small birds and mammals that would scatter from a petrol trimmer may stay put, or at least move calmly, when you are working slowly with hand tools.

The case for a powered trimmer

Speed on long runs

For a run of privet, beech or leylandii hedge that is twenty metres long, hand shears would take a full morning. A cordless trimmer does the same run in fifteen minutes. For homeowners in Worth, Woodnesborough or Eastry where gardens can be genuinely large with substantial hedge perimeters, a powered trimmer is not a luxury, it is a practical necessity.

Consistency over a long length

A powered trimmer blade is straight and stiff. It naturally cuts a flat plane. Over a long run, it tends to produce a more consistent result than hand shears, which can wander slightly as the arm tires. This is why professional hedge cutters use trimmers even on species where shears would give a slightly cleaner cut: the time saving is too significant to ignore, and a light touch-up with shears after the main cut addresses any rough edges.

Height

Any hedge above about 1.5 metres becomes difficult to manage safely with hand tools, because you need a ladder. Ladders are involved in the majority of serious garden accidents. A long-reach pole trimmer lets you cut the top of hedges up to around three metres from the ground, eliminating the need for a ladder on most domestic hedges. For neighbour-height issues, see our hedge heights and the law guide.

Thick or overgrown growth

Where stems are thicker than about 8 to 10 mm, hand shears start to struggle. They will cut through most annual hedge growth, but if the hedge has been left a year or two longer than it should have been, a powered trimmer with a strong motor handles the stems much more easily. Petrol trimmers are strongest; high-voltage (36V or higher) cordless models handle most domestic overgrowth well too.

Safety: both tools carry risks

Before you start with either tool

  • Eye protection: shears and trimmers both send debris flying. Wear it.
  • Gloves: good grip on the handle and protection against stems and blade edges.
  • Ear defenders: for powered trimmers. Not needed for shears.
  • Powered trimmer and a cable: always use an RCD adaptor. Keep the cable behind you.
  • Wasp nests and brambles: check the hedge before you start, particularly from August to October.
  • Prefer a pole trimmer over a ladder for any hedge above about 1.5 metres. Most garden ladder accidents happen at low heights.

Hand shears have their own risks: the blades are very sharp, and a slip on an awkward angle can cause a deep cut. Keep shears clean and dry, and always cut away from your body. Never leave open shears blade-up on the ground where someone could tread on them.

Maintenance: a quick comparison

Hand shears: oil the pivot and the blades after each use. Sharpen once or twice a season with a diamond file or whetstone. For detailed sharpening instructions, see our guide to sharpening hedge shears and secateurs.

Powered trimmers: clean the blades after each use (sap from laurel and leylandii gums the mechanism quickly), oil lightly, and store the battery at 40 to 60% charge over winter. Full servicing guidance in our hedge trimmer maintenance guide.

What the professionals actually do

In practice, the hedges we cut across Sandwich, Deal, Worth, Ash, Woodnesborough, Eastry and Sandwich Bay rarely get cut with one tool alone. A typical session goes: powered trimmer for the main faces and top of any hedge with fine-textured growth, then hand shears for the base, any corners, and the sections around gates and posts where the trimmer cannot get close enough. Laurel and large-leaved hedges get cut almost entirely with shears or loppers, with the trimmer reserved for the rare section of new fine growth on top.

A trimmer is for getting the bulk done quickly. Shears are for getting it done properly. Most hedges deserve both.
