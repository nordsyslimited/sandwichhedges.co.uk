Copper beech vs green beech for a Kent garden hedge

Both make superb formal hedges. The colour is different, the soil requirements are almost identical, and the trimming needs are the same. Here is what actually changes between the two, and when copper beech earns its place.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Beech makes one of the finest formal hedges in East Kent. The formal beech hedges around Sandwich, Worth and Eastry are well-known locally: dense tops, russet winter leaves, and a structure that holds its shape year after year. When customers ask us about planting a new beech hedge, the first decision is often this one: copper or green?

It is a reasonable question. The two look quite different through summer, they cost roughly the same, and planting technique is identical for both. The choice is mostly aesthetic, with a few practical nuances worth knowing.

At a glance: the key differences

Copper beech Green beech
Summer colour Rich purple-brown Mid to bright green
Winter colour Warm russet-brown Warm russet-brown
Winter leaf retention Yes (marcescent) Yes (marcescent)
Growth rate 20–40 cm/yr (established) 20–40 cm/yr (established)

Relative cost Slightly higher Slightly lower

When copper beech earns its place

The purple-brown foliage through summer is the whole point of copper beech as a hedge. It makes a striking backdrop for a garden with lighter-coloured planting, or a strong contrast against stone walls and flint-faced buildings, which are common throughout the Sandwich and Deal area. If the hedge will be a visual centrepiece of the garden, copper beech is worth the slight extra cost.

Copper beech also works well as a "tapestry" hedge when alternate plants of copper and green beech are mixed together. Through summer the effect is a shifting pattern of dark and light foliage; by November both have faded to the same russet, and the hedge reads as uniform again. This suits less formal gardens where a little variation is welcome.

Some customers ask us about copper beech for south-facing boundaries, where the dark foliage absorbs light differently. In reality the choice works in any aspect: beech hedges in East Kent perform reliably on north, south, east and west-facing boundaries, and the copper variety is no exception.

When green beech is the better choice

For a classic formal hedge in a traditional or working garden, green beech is the standard choice and has been for centuries. It is typically a few pence cheaper per plant, which can add up on a long run. On the thinnest chalk soils, common on the higher ground around Ash and Woodnesborough, green beech has a marginal advantage in vigour, though the difference is small under normal conditions.

Green beech is also more uniform: every plant is essentially the same, so a row tends to look consistent from the outset. With copper beech, there can be slight variation in how deep the purple tone is between plants, particularly in the first year or two. This evens out as the hedge matures, but it is worth knowing if you need a very precise, uniform look from day one.

For wildlife value, the two are equivalent: both support many of the same insect species and produce beechmast in maturity. Neither has a significant advantage over the other as a nesting habitat once the hedge is established and dense.

The nesting season applies to both

Regardless of colour, beech makes an excellent nesting habitat once established. Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to damage or destroy an active nest. Nesting season runs approximately 1 March to 31 August. Trim beech hedges, copper or green, in late August to September for best results, and always check for nests first.

Planting either variety: the same approach

Planting technique is identical for both copper and green beech. Bare-root whips are available from UK nurseries between November and March, and planting earlier in that window, in November or early December, gives the best results in East Kent.

Three plants per metre, spaced 30–35 cm apart in a single row, is the standard spacing for a formal garden boundary. Prepare the trench to 25–30 cm depth, incorporate compost on chalk to improve moisture retention, and backfill firmly. The formative cut, reducing each whip by one-third immediately after planting, is the step people are most reluctant to do and the one that makes the most difference to the finished hedge.

Mixing the two: the tapestry approach

If you want some variety, alternating copper and green beech whips along the planting line is straightforward. The standard ratio is roughly one copper plant to two or three green, so the green base tone comes through clearly with the copper providing accent. Order both varieties from the same supplier in the same size, plant them together, and treat the hedge identically from then on.

By winter, as noted above, both varieties fade to the same russet, so the tapestry effect is a summer phenomenon. Some people love this seasonality; others find they preferred the idea in spring and are less sure about it by August. It is worth considering before you plant rather than after.
