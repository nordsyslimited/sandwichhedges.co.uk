RCDs and extension leads for mains electric hedge trimmers

A mains-powered hedge trimmer is one of the riskiest tools in the garden if used without the right electrical protection. Here is what an RCD does, what type to use, and how to choose an extension lead that will not let you down.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Mains-powered hedge trimmers are still the tool of choice for many gardeners: they are lighter than petrol, quieter, never run out of power mid-hedge and produce less exhaust. But they come with a cable, and a cable draped across a hedge while a blade moves along the top of it is an obvious hazard. The question is not whether the cable will ever get cut, but what happens when it does.

The answer, with an RCD in place, is that power cuts in 30 milliseconds. Without one, the answer is a shock that may be fatal. That is not a particularly complex risk calculation, and yet a significant number of people use mains garden tools without RCD protection, either because their existing socket happens to be RCD-protected and they do not realise it, or because they never bought the adaptor and have not yet had a problem.

This guide compares the types of RCD protection available and the extension lead types suitable for hedge trimming, so you can make an informed decision about what you actually need.

Nesting season reminder

Electrical safety applies whatever the season, but before using any hedge trimmer between 1 March and 31 August, check the hedge for active bird's nests under the UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981. An electric trimmer is not safer than a petrol one from a nesting-season perspective. If you find an active nest, do not cut that section until the birds have left.

What an RCD does and what it does not do

A residual current device monitors the current flowing out of a socket and the current returning on the neutral wire. In a healthy circuit these should be equal. When a cable is cut and current takes a different path, through a person for example, the difference (the residual current) is detected and the RCD trips, cutting power in 30 to 40 milliseconds. Modern 30mA RCDs trip fast enough to prevent a cardiac arrest from most cable-cut scenarios.

What an RCD does not do: it does not protect against electric shock between live and neutral (the two conductors in the cable). It does not replace fuses or MCBs. And it is not a substitute for safe working practice, keeping the cable away from the blade, working from the cable-out end of the hedge rather than back towards it, and looping the cable over your shoulder rather than letting it trail on the ground. The RCD is a last-resort protection, not a reason to be careless with the cable.

Comparison: types of RCD protection

Plug-in RCD adaptor

A plug-in RCD adaptor is a small device that plugs into an ordinary socket. The adaptor has a socket outlet on its face, and you plug the tool or extension lead into that. It costs around £10 to £15 and can be used in any socket.

Advantages: Cheap, portable, works in any socket, easy to test (most have a test button on the face). If you buy one and use it every time you use a mains garden tool, you have sorted the problem for under £15.

Disadvantages: Can be forgotten or misplaced. The adaptor adds a slight bulk at the socket end, which can cause issues with sockets in awkward positions. Because it plugs in and out, there is a small risk over time of the contacts degrading. Test the adaptor monthly during the season by pressing the test button.

Verdict for most domestic gardeners: This is the minimum sensible setup and probably the most practical for anyone who does not already have a dedicated outdoor socket.

Built-in RCD socket or RCCB on the consumer unit

A built-in RCD socket has the residual current device integrated into the socket faceplate. You can identify one by the test and reset buttons on the face of the socket. An RCCB (residual current circuit breaker) on the consumer unit protects all circuits on that breaker.

Advantages: Cannot be forgotten because it is always there. Provides consistent, correctly-rated protection. If your outdoor socket already has this, you are protected without any additional adaptor.

Disadvantages: Requires an electrician to install (a registered electrician for any new socket or modification to the consumer unit). Has a fixed installation cost of £80 to £150 or more depending on the job.

Verdict: The right long-term solution if you are having any electrical work done outdoors. If you are having a garden socket installed, ask for one with built-in RCD protection as standard. If you already have a garden socket, check the faceplate for test/reset buttons before assuming you need an adaptor.

RCD extension lead

Some outdoor extension leads are sold with an RCD built into the socket block. These combine the extension lead and RCD protection in a single purchase.

Advantages: Convenient. The protection travels with the extension lead. Some models include a cable drum on a reel.

Disadvantages: More expensive than a separate adaptor and lead. Quality varies considerably: cheaper models often do not state the RCD trip current on the packaging, or use 30mA only at the marketing level. Check that the product states a 30mA sensitivity rating before buying. Avoid models where the RCD is integrated into a cable drum that is not fully unwound during use, as a coiled cable generates heat.

Verdict: A reasonable option if buying a new extension lead for garden use. Choose a reputable brand and confirm the 30mA rating is stated explicitly.

Comparison: extension lead types for hedge trimming

Standard outdoor extension lead, 1.5mm², 13A, IP44

This is the right choice for most domestic hedge trimming applications. A 1.5mm² cable rated at 13A and with outdoor-rated connectors (IP44 or better) handles the current demand of any domestic hedge trimmer up to about 700W comfortably, at cable runs up to 25 metres.

IP44 means the connectors are protected against water splashing from any direction: adequate for use in light rain, though it is better practice to avoid using any mains tool in wet conditions regardless of connector rating.

Avoid extension leads sold for indoor use. The connector housings on indoor leads are not designed for outdoor conditions and will degrade if left outside. The cable itself may be rated differently, and the insulation compound is not UV-stabilised.

Heavy-duty outdoor extension lead, 2.5mm², 13A, IP44

If you regularly need runs above 25 metres, a 2.5mm² cable reduces voltage drop significantly. The cable is heavier and stiffer, which is a minor inconvenience when coiling it back up at the end of the job, but the electrical performance is noticeably better on long runs.

For hedge trimming specifically, runs above 25 metres are uncommon in domestic gardens but do occur in larger properties around Sandwich Bay, Worth and Ash where properties have longer hedges and sockets may be sited centrally rather than at the garden boundary. If you find your trimmer losing power or sounding laboured near the end of a long lead, voltage drop is the likely cause.

Cable drum (reel)

Cable drums are convenient for storage but require care in use. The cable must be fully unrolled from the drum before connecting a hedge trimmer or any tool drawing sustained current above around 500W. A coiled cable acts as an inductor under load and the resulting heating can degrade the cable insulation over time, eventually creating an insulation fault that the RCD will detect but that is better avoided entirely.

Many cable drum models have a thermal overload switch that trips if the drum overheats. This is a useful safety feature but should not be relied on as the reason for not unrolling the cable. Fully unroll, then connect.

The right setup for typical hedge trimming in East Kent

For most gardeners with a standard domestic garden in Sandwich, Deal or the nearby villages, the practical setup is:

  • A plug-in 30mA RCD adaptor plugged into the garden socket
  • An outdoor-rated 1.5mm² 13A extension lead, 15 to 20 metres, fully unrolled
  • The hedge trimmer plugged into the end of the extension lead
  • The cable looped over the shoulder and kept clear of the blade during cutting

Test the RCD adaptor by pressing the test button before each use. It should trip the outlet immediately. Reset and proceed. If the adaptor does not trip on test, replace it: the device has failed and is no longer providing protection.

Inspect the extension lead cable each season for cuts, abrasions, or areas where the outer sheath has been damaged. A hedgehog in the cable run, a lawn mower driven over the lead, or a year of UV exposure are all common sources of damage to outdoor cables. If the outer sheath is breached, replace the lead rather than repairing it with tape.

Not up for the electrical setup?

Mains-electric hedge trimming is entirely safe with the right setup, but it is also entirely reasonable to decide you would rather someone else deals with it.
