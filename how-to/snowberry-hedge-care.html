Snowberry hedge care: pruning and managing Symphoricarpos

Snowberry is one of the most reliably dense informal hedging plants you can grow in a shaded or difficult spot. The berries feed thrushes and finches through the winter, the flowers are a quiet nectar source in summer, and the plant is genuinely unkillable once established. The catch: it spreads, and if you do not stay on top of the suckers, it will do so quite enthusiastically.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

What is snowberry?

Symphoricarpos albus (common snowberry) is a deciduous North American shrub that has been grown in British gardens since the eighteenth century. It is now naturalised in many parts of the UK, forming dense thickets in hedgerows, woodland edges and neglected gardens. The related Symphoricarpos x doorenbosii (a hybrid group sold under names like White Hedge, Magic Berry and Mother of Pearl) offers similar growth habits with slightly different berry colours, from white to pink.

In an East Kent garden context, snowberry is most often encountered as an inherited feature: it was planted decades ago as a low-maintenance informal hedge or as ground cover under trees, and has since spread considerably from its original position. Bringing it back into shape is straightforward; keeping it there requires annual attention.

When to prune snowberry

The main annual prune is in late winter, between February and early March, before the new growth begins. At this point you can see the structure of the plant clearly, the ground is workable, and the berries have usually been taken by birds or fallen naturally. Cut the previous season's growth back to a low framework of main stems, 20-40cm from the ground. Remove any suckers that have travelled beyond the area you want the plant to occupy.

A lighter tidy can be done after flowering in late summer if the plant is getting untidy. This is not a hard prune, just a shortening of stems that have grown beyond the hedge line. Avoid any hard pruning during nesting season (see below).

Managing the spread

Snowberry spreads by underground root suckers, which can emerge some distance from the parent plant. On the lighter, sandier soils around Sandwich and the Stour valley, suckering tends to be vigorous; on heavier clay ground it is usually more contained.

The most effective management is annual removal of suckers in late winter. Cut them off at or just below ground level with sharp secateurs or loppers. If a sucker has already developed into a substantial stem, follow it back to the point where it emerges from the root and cut it there. Running over suckers with a mower suppresses them temporarily but does not stop regrowth from the root; proper removal does.

If the plant has already spread well beyond its intended area, a sharp spade in late winter to cut through the root system at the boundary, followed by removal of all growth on the unwanted side, is usually sufficient. A second pass the following year to catch regrowth completes the job. Chemical treatment is rarely necessary for a healthy plant that has just spread; it makes more sense for old neglected stands with an extensive root system.

Renovation pruning

A snowberry hedge that has been left for many years without pruning typically has a dense tangle of stems at the top and bare, woody trunks at the base, with suckers spreading in all directions. Renovation is simple: cut the whole plant back hard to 20-30cm from the ground in late winter. The root system is large and the regrowth will be vigorous. Prune out suckers at the same time, returning the plant to its original footprint.

Flowering and berrying will be reduced in the first year after hard renovation, but the second season onwards is usually better than before, with denser growth lower down and more flowering stems.

Nesting season and snowberry

Dense established snowberry is popular with dunnock, blackbird and wren as nesting cover: the tangle of stems at ground level provides exactly the hidden, low structure these species prefer. Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, disturbing or destroying an active bird's nest is an offence. Nesting season runs from 1 March to 31 August. Any hard pruning or renovation work should be completed before the end of February if possible. If you find a nest while working between March and August, stop, and return after August when the nest is empty.

Snowberry in a wildlife hedge

The white berries of S. albus are mildly toxic to humans but eaten freely by birds, particularly thrushes (redwing and fieldfare in winter, song thrush year-round), finches and, in good irruption years, waxwing. They persist well into December and January, long after most other berry crops have gone, which makes snowberry a useful component of a mixed wildlife hedge.

The small pink-white flowers in summer, while not showy, provide useful nectar for bumblebees. In an East Kent garden with a mixed hedgerow containing privet, bramble, hawthorn or hazel, snowberry provides a reliable berry contribution without needing the most favourable soil or the most open position.

In the villages around Ash, Woodnesborough and Eastry, snowberry appears as an understorey plant in many of the older mixed hedgerows. It tolerates shade well, which is why it persists under taller hedge species where lighter-demanding plants would thin out.

Tools and PPE

  • Sharp secateurs for the main pruning. Long-handled loppers for older, thicker stems.
  • A sharp spade for cutting through spreading suckers at ground level.
  • Thick gloves: the stems are not spiny but the cut ends are sharp and the plant often grows in dense tangles.
  • Eye protection for any work in the interior of a dense thicket.
