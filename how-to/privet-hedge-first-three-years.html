Privet hedge: first three years

Ten points that determine whether your privet grows into a dense, even hedge or a leggy disappointment. The planting cut matters more than anything else.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Privet is the most common garden hedge in East Kent. Walk down almost any residential street in Sandwich, Deal or Worth and you will find one. It is fast, forgiving, and responds better to hard cutting than almost any other garden hedge. Planted and managed correctly, it reaches a presentable, dense appearance within two to three years. Planted and managed badly, it produces a tall, leggy screen with a bare base and an erratic top that gets worse rather than better with age.

The difference usually comes down to the first week of planting, particularly whether you cut it back hard enough at day one. Here is the full ten-point checklist for the first three years.

The ten-point checklist

  1. Choose your stock: bare-root (November to March) or pot-grown (year-round)
  2. Prepare the ground: clear a 60 cm strip, fork over and add organic matter on chalk soils
  3. Space plants at 30 to 45 cm in a single row for a formal hedge
  4. Cut back hard at planting: take privet down to 15 to 30 cm above ground
  5. Mulch the base immediately after planting
  6. Water once a week in dry spells from April to September in year one
  7. No trimming from 1 March to 31 August (nesting season)
  8. First formative trim: late August of year one, sides only
  9. Year two: June light trim (check for nests) and main August cut
  10. Year three: establish the full annual schedule (two or three cuts per year)

Nesting season: 1 March to 31 August

Privet is a popular nesting habitat for blackbirds, dunnocks and house sparrows. Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 it is an offence to intentionally damage or destroy an active nest. Nesting runs approximately 1 March to 31 August. Only trim between September and February, or after a careful check that the hedge is definitely empty. Never assume.

Before you start any cutting work

PPE and safety

Wear safety glasses or goggles (flying chippings are the main hazard), ear defenders with a powered trimmer, and sturdy gloves. For hedge heights above chest level, a pole trimmer is safer than a ladder: most garden accidents involving hedges happen from overreaching sideways on a stepladder. If a ladder is unavoidable, have someone footing it and never lean past the stile. Electric trimmer users: run the cable behind you and use an RCD adaptor. Before starting on any hedge, check for wasps' nests and trailing brambles growing through the base.

At planting: items 1 to 6

1. Choose your stock

Bare-root privet (Ligustrum ovalifolium) is cheaper and available from November to March. It is the most common type sold by UK hedge nurseries and perfectly suited to planting in East Kent's mild winters. Pot-grown privet can go in at any time of year, which is useful if you are planting in spring, but it costs more and needs more careful watering in summer. For a long run of formal hedging, bare-root is the economical choice; for a short section or an autumn fill-in, pot-grown saves time.

2. Prepare the ground

Clear a strip at least 60 cm wide along the planting line. Remove weeds, grass and any old root material. On the chalk soils common around Sandwich and the Stour valley, fork in a bucket of well-rotted compost or soil improver per metre run before planting: chalk drains fast, and the organic matter helps retain moisture during the critical first summer. On heavier clay soils, good drainage matters more than organic matter at this stage.

3. Spacing

For a formal clipped hedge, plant at 30 to 45 cm centres in a single row. Closer spacing fills in faster but costs more. A staggered double row at 45 cm centres in each row creates a denser base in a wider run. For a more informal, wildlife-friendly boundary, 45 to 60 cm centres in a single row is fine.

4. Cut back hard at planting

This is the most important point on the checklist, and the most often skipped. Take the stems down to 15 to 30 cm above the soil immediately after planting. It looks drastic. Do it anyway. Privet is one of the few hedge species that actively benefits from this treatment: the hard cut forces the plant to push new growth from the base rather than extending existing leggy stems upward. A privet planted and left untouched typically develops a bare, woody base by year three that cannot be recovered without a severe renovation cut. A privet cut back hard at planting develops dense, bushy growth from ground level and maintains a full base for decades.

5. Mulch the base

Apply a 7 to 10 cm layer of composted bark, wood chip or similar organic mulch along the planting strip, keeping it clear of the stems. Mulch does two things: it holds moisture (critical on chalk soils in summer) and suppresses competing weeds that would otherwise draw water and nutrients away from establishing plants.

6. Water in well on planting day

Give the entire planting strip a thorough soak after planting. For bare-root stock going in during winter, one good watering at planting is usually enough to establish them before spring. For pot-grown plants going in spring or summer, water every day for the first week.

Year one: items 7 and 8

7. Water once a week in dry spells

From April through September, water the hedge thoroughly once a week when no significant rain has fallen. The chalk downland around Sandwich Bay and the coastal strip near Deal dries out quickly in June and July, and even a fast-growing species like privet will slow dramatically if it runs short of water in its first summer. Aim for a good soak at the base rather than a surface sprinkle, which encourages shallow roots.

8. First formative trim: late August of year one

By late August, a well-watered privet planted in November or December will have put on a full season of new growth. Use hedge shears or a hedge trimmer to cut the sides back by roughly a third. Do not touch the top yet. The sides-only cut in year one achieves two things: it stimulates further lateral branching at the base, and it begins to establish the finished profile. Take the slight inward taper (batter) from the start: wider at the base, narrowing slightly toward the top. A hedge trained with a batter from day one maintains a fuller base as it ages.

Year two: item 9

9. June light trim, then main August cut

Privet is fast enough to need two cuts in year two. In June, once you have checked the hedge is free of active nests, give the sides a light pass to tidy the spring growth. This is not the main cut: take off no more than 5 to 8 cm and do not go into last year's wood. Then in late August, the main annual cut: sides and, for the first time, begin bringing in the top to your target height if the leaders have reached it. If not, leave the top for another season. Continue to build the slight batter angle with every cut.

Year three: item 10

10. Establish the full annual schedule

By year three, privet in an East Kent garden is typically putting on enough growth to justify two to three cuts a year. The main schedule is a June light trim (after the nesting check), a main cut in August, and an optional October tidy for fast-growing hedges in good growing seasons. Avoid cutting after October: late cuts on privet leave fresh green growth exposed to any sharp frosts, and while privet is semi-evergreen and not particularly frost-tender, keeping the final cuts to August and September means the hedge goes into winter with hardened growth.

By the end of year three, a correctly managed privet hedge should have a consistent height and width, a dense base with no significant gaps, and a finished profile that suits its setting. At this point, annual maintenance is straightforward: the formative work is done.
