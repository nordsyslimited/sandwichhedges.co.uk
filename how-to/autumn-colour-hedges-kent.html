Autumn colour hedges for East Kent: which deciduous species to plant

If you are thinking about a new hedge this autumn, now is the moment. Here is a quick comparison of which deciduous species give the best autumn colour in East Kent, so you do not end up with a wall of bare brown sticks in October when you could have had something better.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

At a glance: autumn colour by species

  • Beech (Fagus sylvatica): amber-bronze, holds through winter when clipped. Excellent on chalk. ★★★★
  • Copper beech (Fagus sylvatica 'Purpurea'): purple-bronze in summer, warm copper-orange in autumn. Same winter retention. ★★★★
  • Field maple (Acer campestre): warm yellow-orange, 2 to 3 weeks in October. Best autumn colour of any formal native hedge. ★★★★★
  • Guelder rose (Viburnum opulus): brilliant crimson, the most intense colour on this list. Also red berries for winter. ★★★★★
  • Spindle (Euonymus europaeus): vivid pink-red, plus flamboyant pink and orange fruit. Brief but spectacular. ★★★★
  • Dog rose (Rosa canina): red-orange, moderate. More valuable for hips than foliage colour. ★★★
  • Hawthorn (Crataegus monogyna): orange-red, good. Reliable in Kent and very common in mixed hedges. ★★★
  • Hornbeam (Carpinus betulus): pale yellow, holds leaves well in winter like beech but less vivid. Better than beech on heavy clay or wet soil. ★★★
  • Blackthorn (Prunus spinosa): yellow, unremarkable. Grown for sloes and thorns, not colour. ★★

About this list

All nine species on this list are native to Britain or naturalised here, and all grow well on East Kent soils. The ratings are a rough guide to intensity and duration, not a strict ranking: a hedge of pure field maple or guelder rose is genuinely striking in October, while a pure hornbeam hedge is more subdued. Most native mixed hedges contain several of these species together, and the combination is usually better than any single species alone.

Kent's mild coastal climate means that deciduous hedges tend to colour up a week or two later than hedges further north. Beech and field maple in the Sandwich area typically peak between mid-October and early November. The same species in Yorkshire might be past their best by the time East Kent's colour is at its height.

Beech and copper beech

Beech is the most practical choice if you want a formal clipped hedge that retains visual interest through winter. When cut as a hedge (rather than allowed to grow freely), beech exhibits marcescence: the dead leaves dry to a pale amber-brown and cling to the stems all winter, falling only when the new growth pushes them off in April. This means a beech hedge provides screening and structure from October to spring, which a hornbeam, hawthorn or field maple hedge does not.

Copper beech (Fagus sylvatica 'Purpurea') does the same thing, holding its leaves through winter in a warm copper and bronze. Its summer colour is deep purple-bronze, making it a strong contrast specimen in a mixed hedge. In a purely formal hedge it is striking year-round. The main consideration is that copper beech mixed with green beech in a formal hedge can look patchy unless you are intentional about the layout.

Both species prefer free-draining soil and full sun. The chalk and flint soils around Sandwich and Woodnesborough suit them well. The heavier clay soils of some East Kent gardens are less ideal, and on genuinely wet or heavy ground, hornbeam is a better substitute: it holds its leaves in winter almost as well, is more tolerant of wet conditions, and clips just as readily.

Pruning: cut once a year, in August. Cutting in August, after the main growth flush has matured, gives the leaves time to colour up properly in October. A May or June cut will push the plant into another growth flush, which is still green when October arrives.

Field maple

Field maple produces the most reliable warm autumn colour of any commonly clipped native hedging plant. In a good year, established field maple hedges in East Kent turn a clean yellow-orange in mid-October and hold the colour for two to three weeks before the leaves fall. The intensity varies year to year depending on how much sun preceded the colour change: a warm dry September followed by cool October nights gives the best result.

Field maple is our only truly native maple and is naturally common in Kent hedgerows on chalk and limestone. It clips well into formal shapes, responds to hard renovation without complaint, and has no thorns, which makes it practical for paths, driveways and boundaries where people walk close to the hedge. It is one of the more underused formal hedging choices in East Kent gardens given how well it performs here.

Pruning: August is ideal, but any time from August through to February is fine for a maintained formal hedge. Field maple is not fussy about timing in the way that flowering shrubs are.

Guelder rose

Guelder rose (Viburnum opulus) produces the most intense autumn foliage of anything on this list. The colour is a clear deep crimson, sometimes verging on scarlet, and it coincides with heavy clusters of translucent red berries that hang on into November and December. Those berries are a magnet for winter thrushes, particularly fieldfares and redwings that move through Kent on autumn migration.

It is a plant that works best in a mixed native hedge rather than as a formal clipped boundary. As a formal hedge it becomes ungainly and the berries are cut off before they form. As part of a mixed hedge alongside hawthorn, blackthorn, field maple and dog rose, it adds autumn colour, winter berries and May blossom (flat white lacecap flowers) to the seasonal sequence.

Guelder rose is more tolerant of wet conditions and heavy clay than many other native hedging plants, which is useful in East Kent gardens where the soil is heavy and poorly draining.

Pruning: cut in late October or November after the berries are largely gone, or defer to February if you want to extend the bird-food season. For maximum berry display, leave the whole plant uncut until February and simply remove dead and crossing branches.

Spindle

Spindle (Euonymus europaeus) is a native shrub that is criminally underused in East Kent gardens. In October it produces a brief but spectacular show: the four-lobed seed capsules split to reveal orange-coated seeds against a hot pink outer case, while the leaves turn a warm red-pink. The whole thing looks slightly too theatrical to be native, but it is, and it has been growing in Kent hedgerows on chalk soils since long before anyone was counting.

As a hedging plant it is best used in a mixed native hedge rather than as a formal boundary, partly because hard clipping removes the fruit and partly because it can be a little variable in growth rate. In a mixed hedge with hawthorn and field maple, two or three spindle specimens spaced 3 to 5 metres apart give autumn colour without dominating the overall shape.

Note: the seed capsules are toxic if eaten in quantity. This is rarely a concern for adults, but worth being aware of in gardens used by small children.

Dog rose, hawthorn and blackthorn

These three are the backbone of most East Kent mixed native hedges and all three turn colour in autumn, though none of them is planted primarily for that reason.

Dog rose (Rosa canina) turns a reasonable orange-red, but its main autumn interest is the hips: large, bright red fruits that persist into December and feed fieldfares, blackbirds and redwings. Hawthorn turns a reliable orange-red and its haws are consumed rapidly by birds in October. Both are valuable; the foliage colour is secondary to the fruit.

Blackthorn's foliage turns yellow and drops unremarkably. It is on the list because it is common and some people wonder about it, but nobody plants blackthorn for autumn colour. Its sloes are the interest in October.

Cutting timing for autumn-colour hedges

Nesting season and cutting timing

The UK nesting season runs from 1 March to 31 August. Most autumn-colour hedges should be cut in August or later, after the season ends. For hedges cut in late August or September, check carefully for late nests: some species (including robin and wood pigeon) may still be on second or third broods in August.

Cutting outside the safe window is not illegal per se, but cutting an active nest is an offence under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981. When in doubt, look before you cut.

Safety: autumn cutting

  • Eye protection: safety glasses, always.
  • Ear defenders: for any powered trimmer.
  • RCD adaptor: for any corded electric tool.
  • Wet leaves: leaves on the ground in October are slippery. Non-slip footwear, and take particular care on any slope.
  • Wasp nests: old wasp nests in hedges are inactive by October but check before putting your hand into a gap in dense cover.

For formal beech and field maple hedges: cut in August. For mixed native hedges containing guelder rose, dog rose and hawthorn: cut in October or November after the berry season is winding down, or in February to give the winter thrushes maximum access to the fruit. For a compromise cut on a mixed hedge, October is usually the practical choice.

Planting this autumn

The bare-root planting season opens from late October and runs through to March. October is slightly early: most bare-root stock is not dispatched until November when the plants are fully dormant. Pot-grown specimens can go in at any time, though the autumn and winter window gives the roots time to establish before the first growing season.

For a new hedge in East Kent, our recommended species mix for autumn colour alongside solid native-hedge value is: 60% hawthorn as the structural backbone, 20% field maple for formal colour, 10% guelder rose for the autumn berries and spring blossom, 10% other (dog rose, spindle, or blackthorn to taste). On chalk soils around Sandwich and Woodnesborough, add beech or field maple at a higher proportion; on wetter, clay-heavy ground, hornbeam in place of beech.
