Hand shears or powered trimmer: which to use on your hedge

Neither tool is universally better. Shears give a finish that trimmers rarely match; trimmers cover ground that would exhaust you with shears. Here is how working hedge cutters choose.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Most gardens have one trimmer and one pair of shears sitting in the shed. The question is never really which is better in the abstract, but which should you reach for today, with this hedge, in this condition. Both tools have situations where they outperform the other, and experienced hedge cutters use both in a single session on the right job.

At a glance: which tool for which situation

Situation Hand shears Powered trimmer
Box balls and topiary shapes ✓ Far better precision ✗ Too aggressive
Long privet run, 30 m+ ✗ Exhausting ✓ Clear choice
Hedge with ivy woven through ✓ Will not jam ✗ Ivy will jam blades
Formal yew hedge, fine finish ✓ Better finish ✓ Acceptable with sharp blades
Overgrown informal hedge, 1 m+ overdue ✗ Too slow ✓ Far faster
Wet foliage ✓ Fine Electric: RCD essential. Petrol: fine.
Hedges containing bramble ✓ Can see and avoid ✗ Risk of jamming and kickback

When hand shears are the better choice

Topiary, balls and pyramids. A powered trimmer struggles with the tight angles that shears handle naturally. On a box ball or a cone-trained yew, shears let you work a small section at a time, rotating around the shape gradually. A trimmer tends to cut too aggressively and can remove more than you intended before you notice.

Ivy-threaded hedges. Ivy stem is tough and stringy; it will jam trimmer blades almost immediately on contact. Shears let you see what you are cutting and work around the ivy rather than into it.

Very small hedges, under 3 m long. The setup time for a trimmer (fetching it from the shed, checking the blade, finding an extension lead or charging the battery) is not worth it for a hedge you can do with shears in ten minutes.

Box after blight. Individual stem cuts with shears reduce the risk of spreading box blight spores from plant to plant. See disinfecting hedge cutting tools for the full protocol.

When you want an instant finish. Shears leave the foliage cleanly cut with no brown stub ends. A trimmer leaves small brown tips that need a few days to dry and blend in before the hedge looks its best. For an event-critical trim, shears win.

When a powered trimmer is the better choice

Any long run of privet, laurel or leylandii over about 10 to 15 metres. The speed advantage is significant. A trimmer covers in 20 minutes what would take two hours with shears, and the fatigue difference on a 30 m run is not trivial.

Renovation cuts. When a hedge is a year or more overdue, the volume of growth and the thickness of the stems make shears impractical. A trimmer, or loppers for the thickest stems, is the right tool.

Working at height. A pole or long-reach trimmer lets you cut the top of a tall hedge while keeping your feet on the ground, which is considerably safer than leaning over from a ladder with shears. This is the main reason professional hedge cutters carry a pole trimmer.

When time is the main constraint. A trimmer is simply faster, and on the right hedge the finish is entirely acceptable.

Using both tools in the same session

The most practical approach on many Kent gardens is to use both. A typical scenario in Sandwich, Deal or Eastry: a 30 m beech hedge with a shaped topiary ball at the end. Use the trimmer along the flat run; switch to shears for the ball. Or a yew hedge with an informal privet section adjoining it at the boundary: trimmer on the privet, shears on the yew for a finer finish.

If the hedge is over 2 m tall and has been a source of disagreement with a neighbour, see hedge heights and neighbour law before cutting: tool choice matters less than knowing what height you can legally maintain.

Nesting season: applies to both tools

Under the UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs roughly from 1 March to 31 August. Neither shears nor a trimmer give you an exemption. The safe cutting window is September to February. Between March and August, check the hedge thoroughly before starting and do not cut if any nests are occupied.

Before you start: tool-specific safety

For hand shears: wear eye protection (clippings flick back), sturdy gloves. Check for wasp nests and brambles before starting.

For powered trimmers: eye protection is mandatory (flying debris carries much further with a trimmer than with shears). Ear defenders for petrol or electric trimmers. For corded electric trimmers, plug into a residual current device (RCD) before the trimmer, every time: an RCD cuts power within 30 ms of a cable fault, which can prevent electrocution. Never use a corded trimmer in wet conditions without an RCD in circuit. Use a pole trimmer rather than a ladder for hedge height work wherever the hedge height allows; if a ladder is unavoidable, have someone footing it and never overreach sideways.
