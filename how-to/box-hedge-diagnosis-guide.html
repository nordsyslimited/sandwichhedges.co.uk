What's wrong with my box hedge? A diagnosis and decision guide

Brown patches, webbing, dying stems: box hedges in East Kent are under more pressure than they were ten years ago. Here is how to work out what you are dealing with, and what to do next.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Box (Buxus sempervirens) has been in East Kent gardens for centuries. It clips tightly, holds a shape beautifully, and grows slowly enough to stay manageable. The problem is that two relatively recent arrivals, box blight (a fungal disease first recorded in the UK in the 1990s) and the box tree moth caterpillar (first reported in southern England around 2011), have made maintaining a box hedge considerably harder than it used to be.

Most struggling box hedges have one of a small number of identifiable problems. Working out which one, or which combination, is the first step before you spend time or money on treatment that may not match the cause.

Before you handle a sick hedge: basic precautions

Wear gloves when handling blight-affected stems: fungal spores can transfer on hands, clothing and tools. Disinfect cutting tools between plants using a dilute bleach solution or proprietary blade cleaner. Bin all removed material immediately rather than composting it. Wash hands and gloves after work.

Nesting season applies to remedial work too

Under the UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting runs roughly from 1 March to 31 August. Even remedial cutting on a diseased box hedge must be checked for active nests first. The safe window for most remedial cuts is September to February.

Step 1: identify the symptoms

Look at the hedge from the outside, then part the stems and look inside. The symptoms will usually point to one of four main causes.

Webbing among the stems, eaten or skeletonised leaves, green caterpillars

This is box tree moth caterpillar (Cydalima perspectalis). The caterpillars are pale green or yellow-striped, up to 4 cm long, and they eat leaves from the inside of the hedge outward, often leaving a papery skin on the leaf surface. You will see green frass (droppings) and sticky webbing binding the stems. See our guide to box tree moth caterpillar damage for full treatment detail.

Brown/white leaf patches, black or dark streaks on stems, leaves falling without webbing

This points strongly to box blight (caused by the fungus Cylindrocladium buxicola). Infected leaves develop tan to white patches with a darker border. Stems blacken and die back rapidly. In humid conditions you may see white fungal growth on the underside of affected leaves. There will be no webbing or caterpillars. See our box blight treatment guide for step-by-step management.

Salmon-pink or orange powdery masses on stems, die-back following stress

This is likely Volutella blight (caused by Pseudonectria buxi). It is less aggressive than Cylindrocladium box blight and typically follows a period of stress: drought, frost damage, heavy caterpillar attack or overfeeding with high-nitrogen fertiliser. The coral-coloured spore masses on stems are the distinguishing marker. Improving conditions (drainage, air flow, regular watering in dry spells) and cutting out affected material usually brings it under control.

General yellowing or browning with no obvious lesions, webbing or caterpillars

Suspect one of the following: drought stress (particularly in a dry summer on a hedge in a south-facing position or close to a wall), waterlogged soil, vine weevil damage to the roots, or simply old age in a very dense, poorly maintained specimen. Check the soil moisture at root level. If it is bone dry even after rain, the hedge may need irrigation in summer. If it is waterlogged despite dry weather, drainage is the problem. For vine weevil, check the soil around the base of the stems for small white grubs with an orange-brown head.

Step 2: assess how much is affected

Walk the whole hedge and estimate the proportion of the volume showing symptoms. This matters because the treatment decision changes significantly above 40% coverage.

  • Under 20% affected: treatment is likely to succeed. Cut out affected material, disinfect tools, improve air circulation, and follow the treatment protocol for the specific cause. Keep a close eye for further spread over the next six weeks.
  • 20–40% affected: treatment is worth attempting but will take sustained effort over two or three seasons. Start the treatment protocol immediately and set a clear review point: if coverage has not reduced by the following season, begin planning replacement.
  • Over 40% affected, or heavy repeated attacks with no recovery: the practical case for treatment weakens significantly. The cost in time, materials and the ongoing risk to neighbouring plantings means that replacement with a blight-resistant and caterpillar-resistant species is usually the better decision.

Step 3: check whether the soil itself is the problem

Box in Kent's chalky soils around Sandwich and Ash can suffer from nutrient lock-up in alkaline conditions. Yellowing from the leaf tips inward on otherwise healthy growth, with no sign of fungal lesions or caterpillar damage, can indicate magnesium deficiency. A dose of Epsom salts (magnesium sulphate) watered in at 20 g per litre in late spring sometimes resolves this quickly.

For vine weevil, dig around the base of a dying section and look for the characteristic grubs. Biological control with nematodes (Steinernema kraussei, applied to moist soil when temperatures are above 5°C) is the most effective approach for ornamental hedges. See our box hedge care guide for general soil management.

Step 4: make the decision

Use this as a rough guide after working through steps 1 to 3:

Treat if: the problem is clearly identified, coverage is under 40%, the plants still have healthy green growth in the majority of the hedge volume, and the soil and air circulation can be improved. Box responds well to hard remedial cuts in dry weather and can recover from surprisingly severe blight or caterpillar damage if caught early and treated consistently.

Replace if: coverage is over 40%, the hedge has failed to recover despite two or more seasons of treatment, or the problem is vine weevil plus blight in combination. There is no shame in this: many Kent gardens have moved from box to alternatives and found them more manageable. Good blight-resistant and caterpillar-resistant options include Ilex crenata (Japanese holly), Euonymus japonicus, Lonicera nitida and Pittosporum tenuifolium. Our guide to box hedge alternatives compares them in detail.
