Privet hedge summer trimming: the June and August light cuts

A quick-reference checklist for the trims that keep privet looking sharp through summer, with the one thing you must do before starting in nesting season.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Summer trim checklist: 9 points before you start

  1. Check the date: is it between 1 March and 31 August?
  2. Walk the hedge slowly and look up into the growth for nests
  3. Gather PPE: eye protection, gloves, ear defenders for a petrol or electric trimmer
  4. Lay a sheet along the base to catch clippings
  5. Run a string line if you need a guide, especially after a rainy period when growth is patchy
  6. Cut only the soft new growth from this season, stop at the darker woodier stems below
  7. Work top first, then sides, moving from the bottom of each side upward
  8. Keep the batter: base wider than top, so light reaches the lower growth
  9. Clear clippings the same day to prevent disease and to discourage mice

Privet is one of the fastest-growing hedge plants in Kent gardens. A row of privet along a Sandwich or Deal boundary can put on 30 to 45 centimetres in a good summer, which means one September cut rarely holds it all year. Most well-established privet hedges benefit from a light additional cut in late June and sometimes again in late August, before the main autumn trim.

The catch is nesting season. Privet is popular with blackbirds, robins and dunnocks as nesting cover precisely because it is dense and fast-growing. So the summer trim requires a specific check before you touch anything. Here is what each checklist item actually means.

The legal position: nesting season applies to privet

Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is a criminal offence to damage or destroy the nest of any wild bird while it is in use or being built. Nesting season runs roughly 1 March to 31 August. A summer trim on privet is only lawful if the hedge is demonstrably empty. That means you have actually checked, not assumed. If you find a nest with eggs or chicks, stop and wait. The safe main cutting window is September to February.

What each checklist step means

1. Check the date

If it is outside the 1 March to 31 August window, you do not need the nesting check for legal purposes, though it is always good practice. If it is inside that window, all subsequent steps apply before you pick up any tool.

2. Walk the hedge and look for nests

This takes five minutes and is not optional. Walk slowly along the hedge, looking into the growth from below and from each end. Watch for birds flying in and out repeatedly, particularly low down. Robin and wren nests are often at knee height. Blackbirds tend to nest slightly higher, one to two metres up. If a bird leaves the hedge in a hurry as you approach and then hovers nearby watching you, assume there is a nest. Come back in September.

3. PPE before tools

Before you start a hedge trimming job, however light: eye protection from flying debris, gloves (privet sap can irritate skin), and ear defenders if you are using a petrol or mains electric trimmer. For a short summer pass on privet, battery-powered trimmers are quieter and there is no trailing cable. If you use a mains corded trimmer outside, fit an RCD adaptor at the socket: cut through a cable under full power and the RCD cuts the circuit before the current can kill. Prefer a pole trimmer over a ladder for anything above shoulder height.

4 and 5. Sheet and string line

A sheet laid at the base saves clearing up later. A string line helps most on long privet runs where patchy summer growth can create a wave effect. Tie string to canes set at the height you want and use it as your eye guide for the top cut.

6. Cut only the soft new growth

This is the difference between a summer light trim and a main cut. New growth is pale green, leafy and flexible. The older wood beneath is darker and stiffer. Stop cutting when you hit that darker layer. For most privet hedges after eight to twelve weeks of summer growth, this means removing about 5 to 10 centimetres from the top and sides, no more.

7. Work top first, then sides, bottom to top on the sides

Cut the top first, then each side. On the sides, start at the bottom and work upward: this lets cut material fall away from the part you are still trimming rather than landing back on uncut growth. It also means you finish at the top of each pass, where you can step back and check the line.

8. Keep the batter

Batter means the cross-section is slightly wider at the base than at the top. Light reaches the lower shoots and the hedge stays dense from ground level, not just at the top. A summer trim is a good moment to correct any vertical faces that have developed, so the base is wider than before.

9. Clear clippings

Privet clippings left on the base can harbour fungal disease in warm wet summers. Clear them on the day, compost if there is no sign of disease, or bag them.

How the summer trim fits into the annual privet cycle

Most privet hedges in East Kent follow this rough pattern across the year:

  • January to February: leave alone unless doing a hard renovation cut. See our guide to general privet care for the full renovation approach.
  • March to May: do not cut. Nesting season begins. Even a light surface trim disturbs birds settled into the hedge.
  • Late June: first summer light trim if the hedge has grown noticeably. Check for nests first (see step 2 above).
  • Late August: second light trim if growth warrants it. Most birds have fledged but pigeons and late breeders may still be active, so check again.
  • September to October: main cut. The safe window opens. This is when you do the harder shaping work, reduce any sections that have grown oversized, and set the hedge up for winter.
  • November to December: a tidy pass on sections that broke dormancy late. Avoid hard cuts in this period as the new edges will not harden before frost.
