Planting hedges in chalk soil in Kent

The chalk and flint soils of the Kent Downs are thin, sharp and free-draining. They suit yew and beech beautifully. They will punish laurel in a dry summer. Know which is which before you order your plants.

Updated September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

East Kent has two quite different soil types. The low-lying ground around Sandwich, Worth, Ash and Woodnesborough is heavy clay: cold, wet in winter, slow to drain. The downland above Deal, the ridge running inland toward Barham and the high ground behind the white cliffs near Kingsdown is chalk: thin, pale, very free-draining and quick to dry out.

Yesterday we published a companion guide to planting hedges in clay soil. This one covers chalk. The problems are different, the solutions are different, and several of the best chalk hedging species are poor choices on clay, and vice versa.

Monty Don covered the principles of autumn planting and species preparation in his September Gardeners' World advice, a useful reference for the planting season ahead: Monty Don's Pruning and Planting Secrets, Gardeners' World Season 7 Episode 15 (Gardening With Monty Don, YouTube).

Before you start: safety and nesting season

Wear sturdy boots and gloves when planting thorny species like hawthorn or dog rose. If removing an old hedge before replanting, use eye protection and, if working with a powered trimmer or chainsaw, full PPE including ear defenders. Prefer a pole-mounted tool over a ladder wherever possible; if a ladder is needed, always have someone footing it.

If your planting site has an existing hedge or scrub: under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to damage or destroy an active nest. Nesting season runs roughly 1 March to 31 August. Clear ground between September and February; do not clear nesting habitat during the nesting season.

The challenge of chalk soils

Chalk soils have a paradox. They are usually alkaline and reasonably fertile. But the topsoil layer is thin, often only 15-30 cm before you hit chalk rock, and the rock beneath is porous. Rainfall passes through quickly. In a dry East Kent summer, a chalk garden can dry out in two to three days after rain.

This is the main problem for newly planted hedges on chalk: the roots have not yet penetrated into the deeper soil reservoir. During the plant's first summer, before it has developed enough root volume to handle extended dry spells, chalk soils can dry out fast enough to kill new hedging plants outright. The solution is to choose drought-tolerant species, prepare the soil to hold more moisture, and water reliably for the first two summers.

Step 1: Choose the right species

Yew is the outstanding formal hedging species for chalk. It is native to the chalk downlands of southern England and has grown on Kent chalk for millennia. Yew is lime-loving, tolerates the alkaline pH of chalk well, and once established is remarkably drought-resistant. The beech and yew hedges in the old gardens at Eastwell Park and on the high ground above Barham are testament to what yew does on chalk over the long term. See our yew hedge care guide.

Beech is the first choice for a formal deciduous hedge on chalk. It is a chalk downland tree: the great beech hangers of the Hampshire Downs, the North Downs beeches and the old hedgerow beeches in the Barham and Elham valleys all grow on chalk or chalk-derived soils. Beech holds its rust-brown dead leaves through winter, making it visually appealing year-round. On chalk, beech outperforms hornbeam (which prefers clay). See our beech hedge care guide.

Privet is underestimated on chalk. It is drought-tolerant, grows quickly and is very forgiving of poor, thin soils. For a garden hedge on chalk that needs to establish quickly and cheaply, privet is an excellent choice. It is semi-evergreen and needs two or three cuts a year, but it copes with chalk conditions better than most people expect. See our privet hedge care guide.

Hawthorn, field maple and dog rose are all excellent native chalk hedging plants. The old hedgerows of the Kent Downs are largely composed of these three. For a mixed native hedge on chalk they are the obvious starting point, and they will establish without difficulty if planted in autumn with reasonable aftercare.

Box thrives on free-draining, moderately alkaline soils. On chalk it can grow well if moisture is not too limited. The main concern with box in East Kent is box blight (Cylindrocladium buxicola), which is widespread across the county regardless of soil type. See our box blight treatment guide before committing to a box hedge on any soil.

Species to be cautious about on chalk

Cherry laurel and Portuguese laurel prefer moisture-retentive, fertile soil. On thin chalk they grow slowly and can show yellowing (lime-induced chlorosis) in dry summers. They will survive, but they will not be impressive. If you want an evergreen hedge on chalk, yew will serve you far better over the long term.

Hornbeam performs best on clay or neutral loam. On chalk, particularly thin chalk with low moisture retention, it tends to be slow and prone to dry-season stress. Choose beech instead for a formal deciduous hedge on chalk.

Photinia, griselinia and escallonia all prefer richer, more moisture-retentive soils. On thin chalk they can establish but will not be at their best. If the chalk is reasonably deep and your soil has a reasonable organic content, griselinia and escallonia in a sheltered position can work, but they are not the first choice.

Step 2: Prepare the chalk ground

On chalk, the priority is increasing the soil's ability to retain moisture and improving the topsoil depth. You cannot add more chalk depth, but you can improve the zone roots will grow into during the first two or three years.

Mark your planting line and dig a trench 45 cm wide and 40 cm deep. On shallow chalk, the trench may hit solid chalk rock at 20-25 cm. This is normal. Break up the chalk surface at the base of the trench with a fork or digging bar to allow roots to penetrate downward.

Work a generous quantity of well-rotted compost, garden compost or leaf mould into the full trench. On chalk, unlike clay, you can be more generous with organic matter in the planting pocket because drainage is not a concern. Three or four spadefuls of compost per metre run of trench is not too much. Work it in thoroughly through the upper 25-30 cm.

Do not add fertiliser at planting time. Chalk soils are usually reasonably fertile, and overfeeding new hedge plants pushes leaf growth at the expense of root development, which is the opposite of what you want in year one.

Step 3: Plant correctly

Bare-root planting from late October to February is ideal on chalk, exactly as on other soil types. The autumn and winter rains provide free irrigation during the critical establishment period. See our full bare-root hedge planting guide for the complete planting method.

On chalk, pay particular attention to backfilling. Chalk crumbles and the soil can look firmly packed when it is not. Firm the soil around the roots in layers as you backfill, and firm with your boot after the trench is filled. A plant that rocks in the ground will not establish well.

Set plants at the same depth as they were growing in the nursery (indicated by the soil stain on the stem). On chalk, resist the temptation to plant shallow to avoid hitting chalk rock: even if the roots are confined to a narrow zone at first, they will find their way down through chalk fissures. Plant at the correct depth and let the roots do their work.

Step 4: Mulch heavily

On chalk, mulching is not optional. A 10 cm layer of bark chippings, wood chip or straw along the full planting run, kept clear of the plant stems, can double the amount of moisture retained in the soil surface layer. This is the single most effective thing you can do for a new hedge on chalk.

Lay the mulch immediately after planting. Maintain it through at least the first two growing seasons. On chalk, even an established mulch will need topping up each autumn as organic matter breaks down into the soil, but this breakdown is actually beneficial: it slowly increases the topsoil depth and organic content of the chalk ground year by year.

Step 5: Water in dry spells

Water is the critical variable on chalk. New hedge plants on chalk need reliable watering through their first two dry seasons, even in a normal Kent summer. East Kent has relatively low rainfall (around 600-650 mm per year, similar to parts of East Anglia) and chalk soils drain fast: the combination means newly planted hedges can be in drought stress within a week of dry weather.

Water deeply and infrequently: a full watering can or a slow trickle from a hose for several minutes per plant, once or twice a week in dry weather, is better than a light daily sprinkle. Water at the base of the plants, not overhead. In a dry spring or summer, keep watering through the first three years, not just the first; chalk soils do not become moisture-retentive quickly.

Step 6: Formative pruning

Formative cutting in the first two or three years builds the dense, branching structure that makes a good hedge. For yew and beech on chalk, follow the same formative programme as on other soils: cut back by a third at planting to encourage bushing out, then follow a year-by-year regime. See our formative hedge pruning guide for the detail.

On chalk, do not rush formative cutting. Drought-stressed plants in the first summer should not also be hard-pruned; if a dry summer has clearly stressed your new hedge, skip the formative cut that year and let the plants recover.
