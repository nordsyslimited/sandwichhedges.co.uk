Hedge cutting near overhead power lines and buried services

A hedge that has grown into overhead cables or planted above buried services creates a different kind of problem to a straightforward overgrown hedge. Here is what the rules are, who is responsible, and what to do.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981: nesting season

Whatever the reason for the hedge job, it is an offence to damage an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs roughly from 1 March to 31 August. If a cable or utility issue means work cannot wait until the safe cutting window, check the affected section of hedge carefully before any cutting begins. The cable clearance issue does not override the nesting-season rule.

Overhead electricity cables: the basics

In residential streets across East Kent, overhead cables run at varying heights and carry varying voltages. The high-voltage transmission lines carried on large pylons are obvious and typically well clear of garden hedges. The cables more likely to concern householders are the low-voltage distribution lines that run along back lanes and between properties, and the service connections that run from a pole or wall to a house or outbuilding.

These low-voltage cables are usually insulated, but the insulation on older cables degrades. A cable that looks intact from the ground may not be. This matters because electricity does not need direct contact to cause a serious injury: at short range, arcing is possible, and touching a tool to a cable that carries even low household voltage is potentially fatal.

The practical rule is this: trim the parts of the hedge that are clearly well clear of any cable, and stop when you reach the part of the hedge that is in or near the cables. The safe working distance from an overhead distribution cable with no special equipment is at least 3 metres. If the hedge has grown to within that distance of the cable, the cutting work in that section is not safe for a homeowner or a standard contractor to carry out.

What to do if the hedge has grown into the cables

Contact UK Power Networks, the electricity network operator for Kent and most of the South East. Their vegetation management team handles exactly this situation. You can report a problem online or call their network operations line on 0800 028 8247 (free from a landmobile or landline, charged from some networks). They will assess whether the cables need to be temporarily de-energised or whether the configuration allows safe clearance work by an approved contractor with the right equipment.

Do not wait until the problem is severe. A hedge that is touching cables creates risk of a short-circuit fault and may already be causing intermittent supply issues. Reporting it early, even if the situation looks borderline, is always the right call.

There is no charge to the householder for UK Power Networks to assess the situation, though there may be a charge if you have to arrange a private registered electrical contractor for the service cable to your property specifically (as opposed to the network cable on the street). Your network operator will advise you on which category the cable falls into.

Overhead service cables to outbuildings

A number of older properties in villages like Ash, Worth, Woodnesborough and Eastry have overhead service cables running from the house to a garage, workshop or other outbuilding, at heights that can be quite low. If a hedge has grown up between the house and outbuilding, these cables can become entangled in the hedge canopy.

These service cables are your responsibility as the property owner. If the cable has become entangled in the hedge, contact a registered electrician (a contractor on the NICEIC or NAPIT register) before attempting any cutting. The electrician can isolate the circuit and assess whether the cable itself needs replacing. Do not attempt to cut the hedge and hope the cable stays clear of the blades: a hedge trimmer blade contacting a live service cable is a serious risk of electrocution.

Buried services: cables, gas and water

The risks below ground are less dramatic but can still be serious. Buried electricity cables, gas pipes, water mains and telecoms ducts can all run through or alongside a hedge boundary, particularly on older properties where utilities predate the hedge and where there may be no accurate records of routing.

If you are planning any digging at the hedge base, whether for root clearance, replanting, or installing fencing or edging, check for buried services first. The free service to use in the UK is Linesearch Before U Dig (LSBUD), which provides information from utility companies on their known assets in a given area. Register online (lsbud.co.uk), submit the location, allow at least 48 hours, and act on what you are sent before any digging starts.

LSBUD data is not a guarantee that nothing is there: records are incomplete, utilities from previous decades may not be mapped, and some private service runs are not on any public register. If you are on an older plot in the Sandwich or Deal area and you have any uncertainty at all, hand-dig the first 30 cm before using any mechanical tool.

When LSBUD identifies a cable or pipe nearby, use hand tools only within 500 mm of the marked line. No powered tools, no garden forks. This applies to a hedge trimmer being used with the blades near ground level as well as to digging equipment.

What we can and cannot do for you

We will trim the sections of hedge that are safely clear of overhead cables and will not approach areas where cables are within safe working distance. We will tell you clearly when a section of hedge needs utility involvement before it can be cut safely, and we will not bill for work we cannot safely complete.

If you have asked us to quote on a hedge and we have identified a cable clearance issue on a site visit, we will let you know what needs to happen before we start and can recommend who to contact. We do not carry the specialist equipment or authorisation required for live-cable proximity work, and we do not pretend otherwise.
