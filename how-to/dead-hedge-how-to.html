How to make a dead hedge: turning garden trimmings into wildlife habitat

A dead hedge turns the heap of cuttings and prunings from your autumn hedge trim into a structure that shelters hedgehogs, wrens, slow worms and a long list of beetles. Here is what you need and how to build one.

Updated September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

In East Kent, an autumn hedge cut on a garden with 30 or 40 metres of hedge generates more green waste than most people know what to do with. Most of it goes in refuse sacks to the tip, or into a green waste bin if the council collects it. Either way, the material is lost from the garden.

A dead hedge is the simplest way to turn that material into something useful. It does not require any particular skill, it uses material you already have, and it creates a habitat structure that the surrounding garden benefits from for years. This guide covers what you need, where to put it and how to build it.

Nesting season note

Building a new dead hedge involves driving stakes into the ground, which is generally low-risk during nesting season. However, if you are building adjacent to an existing hedge, scrub or dense vegetation, check for active nests before disturbing the ground. Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, nesting season runs from 1 March to 31 August. September onwards is ideal for starting or adding to a dead hedge, since the main hedge-cutting season also begins then.

The checklist: what you need before you start

  • A supply of woody prunings and hedge cuttings (at least a couple of barrowloads to start)
  • Wooden stakes, 1.2 to 1.5 metres long and at least 4 cm diameter (hazel, sweet chestnut or any straight hardwood; avoid conifer stakes which rot quickly)
  • A mallet or post driver
  • Thick gardening gloves (essential if you are handling blackthorn, hawthorn or bramble)
  • A tape measure or cane for spacing
  • Optional: twine or wire to lash the stakes at the top if the structure needs extra rigidity

You do not need to buy premium materials. Stakes cut from a hazel or ash coppice are ideal. If you are cutting hedges anyway, keep any straight, thick stems from hawthorn or blackthorn as stake material.

Step 1: choose a site

A dead hedge works best:

  • Along an existing boundary, fence line or the edge of a lawn, where it defines a space naturally
  • In a slightly shaded or semi-shaded spot, where it retains moisture and breaks down at a useful rate
  • Away from buildings, to avoid giving rats a sheltered corridor close to the house
  • Within reach of the spots where you generate most of your pruning material, so you do not have to carry it far

In the smaller gardens of Sandwich Old Town and the Deal side streets, a dead hedge along the back wall or beside a fence can replace a compost bay while providing significantly more wildlife value. In larger gardens around Worth, Ash and Eastry, a dead hedge along a field boundary or at the edge of a paddock is a practical way to deal with larger volumes of material.

Step 2: mark out the lines and drive the stakes

A dead hedge uses two parallel rows of stakes with a gap between them, filled with loose material. The width of the gap determines how thick and how long-lasting the structure will be.

For a garden dead hedge: space the two rows of stakes 40 to 60 cm apart. This gives a structure wide enough to hold material well but narrow enough to fit a typical garden boundary.

For a dead hedge on a larger boundary: a gap of 60 to 90 cm creates a more substantial structure that holds more material and lasts longer between top-ups.

Drive the stakes 30 to 40 cm into the ground. Spacing between stakes in each row should be about 50 to 70 cm: close enough to hold material in, wide enough to be able to push material through easily. Drive them in straight using a mallet or post driver. For chalk and flint subsoil around Sandwich and Deal, you may need to pre-make the hole with an iron bar or a crowbar before driving the stake.

Step 3: fill with prunings

Start filling from one end, working along the length. Lay prunings loosely between the two rows of stakes, oriented roughly lengthwise. Mix thicker stems with thinner, twiggy material: the thicker stems create air pockets that beetles, solitary bees and small mammals will use; the finer material fills gaps and breaks down to create a soil-building layer at the base.

Do not pack tightly. The value of a dead hedge as habitat depends on there being cavities and spaces within the structure. Fill loosely, top up with more material, and let gravity settle things over time.

Aim for a finished height of around 80 cm to 1 metre when freshly filled. The structure will settle to 60 or 70 cm over the first season.

Step 4: what to add (and what to leave out)

Suitable material:

  • Hawthorn, blackthorn, field maple, hazel and mixed native hedge cuttings (excellent material)
  • Bramble canes (handle with thick gloves)
  • Fruit tree and shrub prunings
  • Thicker stems from previous dead hedges that have broken down
  • Autumn leaf fall mixed with twiggy material

Leave out:

  • Diseased material: box blight cuttings, fire blight-affected material, anything with active fungal infection. Bin these rather than adding to any garden structure.
  • Yew prunings where children, dogs or other animals have access. Yew is highly toxic.
  • Japanese knotweed: a Schedule 9 invasive species. Any part of the plant can generate new growth. It must be disposed of as controlled waste, not added to a dead hedge or compost.
  • Conifer prunings in large quantities: these are slow to break down and acidify the soil as they do. A small amount mixed into the structure is fine, but a dead hedge made mostly of conifer cuttings will last a very long time without enriching the soil beneath it.

Step 5: maintain and top up

As the lower layers break down and the structure settles, add fresh material each autumn. This keeps the dead hedge at a useful height and continually renews the habitat within it. Over time, the base of the structure becomes a layer of rich, dark material similar to leaf mould. Gently rake this out from the bottom edge each spring and use it as a mulch or soil improver on nearby beds.

After three to five years, some of the original stakes will have rotted at ground level. Replace them one at a time as needed rather than taking down the whole structure: this avoids disturbing any overwintering animals using the base of the dead hedge.

Wildlife that uses a dead hedge

The main residents and visitors in an East Kent garden:

  • Hedgehogs: use the base of the dead hedge for shelter and occasionally for hibernation in November to March. This is one of the best reasons to build the structure in September, giving hedgehogs time to find it before the cold sets in.
  • Wrens: one of the most enthusiastic users. A wren will work every inch of a dead hedge hunting insects and spiders, and may occasionally nest in a well-established structure.
  • Dunnocks: similarly at home in a dead hedge, foraging in the base layer for invertebrates.
  • Slow worms: use the warmth retained in the base of the structure, particularly in gardens with sandy or well-drained soil near the coastal strip.
  • Stag beetles and other saproxylic beetles: the decaying wood at the base is prime larval habitat. These are declining species in Britain and a dead hedge is one of the most straightforward things a gardener can do to support them.
  • Solitary bees and wasps: use cavities in the structure for nesting, particularly in the drier conditions of an established dead hedge.
