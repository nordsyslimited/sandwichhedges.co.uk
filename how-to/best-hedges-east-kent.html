Best hedges for East Kent: choosing the right species for your site

The right hedge for a garden in Sandwich, Deal or the East Kent villages depends on the soil, the exposure and what the hedge needs to do. Here is a practical guide to the main species options, matched to the conditions you are likely to have.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Quick picks by site type

  • Chalk soil, formal garden: beech, yew
  • Clay soil (Woodnesborough, Eastry, Stour valley): hornbeam, field maple
  • Coastal exposure (Sandwich Bay, Deal seafront strip): privet, escallonia, hawthorn
  • Mixed soil, any aspect: hornbeam, privet, hawthorn
  • Wildlife-friendly native hedgerow: hawthorn, blackthorn, field maple, dog rose
  • Formal low box edging (not blighted sites): box (Buxus sempervirens), or ilex crenata as an alternative where box blight is a risk

East Kent's gardening conditions are more varied than the county's modest size suggests. The chalk downland running south and east from Sandwich sits above free-draining alkaline soil that suits native chalk-land species. The Stour valley floor around Sandwich itself and the lower ground through Woodnesborough and Eastry carries heavy clay, with seasonal waterlogging. The coastal strip, particularly Sandwich Bay and the seafront gardens of Deal, faces salt wind. Each condition calls for different hedging. The quick-picks above are a starting point; the sections below give the reasoning behind them.

Chalk downland sites

The chalk ridge running through the countryside east of Canterbury toward Folkestone and the North Downs of East Kent creates a distinctive gardening context: free-draining, alkaline soil that dries quickly in summer but supports a remarkable range of native plants. For formal hedging on chalk, beech (Fagus sylvatica) is the natural choice. It is native to chalk downland, produces a dense formal hedge with the distinctive retained winter leaves (marcescence), and grows to whatever height is required. Yew is the premium alternative: extremely dense, long-lived, and deeply adapted to alkaline chalk. It is slower than beech to establish but requires less frequent cutting once it reaches its intended size.

For a less formal mixed hedge on chalk, hawthorn is reliable and native. It tolerates thin chalk soils that would stress hornbeam and provides excellent wildlife value: berries, nesting cover, insect forage. See the guide on chalky soil hedges in Kent for the full range of options on this soil type.

Clay soil sites: Woodnesborough, Eastry and the Stour valley

The low-lying ground around Sandwich, following the Stour eastward and through Woodnesborough, Eastry and the lower ground near Ash, is primarily heavy clay. This soil holds water in winter and can crack in a dry summer. It is demanding for many hedging species but excellent for hornbeam (Carpinus betulus). Hornbeam is a native broadleaf species that tolerates wet, poorly drained ground where beech would struggle. It produces a formal dense hedge with a pleasing winter outline: the retained leaves are lighter in colour than beech marcescence, more of a pale buff than a warm tan.

Field maple (Acer campestre) is another strong choice on clay: it is native to this part of Kent, grows quickly, and produces an attractive hedge with reliable autumn colour. For a formal evergreen hedge on heavy clay, laurel (Prunus laurocerasus) tolerates wetter soils better than most conifers and establishes quickly from pot-grown plants.

Coastal exposure: Sandwich Bay, Deal and the coastal strip

The exposed gardens east of Sandwich, particularly Sandwich Bay and the linear properties along the Deal coastal road, face salt-laden onshore winds that damage many ornamental hedging species. Privet (Ligustrum ovalifolium) is the workhorse hedge for these conditions: it is semi-evergreen in mild Kent winters, grows quickly, and is genuinely tolerant of coastal salt. It needs two cuts a year to look respectable but is otherwise unfussy. Escallonia is a popular alternative for coastal positions: it is tougher against wind than many evergreens, produces small pink or red flowers through summer, and tolerates clipping reasonably well.

For a windbreak hedge rather than a formal boundary, hawthorn and blackthorn are both reliable on the coastal strip. Blackthorn (Prunus spinosa) is particularly effective as a stock-proof or security hedge and handles the full force of East Kent's north-easterly winds. Avoid leylandii on exposed coastal sites: it is not salt-tolerant and the windward face browns reliably in exposed positions. Beech also struggles on the most exposed coastal strip, though it manages well a kilometre or two inland.

Wildlife hedgerow planting

A mixed native hedgerow planted for wildlife rather than formal containment suits the full range of East Kent soils. The core mix for most sites is hawthorn (around 60% by number), field maple (20%) and blackthorn (10%), with the remaining 10% adapted to the specific site: dog rose (Rosa canina) for wildlife value, hornbeam for clay-heavy sections, beech for chalk sections. This species-mix approach creates the structural variety that maximises nesting sites, food sources and invertebrate habitat.

For bare-root hedgerow planting in East Kent, the season runs from November to February: hedges planted in this window establish well and require minimal watering. The mild coastal winters around Sandwich and Deal mean the soil rarely freezes hard enough to prevent planting through December and January, extending the practical window usefully.

Box: still worth planting?

Box blight (Cylindrocladium buxicola) is now sufficiently widespread in East Kent that planting new box hedging in a garden where the disease has previously appeared is a real risk. For formal low edging, ilex crenata (Japanese holly) is increasingly used as a blight-resistant alternative: it looks broadly similar to box, tolerates clipping, and is widely available from Kent nurseries. For a garden where box has never shown blight problems, Buxus sempervirens is still a viable choice for formal edging hedges, particularly in a drier, sheltered position with good air circulation. See the separate guide on box blight treatment for the full disease context and management options.
