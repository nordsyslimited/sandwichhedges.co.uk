Tapestry hedges in Kent: mixing species for year-round colour and wildlife

A tapestry hedge mixes four or more species in the same boundary to give you changing colour, texture and flower through every season. Here is how it differs from a formal single-species hedge and a native wildlife hedge, which plants work best together in East Kent, and how to manage the result.

Updated September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

The idea behind a tapestry hedge is straightforward: instead of planting one species from end to end and clipping it to a uniform surface, you plant several species in repeating groups and manage them together as a single, shared boundary. The result is a hedge that looks different every few metres and changes character with the seasons.

In practice this means hawthorn next to field maple next to beech next to hornbeam, perhaps with a section of blackthorn or holly for contrast. In summer the hedge reads as a textured green wall. In autumn the maples and beeches turn gold and orange while the hawthorn reddens with berries. In winter the beech and hornbeam hold their russet dead leaves, giving structure through the bare months. In spring the hawthorn is first into white blossom.

You often see this kind of boundary in old East Kent lanes without realising it is a design choice. The roadside hedges between Worth and Ash, or along the lanes behind Sandwich Golf Links, frequently mix five or six native species that have been managed together for centuries. A garden tapestry hedge applies the same principle deliberately.

Before you plant or convert an existing hedge, it helps to understand how a tapestry hedge actually compares to the alternatives.

Which species to include

The best tapestry hedges in East Kent contain three to five native deciduous species, with one evergreen component for winter structure. Here is how to think through the choice:

The backbone species

Hawthorn (Crataegus monogyna) is the most reliable backbone plant. It grows at a consistent pace, tolerates very hard cutting, produces masses of white blossom in May and red berries from September onwards, and it supports hundreds of species of invertebrate. Include it at 30 to 40% of your planting.

Blackthorn (Prunus spinosa) is an excellent companion. It is a little faster than hawthorn, very thorny, and produces white blossom in March before the leaves open. The sloes ripen purple-black in October. Include it at 20 to 30%.

Colour and autumn interest

Field maple (Acer campestre) brings the best autumn colour of any native hedging plant: gold to orange from late September on chalk soils, sometimes holding into November in mild years. It grows at a similar pace to hawthorn. Include it at 15 to 20%.

Hazel (Corylus avellana) adds a different texture: large, rounded leaves and early catkins in February that are among the first pollen sources of the year. It is faster-growing than hawthorn and may need slightly harder pruning to keep in balance. Include at 10 to 15%.

Winter structure

Without an evergreen element, a fully deciduous tapestry hedge becomes quite transparent from November to March. One in five plants as holly (Ilex aquifolium) solves this. Holly grows slowly, which means it may look undersized against faster neighbours in years one to three, but it catches up. Alternatively, use hornbeam (Carpinus betulus), which is technically deciduous but holds its dead leaves through winter in a way that provides useful screening even when bare.

Avoid mixing box (Buxus sempervirens) into a tapestry hedge in Kent at this time. Box blight (Cylindrocladium buxicola) is endemic in much of Kent and near-impossible to eliminate once established. If you want evergreen formality, use holly or Portugal laurel for a section instead.

Optional additions for longer runs

For a boundary longer than about fifteen metres, consider adding dog rose (Rosa canina) at 5 to 10% of the planting. It produces pink flowers from June, excellent hips from September to February, and it threads itself through the supporting species attractively. It does require management to keep it in balance since it can be vigorous. Spindle (Euonymus europaeus) is another option: unremarkable in summer but extraordinary in October when it opens its vivid pink and orange capsules. A few spindles in a tapestry hedge are worth having purely for that fortnight.

Proportions and planning

The commonest mistake is mixing species in random order, which produces a patchy, uneven result. The better approach is to plant in repeating groups of three to five of each species, so the hedge has a rhythm. A simple pattern for a ten-metre boundary might run: three hawthorn, two field maple, three blackthorn, two holly or hornbeam, then repeat. At twenty metres you have a complete sequence twice over, which reads as designed rather than accidental.

For a very long boundary, resist the temptation to include more species to add variety. Four or five species mixed well beats eight species mixed randomly. Each species you add increases the management complexity slightly, because each has its own preferred trimming time and growth rate.

Spacing is the same as for any native hedge: plant at two to three plants per metre for a single row. If you want a denser, wider boundary, use a double staggered row at four to five plants per metre, with the back row offset by half the in-row spacing. Double rows are genuinely better for wildlife but require a wider strip.

Managing the growth rate difference

The practical challenge with a tapestry hedge is that hazel and blackthorn grow faster than hawthorn, which grows faster than holly. If you trim everything to the same line at the same time, the slower species gradually get shaded out by their faster neighbours.

Two techniques keep things in balance. The first is to cut the fast-growing species (particularly hazel) slightly harder than the slower ones in the first three or four years. You are aiming for a hedge where all the species reach the same target height at roughly the same time, so the faster ones need more restraint early on. The second is to allow the holly a little extra height in the first few years. Let it grow slightly proud of the line until it has matched the volume of its neighbours, then bring it back to the shared profile.

Once the hedge is established, a single annual cut in August to September, after nesting season has ended, is sufficient to keep everything in balance. You can add a light tidy in February before the new growth starts, taking only the longest shoots, if you want a particularly neat winter appearance.

Safety and the law before trimming

PPE: Eye protection and gloves are essential when using any powered trimmer. Ear defenders for petrol or mains-electric tools. Check for wasp nests along the base before starting. For sections above chest height, use a pole/long-reach trimmer rather than a ladder where possible.

Electric tools: Always use an RCD on the circuit. Keep the cable behind you. Prefer a battery or petrol trimmer to remove the cable hazard on longer runs of hedge.

Nesting season (Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981): It is an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs from 1 March to 31 August. The safest cutting window is September to February. Do not trim a tapestry hedge in summer unless you have checked visually that no active nests are present.

Converting an existing single-species hedge

If you already have an established privet, leylandii or laurel hedge that you want to convert to a tapestry, you face a choice. The clean option is to remove the existing hedge completely and replant from scratch with a tapestry mix. This takes time but gives a uniform result.

A gentler approach is to introduce new species at intervals along the existing hedge as you progressively take it down. Cut a section of the old hedge back hard in January, plant native bare-root whips into the gap, and let the new whips establish before cutting the next section. This staged conversion takes three to four years but maintains some screening throughout and is less disruptive.

Do not try to weave new plants into a dense existing hedge without cutting it back first. The established plants will shade out any new arrivals before they can compete. Space and light are more important than the new plant's intrinsic vigour.

Kent specifics

East Kent's chalk soils suit the core tapestry species well. Hawthorn, field maple and blackthorn all thrive on chalk. Hazel prefers slightly moister, deeper soil and performs best in the clay areas around Worth, Ash and Woodnesborough rather than on the thinner chalk above Sandwich and Deal, though it will establish on chalk given good preparation and watering through the first season.

The chalk downland immediately behind Sandwich Bay and along the ridge above Deal is dry in summer. On those sites, avoid hazel as the dominant species and lean more heavily on hawthorn, field maple and blackthorn, all of which handle summer drought well once established. Holly is slower than usual on exposed chalk sites but eventually catches up.

The coastal exposure at Sandwich Bay calls for a windbreak component in the planting. Blackthorn is the toughest of the standard tapestry species in exposed positions and should be at 40 to 50% of the mix on any boundary within half a mile of the coast. Hawthorn handles coastal exposure well too. Field maple is less tolerant of salt wind and should be kept at lower proportions or positioned on the leeward side of the boundary if the planting is exposed.
