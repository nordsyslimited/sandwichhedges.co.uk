Slow worms and reptiles in your hedge

Slow worms, grass snakes and common lizards are all found in East Kent gardens. All three are legally protected, and the hedge base is often where they live. Here is how to attract and avoid harming them.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Most hedge trimming guides focus entirely on what you cut. This one focuses on what lives at the base of the hedge, specifically the legless lizard that gets mistaken for a snake, found coiled under a stone or in the leaf litter along a sunny hedge base, and immediately frightened to death by the strimmer.

Slow worms (Anguis fragilis) are not snakes. They are legless lizards, smooth-scaled, bronze-brown, with a blunt tail, and they are perfectly harmless. They eat slugs. They are legally protected. They are a useful garden resident that most people have never knowingly seen, partly because they hide well and partly because habitat management has steadily removed the places they live.

East Kent gardens near Sandwich, Ash and the rougher boundaries of Worth and Eastry still hold good slow worm populations. A well-managed hedge base is exactly the kind of habitat they need.

Legal protection for all UK reptiles

Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, all three native reptile species commonly found in Kent gardens are legally protected. Slow worms, grass snakes and common lizards cannot be deliberately killed, injured or taken. Adders share this protection. This means that if you find any of these animals during hedge or garden work, you must stop work in that area and wait for the animal to move away, or move it gently and carefully to a safe patch of vegetation. Strimming through a known reptile habitat without checking is not a defence.

Step 1: Know what you might have

Before you manage your hedge base for reptiles, it helps to know which species are realistic for your garden.

Slow worm: by far the most likely in a garden setting. Looks like a small snake, 30 to 50 cm, smooth bronze skin with a faint dark stripe in females. Found under flat stones, in compost heaps, in leaf litter and under dark sheeting used as warm refuges. Active from March to October.

Grass snake: the largest, up to a metre, with the distinctive yellow and black collar behind the head. More likely near water, a pond, stream or damp rough grass area. Not unusual in older East Kent gardens with rough boundaries near the Stour valley.

Common lizard: small (15 cm including tail), brown with dark streaking, moves fast and is most often seen basking on a south-facing bank, wall or log on a warm spring morning. Less tied to the hedge base than slow worms; more likely on a dry, rubble-free bank.

Step 2: Leave a rough strip at the hedge base

The single most useful thing you can do is stop mowing and strimming a 30 to 50 cm strip along the base of the hedge. Tussocky grass, leaf litter, dead stems and occasional bramble provide cover, warmth and foraging ground. This strip does not need to look wild from the garden side: a neatly edged rough strip is enough.

If the hedge base is on the shaded north side of the garden it is less useful for basking reptiles (which need sun for thermoregulation), but it still provides shelter and slug foraging ground, which suits slow worms well. A south-facing hedge base is the prime location.

Step 3: Create a warm refuge

Slow worms and common lizards use warm refuges under flat objects to raise their body temperature. The traditional approach used by reptile surveyors is a sheet of dark corrugated iron or roofing felt laid flat on the ground near the hedge base, in a spot that gets morning or midday sun. Leave it undisturbed for at least two weeks before the first check. The underside warms up faster than the surrounding soil and provides a safe, dark hiding place.

A log pile against the hedge base works almost as well and looks better. Stack the logs loosely so there are gaps for the animals to get in and out. Old fence posts and stumps that are rotting are particularly good.

Step 4: Stop slug pellets near the hedge base

Slugs are the primary food source for slow worms. Gardens that use metaldehyde or ferric phosphate pellets throughout the growing season reduce the prey base that slow worms depend on. If the aim is to attract slow worms, reduce pellet use near the hedge base. Rough grass, dead wood and leaf litter bring the slugs that bring the slow worms.

Step 5: Check before strimming

The hedge base is exactly where slow worms, grass snakes and common lizards shelter from late March to October. Before strimming along a hedge base, particularly near a log pile, warm bank or known rough strip:

  • Walk slowly along the area and scan for movement or basking animals.
  • Gently lift any boards, stones or logs that might be used as refuges, check underneath, and replace carefully.
  • Work from the hedge outward towards open grass rather than strimming toward the hedge base.
  • In spring (March to May) and autumn (September to October), when reptiles are basking more actively, do this check at a different time of day: early morning is when they are still cool and less reactive; midday is when they are most active and most likely to be on the surface.

If you find an animal, stop. Wait. They move off on their own in a few minutes once disturbed, as long as you have not injured them.
