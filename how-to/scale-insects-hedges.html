Scale insects on hedges: identify, treat and prevent

A sticky coating on your hedge leaves, black sooty mould on stems and paving beneath, or unexplained die-back on cotoneaster or privet: this is often scale insects. Here is how to spot them, what to do, and how to keep them from coming back.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Scale insects: your action checklist

  1. Identify the type: look for flat or dome-shaped waxy bumps on stems and twigs. White, cream, brown or grey. Check the underside of leaves too.
  2. Check for sooty mould: a black, dusty coating on leaves below the infestation is honeydew from scale. Confirms the diagnosis.
  3. Light infestation: physical removal with a soft nail brush or old toothbrush, followed by a wipe with a damp cloth. Effective on privet, box and small euonymus sections.
  4. Winter treatment (November to February): apply a plant-oil-based winter wash to dormant deciduous hedge stems. Kills overwintering scale under their protective shells.
  5. Spring treatment (March to April): apply horticultural oil spray to evergreen hedges when the young crawlers are active. More effective than treating mature scale in summer.
  6. Prune badly affected sections: cut out heavily infested stems back to healthy wood and remove the cuttings from the site. Do not compost them.
  7. Avoid broad-spectrum insecticides: these kill scale, but they also kill ladybirds, parasitic wasps and blue tits that would otherwise do the job for you.
  8. Improve air circulation: a hedge cut too tightly and densely creates the sheltered, humid microclimate that scale insects prefer. A slightly more open cut helps.

What scale insects are

Scale insects are small sap-sucking bugs that spend most of their adult life in one place, protected under a hard or waxy shell. They feed on plant sap and excrete a sticky substance called honeydew, which drips onto the surfaces below. Sooty mould fungi then colonise the honeydew and produce the familiar black coating on leaves and stems.

Unlike aphids, adult scale insects do not move. The mobile stage is the newly hatched "crawler" nymph, which is tiny and easily missed. By the time you notice the problem, you are usually looking at established adults on the stems, often in large numbers.

Scale insects are not new to UK gardens, but their populations have increased in recent decades, partly because of warmer winters (the East Kent coast rarely sees sustained hard frosts now) and partly because of the declining use of broad-spectrum pesticides, which has removed some of the chemical controls that once kept them in check, but also reduced populations of their natural predators in gardens that still use other pesticides indiscriminately.

The four types you are most likely to see in East Kent

Euonymus scale (Unaspis euonymi)

White, elongated shells on euonymus stems and leaves, with smaller brown shells mixed in. Common on Euonymus japonicus hedges in domestic gardens throughout Sandwich, Deal and Eastry, and on ivy where it grows alongside euonymus. A heavy infestation turns the hedge stems almost white. This is one of the more damaging scale insects on UK ornamental hedges.

Horsechestnut scale (Pulvinaria regalis)

Large (4-6 mm), brown, dome-shaped adults on stems and larger branches, with a distinctive white waxy egg mass beneath the female in late spring and early summer. Affects a very wide range of woody plants: cotoneaster, pyracantha, privet, amelanchier and many others. The egg masses in June are often the first sign gardeners notice. You may also see the sticky honeydew and sooty mould on paving beneath an affected hedge.

Brown scale (Parthenolecanium corni)

Smooth, brown, oval scales on twigs and stems. Common on roses, cotoneaster, pyracantha, berberis and many other ornamental shrubs in Kent gardens. Less visually dramatic than horsechestnut scale but capable of building to damaging numbers on stressed plants. Look on the undersides of small twigs in autumn and winter when leaves have fallen.

Woolly vine scale (Pulvinaria vitis)

Similar to horsechestnut scale in appearance: large, brown adults with white woolly egg masses. Targets cotoneaster and pyracantha in particular. Common in older established gardens in Woodnesborough and Ash where large cotoneaster hedges have been in place for decades without renovation.

Before you start treatment: pruning safety

If treatment involves pruning affected sections, observe the safety points below. Scale insect removal in winter (which is the best time) also typically means working on a dormant hedge that is outside the nesting season, but keep these habits year-round.

Pruning safety and nesting season

Under the UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, damaging an active bird's nest is a criminal offence. The nesting season runs roughly 1 March to 31 August. The best time to prune scale-affected hedge sections is November to February, well outside the nesting season. If you must treat or prune in spring or summer, check for active nests before cutting.

  • Eye and hand protection: gloves and safety glasses when pruning. Scale-infected stems can harbour brambles, broken twigs and debris.
  • Dispose of infected cuttings: bag them and put them in the general waste, not on the compost heap. Scale insects can survive on cut material for some time.

Treatment in practice

Physical removal for small infestations

On a small section of privet, euonymus or box, you can remove scale by scrubbing the affected stems with a soft nail brush or old toothbrush dipped in soapy water. Work along the stems, dislodging the scale and wiping them away with a damp cloth. Rinse the stems with clean water afterwards. This is effective for early-stage infestations and is entirely chemical-free.

Winter wash (November to February)

A plant-oil-based winter wash (available from garden centres as a liquid concentrate) applied to the dormant stems of deciduous hedges kills scale insects and their eggs under the protective shells. Spray in dry conditions when temperatures are above 5°C. Avoid spraying on or near open water. For euonymus and other evergreen hedges, a winter wash is less effective as the foliage traps the spray: focus on exposed stems and prune back to improve coverage.

Horticultural oil spray (spring)

Applied in March and April when newly hatched crawlers are active, horticultural oil (sometimes labelled as pesticide-free pest control or plant-based insecticide) smothers the young scale before they have developed their protective shells. This is the most effective treatment window for evergreen hedges like euonymus. The crawlers are tiny and move fast, so thorough coverage of all stems and the undersides of leaves is important.

Pruning heavily infested stems

For sections of hedge where scale has built to very high densities and sooty mould is extensive, the most practical option is to cut the affected stems back to healthy wood and remove the cuttings from site. On a euonymus hedge, this can mean a significant hard cut. Euonymus recovers well from hard pruning. On cotoneaster, cut back to a main stem and the plant will typically regenerate. Remove cuttings in sealed bags: do not leave them on the ground or compost them.

Prevention

A healthy, well-maintained hedge is significantly less likely to develop damaging scale insect populations. Good air circulation through the hedge structure matters: a hedge that is cut so tightly that air cannot move through the inner stems creates exactly the sheltered, humid microclimate that scale insects prefer. A properly tapered cut, wider at the base than at the top, allows light and air in. Garden Ninja (Lee Burkhill), a garden designer with over 66,000 subscribers on YouTube and a presenter on BBC's Garden Rescue, covers the principles of proper hedge shaping and maintenance in his ultimate guide to cutting and trimming hedges: the technique of keeping the outer leaf layer healthy with a proper cut directly supports long-term hedge health and reduces the conditions that favour scale.

Avoid over-feeding hedges with high-nitrogen fertiliser. Lots of nitrogen encourages soft, sappy new growth, which is more attractive to scale insects and other sap-suckers. A balanced slow-release fertiliser in spring is enough for most established hedges. Our guide on feeding hedges covers the right products and timing.

Encourage natural predators. Ladybirds overwinter in the base of hedges and in the hedge structure. Blue tits actively search hedge stems for scale insects in autumn and winter. A hedge base that has some leaf litter and rough vegetation rather than being raked bare to the soil provides the insect habitat that supports these predators. Our guide to hedge base management has more on this.

Inspect new plants before introducing them to your garden. Scale insects spread on nursery stock. Before planting a new euonymus or cotoneaster hedge in a garden in Sandwich, Deal or Worth, check the stems of new plants carefully and reject any that show scale, sooty mould or unusual stem bumps.
