Protecting bare-root hedge plants through their first winter in Kent

You have planted a bare-root hedge. Now what? The first winter is the most critical period, and the care you give between planting and late March makes the difference between a hedge that establishes and one that has to be replaced.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Bare-root hedge plants are sold and planted from November to March, when they are dormant. They are cheaper than pot-grown plants and establish just as well when planted correctly, but the first winter does involve a few specific tasks that pot-grown hedges do not need.

East Kent has a mild coastal climate compared with much of Britain, which helps. Hard prolonged frosts are less common than inland, and the winters around Sandwich and Deal are rarely harsh enough to cause serious damage to properly planted native species. That said, a new hedge is always more vulnerable than an established one, and these steps are worth doing.

Do not cut in the first growing season

The exception to this is the formative cut. Young bare-root plants benefit from being cut back by a third immediately after planting or in the first spring, to encourage bushy growth from the base. Left uncut, they tend to grow tall and leggy with a thin base that is hard to correct later. See our formative pruning guide for the method.

Step 1: Check firm contact at the roots immediately after planting

Air pockets around bare roots are the most common cause of failure. After planting, firm each plant in firmly with your foot, pressing soil down around the root zone rather than just filling in from above. Bare-root plants should not wobble when you grip the stem and gently push. If they do, firm them in more.

Do this check again after any hard frost or strong gale in the first few weeks. Frost can lift soil and loosen the root zone. Gently firm any plant that has moved.

Step 2: Mulch along the planting line

Apply a 5–7 cm layer of mulch (bark chippings, compost or well-rotted manure) along the full length of the planting line, keeping a small gap around each stem. Mulch does three things in a new hedge's first winter: it insulates the root zone against hard freezes, it suppresses weeds that would otherwise compete with new plants for moisture and nutrients, and it helps retain soil moisture during dry spells. Most losses in first-winter hedges can be traced to competition from weeds, not cold.

Do not pile mulch up against stems, as this encourages collar rot.

Step 3: Check for wind rock through the winter

New bare-root plants have no established root system to anchor them. Any top growth catches the wind, and this movement can loosen the root-to-soil contact. After any significant gale (East Kent gets a reasonable amount of coastal wind through the winter months), walk the hedge line and check whether any plants have moved. Firm them back in as needed. If a plant is consistently rocking, it may benefit from a short cane and a single tie at the base to hold it steady while roots establish.

Step 4: Water during any dry spells

A wet winter usually means you will not need to water at all. But if there is a dry period of more than two weeks, or if the soil is visibly dry around the base of plants, water thoroughly along the hedge line. Aim for soil that is consistently moist but not waterlogged. Do not water during hard frost.

The critical watering months for a bare-root hedge planted in autumn are actually March, April and May, when plants break dormancy and start drawing on soil moisture, often before significant rainfall returns. Watch carefully through early spring and water generously at the first sign of wilting.

Step 5: Deal with rabbit or deer pressure

In rural parts of East Kent, rabbits are the single biggest threat to a new hedge in the first winter. A rabbit can strip the bark from a young stem overnight, killing the plant entirely. If your site has rabbits, tree guards or mesh protectors on individual plants are worth the investment. Standard spiral guards work for most bare-root species. For a full hedge run, a temporary wire mesh fence set at least 30 cm below ground level is more robust.

Roe deer are present in parts of the Kent countryside and will browse young growth. If your site is at risk, deer guards are taller and sturdier than rabbit guards. Ask your nursery what height is appropriate for your site.

Step 6: Leave plants alone in January and February

This is genuinely the right answer for most of the winter. Bare-root deciduous plants (hawthorn, beech, hornbeam, field maple, blackthorn) are fully dormant and will look dead. Leafless stems, no visible growth, nothing happening. This is normal. The scratch test (gently scratch a small section of stem bark; if the tissue underneath is green, the plant is alive) is the only reliable check during dormancy. Do not pull plants out in January because they look dead.

Wait until late March or April. If a plant has not budded by the time its neighbours have leafed out, only then is it likely to have failed.

A note on Kent's milder winters

The coastal climate around Sandwich, Sandwich Bay, Deal and the surrounding villages tends to stay a degree or two milder than inland during hard weather. The sea moderates temperature extremes. For most native and semi-native hedge species, this means the first winter is rarely as challenging here as it would be in, say, the Weald or the North Downs. Tender evergreens such as griselinia and pittosporum benefit from a fleece wrap during prolonged hard frosts, but hawthorn, beech, hornbeam, blackthorn and field maple look after themselves once planted correctly.
