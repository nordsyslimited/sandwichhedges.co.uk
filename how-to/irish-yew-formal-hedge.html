Irish yew vs common yew for formal hedging in Kent

Both are evergreen, long-lived and exceptionally responsive to clipping. But Irish yew and common yew suit different situations, and choosing the wrong one for a narrow site or a wide formal hedge creates problems that take years to fix.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Yew is probably the most satisfying hedging plant in the British Isles. It is dense, evergreen, long-lived, and tolerates harder renovation cuts than almost any other hedging species. A well-maintained yew hedge has a quality to it that privet or leylandii do not come close to matching. It is no coincidence that the best-kept formal gardens in Kent, the churchyard boundaries in Sandwich and Ash, the cottage garden walls in Eastry and Worth, still use yew where something permanent and genuinely beautiful is wanted.

The question, when planting, is which yew to use. Most people are aware of common yew (Taxus baccata), the species you see in churchyards and established formal gardens across the county. Fewer are familiar with Irish yew (Taxus baccata 'Fastigiata'), which looks quite different in the garden centre but is the same species in a fastigiate, upright-growing form. The two have very different natural habits, different maintenance requirements, and genuinely different best applications.

Nesting season: check before you cut

Under the UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs roughly from 1 March to 31 August. Yew hedges, with their dense, evergreen growth, are regularly used as nesting sites by blackbirds, robins, wrens and dunnocks in East Kent gardens. The safe trimming window is September to February. If work is needed between March and August, check the hedge thoroughly for active nests before starting.

Toxicity: both forms are hazardous

All parts of both Irish yew and common yew are toxic to humans, dogs, cats, horses and most livestock. The red berries (arils) in autumn are particularly attractive and the most dangerous. Consider this when siting either form near children's play areas or in gardens used regularly by dogs. For horses and livestock in adjacent fields, a solid boundary or fence to prevent access is essential: yew poisoning is rapid and there is no antidote.

What is Irish yew?

Irish yew (Taxus baccata 'Fastigiata') is a naturally occurring variant of common yew, first found in County Fermanagh in the late eighteenth century. It grows in a narrow, upright, columnar shape rather than the wide, spreading form of common yew. Left unpruned, Irish yew will reach 5–8 metres in height while remaining relatively narrow, typically 1–2 metres wide at the base of a mature specimen. The foliage is the same dark green, though the needles are arranged around the stem at a slightly different angle, giving the plant a denser appearance when viewed from the side.

What distinguishes it from common yew is that it holds its columnar shape naturally without annual clipping. This makes it useful in formal garden settings where the goal is an architectural column or avenue effect rather than a continuous clipped screen. Irish yew columns appear in churchyards across Kent, including several in Sandwich and the surrounding villages, where they were planted in pairs to frame paths or in rows along boundaries.

The golden form, Taxus baccata 'Fastigiata Aurea', has the same columnar habit but with yellow-edged foliage. It is used in the same situations as Irish yew but provides a colour contrast with green planting that some gardens find effective.

How common yew and Irish yew compare for hedging

The table below sets out the main differences in practical terms. Neither is better across the board; each suits different applications.

Characteristic Common yew (T. baccata) Irish yew (T. baccata 'Fastigiata')
Natural growth habit Spreading, irregular; needs clipping to maintain a hedge shape Columnar, upright; holds a narrow form without clipping
Annual growth rate 15–30 cm/year (slow) 15–25 cm/year (slow)
Mature width 1–3 m if unclipped; can be maintained at any width 40–100 cm naturally; wider with clipping but resists it
Maintenance cuts per year One cut, mid-to-late summer (August) One light clip per year, or leave entirely
Hedge density Very dense from base when trained correctly Dense at top; can thin at base if not managed in youth
Best use Traditional formal hedge, screen, topiary Columnar accent, avenue, narrow formal feature
Renovation tolerance Excellent: responds well to hard renovation cuts Moderate: tolerates clipping but major renovation is harder

When to choose Irish yew

Irish yew earns its place when the goal is an upright, architectural column or a formal avenue effect. Its natural columnar habit means it can be used in a narrow site where a common yew hedge would quickly become too wide. A single Irish yew, or a pair flanking a gate or path, is a classic formal garden feature that requires almost no maintenance once established at the desired height.

It is particularly well-suited to:

  • Narrow side passages and tight boundaries where the available width is under 60–80 cm
  • Formal avenue plantings where the column shape reads well along a straight path
  • Small formal gardens where a continuous clipped hedge would look too heavy
  • Pairs as architectural accents flanking steps, gates or garden openings
  • Situations where very low maintenance is a priority, since the plant largely looks after its own form

Churchyard plantings in this part of East Kent show how effective Irish yew can be in a formal setting. The examples in Sandwich and in the village churchyards at Ash and Woodnesborough have been in place for many decades and need little beyond an occasional clip to remain tidy.

When common yew is the better choice

For a traditional formal hedge, particularly one intended to form a continuous screen, common yew (Taxus baccata) is usually the right choice. Its spreading growth habit means it fills out more reliably across all three faces of a hedge, produces a denser basal screen, and responds better to the kind of hard renovation that may be needed after a few missed years or a severe winter.

Common yew is better suited to:

  • A hedge intended to be wider than 60–80 cm at maturity
  • Sites where dense screening at ground level is important from the outset
  • Topiary work, where the spreading growth provides more material to shape
  • Long runs of formal hedge where visual consistency across the full length is needed
  • Any situation where you may need to renovate the hedge hard at some point: common yew is more forgiving

Trimming Irish yew as a formal feature

If you are using Irish yew as individual columns or accent plants rather than as a formal clipped hedge, the maintenance requirement is minimal: one light clip in August or September to tidy the outline and remove any wayward growth that has broken from the natural column form. The plant does the work of maintaining its own shape.

If you are using Irish yew in a row as a narrow formal screen, it needs slightly more attention in its early years to ensure it fills out from the base. Light tip pruning in year one and two, taking 5–10 cm off the growing tips on each face, encourages lateral branching and prevents the lower section from becoming thin. Yew that is left entirely to its own devices from planting sometimes develops an open, thin-based form in its first few years that takes longer to correct later.

The key rule with Irish yew, as with any yew, is not to cut back into old, brown, leafless wood. Provided there is live, green-needled growth behind the cut point, yew regenerates reliably. Where interior wood is entirely bare and brown, it will not regrow.
