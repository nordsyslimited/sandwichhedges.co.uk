Sarcococca (sweet box) hedge care

Slow-growing, deeply shade-tolerant, evergreen, and scented in January when everything else is grey. Sarcococca does one specific job, a low hedge in a dark corner, better than almost anything else.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Sarcococca at a glance: the checklist

  • Shade: grows in full shade, including north-facing aspects and under trees. Better in shade than sun.
  • Soil: most soils, including chalk and dry conditions once established. Prefers moisture-retentive but drains well in Kent sandy soils.
  • Height: 60 cm to 1 m for S. confusa as a hedge; S. hookeriana var. humilis stays to about 50 cm.
  • Spacing: 30 to 45 cm apart for a continuous hedge.
  • Pruning: minimal. A light tidy in April after flowering, or nothing at all for years on end.
  • Flowering: January to February. Tiny white flowers with a strong sweet scent.
  • Berries: black, ornamental, not edible. Birds eat them in autumn.
  • Speed: slow. Allow four to five years from planting to a solid hedge at 30 to 45 cm spacings.
  • Nesting season: pruning in spring falls after nesting season starts (1 March to 31 August). Check before cutting.

Why sarcococca for a shaded boundary

Most hedging plants need at least partial sun to perform well. Box hedges in deep shade thin at the top and become straggly. Privet in shade grows too loose to hold a line. Portuguese laurel survives but grows slowly and often looks unhappy. Sarcococca is different: it actively prefers the cooler, moister conditions of a north-facing aspect, a wall base, or a garden that gets no direct sun. In those situations, it looks better than it does in full sun, where the leaves can go pale and peaky.

For town gardens in Sandwich and Deal where a side passage, a north-facing rear garden or a gloomy corner under established trees needs a low, tidy edge, sarcococca is often the most practical choice available.

Nesting season and pruning timing

Sarcococca flowers in January and February, which is within the safe legal cutting window. If you prune it lightly after flowering, in March or April, you are at the start of the nesting season. The UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs from 1 March to 31 August. Because sarcococca is a low, dense evergreen shrub, birds will nest in or very close to it. Always check before you start, even for a light trim.

Before you start: safety

  • Gloves: sturdy gloves. Sarcococca has no thorns but secateurs can slip on the woody stems.
  • Eye protection: safety glasses, especially when using powered tools near eye level on a low hedge.
  • RCD adaptor: essential for any corded electric trimmer. No exceptions.
  • Kneel safely: sarcococca is a low hedge. Working at ground level for any length of time is hard on knees. Use a kneeling pad.

Planting a sarcococca hedge

Species and cultivar choice

For most domestic hedges in East Kent, Sarcococca confusa is the standard choice. It reaches 1 to 1.5 m if left unpruned, making a solid low hedge at 60 to 80 cm with occasional light tidying. It spreads slowly by suckers and fills in gaps gradually. The flowers are cream-white, strongly vanilla-scented, and appear from January to March.

Sarcococca hookeriana var. humilis is shorter and more compact, staying to about 50 cm, which suits a really low edging hedge. It spreads more readily by underground runners than S. confusa. 'Ghorepani' (S. hookeriana var. hookeriana) is taller and narrower with pink-flushed flowers and is useful for a tighter upright hedge.

RHS curator Matthew Pottage covers sarcococca among other box alternatives at RHS Wisley in the RHS video Alternatives to box, which is a useful introduction to the plant's habits and garden uses.

Spacing and soil preparation

Plant at 30 to 45 cm apart for a continuous hedge. On the free-draining chalk soils around Woodnesborough and Eastry, add well-rotted compost to the planting hole and mulch the surface after planting, which conserves moisture through the first summer. On heavier soils in the Sandwich valley, drainage is less of a concern, but good organic matter still helps establishment.

Autumn planting (October to November) gives the roots a long establishment period through the cooler months before summer drought. Avoid planting in frozen or waterlogged ground.

Establishment care

Water well in the first summer if conditions are dry. Sarcococca establishes slowly and the first season is the critical one for root development. Once through the first full year, the plant is substantially more drought-tolerant and can manage without supplementary watering in all but exceptional summers.

A mulch layer of 5 to 8 cm of bark chip or well-rotted compost around the base, kept away from the stems, reduces moisture loss and suppresses weeds during establishment.

Pruning and long-term maintenance

Routine pruning

Most established sarcococca hedges need almost no pruning. If the hedge becomes uneven in height or a few stems push out ahead of the rest, a light trim with hand shears in April after the flowers have finished is all that is needed. Do not cut hard into old wood unless undertaking deliberate renovation, as the plant recovers slowly from severe cuts compared with faster-growing hedging species.

Renovation pruning

A neglected sarcococca that has outgrown its space can be cut back quite hard in spring, in March or April after the flowers finish. Remove the largest, oldest stems at the base with loppers and reduce the height by up to half if necessary. The plant will regenerate over one to two growing seasons, and the slow growth rate means it will take several years to return to its previous size. Renovation is not something most sarcococca hedges will need in the first ten years.

A note on honey fungus

Sarcococca can be susceptible to honey fungus (Armillaria species) in gardens where the fungus is already established in old tree roots or woody debris. If black bootlace-like rhizomorphs are visible in the soil, or if adjacent plants have died unexpectedly, be cautious about planting sarcococca or any other susceptible shrub in the same spot without removing the infected root material first.

Sarcococca in Kent gardens

For north-facing town garden boundaries in Sandwich and Deal, where side passages run between terraced properties and get almost no direct sun, sarcococca gives something that most other hedging plants cannot: a tidy, evergreen, low-maintenance edge that actually looks good in the conditions. The January scent near a gate or doorway is a genuine bonus. We have planted it in several such situations around the Sandwich area and it reliably outperforms the alternatives.
