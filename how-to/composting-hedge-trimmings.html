What to do with hedge trimmings after a cut

A decent-sized hedge produces more clippings than most people expect. Here's what actually works, and what just makes a soggy mess at the bottom of the garden.

Updated August 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Composting, the free option

Hedge trimmings are what's called brown material in composting terms, mostly carbon-rich woody waste. On its own it breaks down slowly, so it needs mixing with something nitrogen-rich, grass clippings, vegetable peelings, that kind of thing, or it just sits there for a year.

Snip smaller, leafier trimmings up before adding them, whole branches take much longer to rot down and can make the pile harder to turn.

Shredding, worth it for a big job

For anything more than a bin bag or two, a garden shredder turns a pile of awkward branches into a manageable heap of chippings in a few minutes. Shredded material composts faster and can also be used directly as mulch around borders and beneath the hedge itself.

If you don't own a shredder, some Kent councils run seasonal shredding events or hire days, worth checking before buying one for an occasional job.

Council green waste collection

Most Kent councils run a garden waste collection service, usually a brown bin on a fortnightly or monthly cycle, sometimes a chargeable subscription. It's the easiest option for a one-off big cut with no composting space, check your specific council's scheme and any size limits on branch thickness before putting trimmings out.

The dead hedge option

If there's a corner of the garden nobody minds looking a bit wild, woody offcuts can be woven or stacked between a couple of stakes to build what's called a dead hedge, a simple wildlife feature that gives hedgehogs, insects and birds somewhere to shelter. It slowly rots down over a few years, no bin, no shredder, no trip to the tip.
