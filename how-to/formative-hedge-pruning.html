Formative hedge pruning: training a new hedge in years 1, 2 and 3

Most new hedges fail to thicken up for the same reason: nobody cut them early enough. Here is the year-by-year cutting regime that builds a dense, well-shaped hedge from bare-root or pot-grown stock.

Updated September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

The formative pruning checklist at a glance

  • Planting day: cut back deciduous plants by a third to a half immediately after planting (late autumn to early spring). Tip back evergreens lightly.
  • Year 1, late winter: cut deciduous hedging back hard again, by a third. Leave evergreens to establish, just remove any dead or crossing stems.
  • Year 1, summer: trim side growth lightly on deciduous species to encourage bushiness. Leave height.
  • Year 2, late winter: cut deciduous hedging back by a third again. Begin defining the A-frame profile: slightly wider at the base than the top.
  • Year 2, summer: trim sides and, for the first time, lightly tip the top to encourage the leading shoot to branch.
  • Year 3: establish the final shape. Cut to the intended outline, including the top, for the first time. From here, routine annual or biannual maintenance takes over.
  • All years: always check for nesting birds before any cut between 1 March and 31 August.

The instinct when you plant a new hedge is to leave it alone and let it grow. That instinct produces tall, spindly plants with bare stems at the base and a thin canopy at the top. The instinct is wrong.

Cutting back early forces the plant to put energy into side branches rather than racing upward. Do it right in the first two winters and by year three you have something that genuinely reads as a hedge: dense to the ground, with a consistent outline and real screening value.

Nesting season applies to new hedges too

Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to damage an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs roughly from 1 March to 31 August. Even a new hedge with thin coverage will attract nesting robins and sparrows by year two. Check before any spring or summer cut. If in doubt, wait until September. See our guide on wildlife-friendly hedge trimming.

Before you start

For formative cuts, hand shears or secateurs usually give more control than a powered trimmer on young stems. Wear gloves and eye protection. On hawthorn and blackthorn, the spines are serious: thick leather gloves and eye protection are mandatory. Check the area for wasp nests before any cut in summer.

Year 1: cut to encourage branching

At planting (October to February)

For deciduous species (hawthorn, hazel, hornbeam, privet, beech, field maple): cut the main stems back by a third to a half immediately after planting. This looks brutal and most people do not do it. Do it. You are trading height in the short term for density in the long term. The cut forces side buds to break lower down the stem, giving you coverage at the base where you will always want it most.

For evergreen species (laurel, yew, box, privet, lonicera nitida): do not cut back as hard at planting. Remove any damaged growth and just tip back the leading shoot by 5-10 cm to break apical dominance and encourage side branching. The main formative pruning for evergreens comes in year two.

Midsummer, year 1

By June or July, deciduous hedging will have put on a flush of new growth. Clip the side growth lightly, by about a quarter, to maintain a bushy profile. Do not cut the top: you want height at this stage. Evergreens can be left entirely in year one unless they are growing very one-sided, in which case tip back the dominant side very lightly.

Year 2: define the shape

Late winter, year 2 (January to March)

Cut deciduous hedging back by another third. If the hedge is beginning to look dense at the base, you can start easing up on the reduction: the aim here is to keep stimulating branching from lower down while gradually allowing the plant to put on height. Begin establishing the A-frame profile: the base of the hedge should be 10-15 cm wider than the top. This ensures light reaches the lower stems year after year, keeping the base covered. A hedge that is wider at the top than the base will lose its lower foliage within a few seasons.

For evergreens: now is the time for the first real shaping cut. Clip the sides lightly to define the intended outline. Tip back the top if the plant has reached roughly two-thirds of the eventual target height.

Midsummer, year 2

For deciduous hedging, trim both sides and, for the first time, lightly tip the top. This encourages the leading shoots to branch at the crown, giving you a denser top section. Keep the A-frame profile consistent. For evergreens, a light summer tidy maintains the shape you set in winter.

Year 3: establish the final outline

By year three, a well-managed deciduous hedge should be approaching its target height and showing genuine density from base to tip. Now you cut it to its final intended shape, including the top, for the first time. Take the sides in to the intended finished line. Level the top with a string line or, for short runs, a spirit level and cane. This is the cut that commits you to the shape.

From year three onwards, the routine is maintenance rather than formation: one to two cuts per year, September to February where possible, keeping the shape you established. See our guide on hedge trimming timing for the ongoing calendar.

Species that need a different approach

Yew

Yew is slow and you need patience. In years one and two, do almost nothing: remove damaged growth and just tip the leading shoot lightly. Begin shaping in year three. Yew responds so well to hard pruning once established that the early years are about letting it build root and stem mass first.

Leylandii and fast conifers

Conifers grow fast but cannot regenerate from bare brown wood. The formative years are critical: establish the final height sooner than you think you need to. If you let leylandii run to 5 m before you start managing it, you cannot cut it back below the green zone without leaving permanent dead patches. See our leylandii guide for the detail.

Hawthorn for a stockproof or wildlife hedge

Hawthorn used for stockproof or naturalistic hedges in Kent fields is managed quite differently from a formal garden hedge. The traditional approach is a hard cutback in year one followed by allowing it to grow relatively freely for several seasons before laying (if you want a traditional Kent hedgerow). That's a separate discipline entirely from the formal-hedge routine above.

Privet

Privet grows fast and responds extremely well to formative pruning. Cut back hard at planting and again in year one. By year two it may already look like a reasonable hedge. The risk with privet is going the other way: leaving it to grow unmanaged and ending up with a large, hollow plant rather than a dense hedge.
