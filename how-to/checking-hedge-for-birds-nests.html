Checking your hedge for active birds' nests before cutting

The law is clear: you cannot cut a hedge while birds are nesting in it. This guide explains what to look for, how to inspect the hedge safely, and what to do if you find a nest in use.

Updated September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

The law: Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981

It is an offence to intentionally damage or destroy an active bird's nest, or to disturb birds at or near a nest containing eggs or young. This applies to all wild birds. There is no permit system, no exemption for accidental damage caused by cutting, and no defence of "I didn't know." The nesting season runs roughly from 1 March to 31 August. Cutting a hedge in this period without checking is a legal risk. The safe window for most hedges is September to February.

We check every hedge before we cut it. It takes a few minutes, it keeps everyone on the right side of the law, and it avoids the genuinely distressing situation of finding a nest after the damage is done. This guide explains exactly what we do.

The Royal Horticultural Society has published useful guidance on encouraging wildlife in gardens, including how hedges function as nesting habitat and what birds to expect: How to encourage wildlife in your urban garden (The RHS, YouTube).

Should you cut at all? The first decision

Use this sequence before picking up any tools:

Step 1: What month is it?

September to February? The main safe window. Most birds have fledged. A quick visual check is still good practice, but the risk is low. Proceed to Step 2.

March, April or May? High risk. Nesting in full swing. You must check thoroughly before cutting anything. Proceed to Step 2, but be prepared to postpone.

June, July or early August? Mixed. Many first broods have fledged but second and third broods are active. Species like woodpigeons can nest at any time. Check carefully before any cutting. Proceed to Step 2.

Late August? Most nesting is over. Risk is low but not zero for late nesters. Quick check recommended. Proceed to Step 2.

Step 2: Watch before you approach

Stand back from the hedge for two or three minutes before doing anything. Look for:

  • Birds flying repeatedly into the same point in the hedge
  • A bird sitting very still and not moving away as you stand nearby
  • Alarm calls (sharp, rapid, agitated) from one bird above or near the hedge
  • A bird carrying nesting material in or food towards the hedge

Any of these is a strong indicator of an active nest. Proceed to Step 3 before cutting anywhere near that area.

If you see none of these signs, proceed to Step 3 anyway for a physical check during the March-August period.

Step 3: Physical inspection

Walk slowly along the hedge from one end, looking in from the sides and underneath. For a tall hedge, use a long-handled tool or pole to gently part the outer foliage at different points and peer in, rather than putting your face or hands into the hedge.

You are looking for:

  • A constructed nest: a cup of grass, moss and fine material, sometimes with a mud lining (as in a blackbird or song thrush nest)
  • Eggs (halt immediately; do not disturb)
  • Chicks or partially-grown nestlings
  • A parent bird sitting tight and not flushing

Found an empty nest from a previous season? Old nests are usually ragged, collapsed and free of any adult bird interest. These are not protected. You may cut.

Found an active nest? Proceed to Step 4.

No nest found? Safe to cut. Work from the outside in, moving steadily along the hedge so any animals present can move away ahead of the tools.

Step 4: Active nest found. What to do.

Stop cutting in that area immediately. If you have already started on another section of the hedge, you may continue there, staying at least a metre away from the nest site.

Mark the area with canes, string or a small flag so everyone working near the hedge knows to avoid it. A one-metre buffer on each side of the nest site is a sensible minimum.

Note the species if you can identify it, and note the date. Do not revisit the nest more than once a week to check: repeated disturbance can cause adults to abandon even an established nest.

For most common garden species, the sequence is: incubation (two to three weeks) followed by nestlings in the nest (another two to three weeks) followed by fledging. Once the young have left and adult activity at the nest site stops, the nest is no longer active and the hedge can be cut.

Recheck after four to six weeks. If there is no adult activity and the nest is empty, the nest is no longer protected and the section can be cut.

Which species nest in Kent hedges?

East Kent hedges host a wide range of nesting birds. The most common species you are likely to encounter include:

Blackbird: one of the most frequent. Builds a substantial cup nest of grass and mud, often at low height in the hedge interior. Three or four broods per season, so a blackbird hedge can be active from March well into August.

Robin: nests at low level, often towards the base of the hedge, sometimes tucked into a bank below it. Very tolerant of human presence, but will flush from the nest if you approach closely.

Dunnock: similar to robin in nesting position. A small, low-profile nest, easy to miss. Dunnocks are common across East Kent gardens including around Sandwich, Deal and the rural hedgerows between Ash and Eastry.

Song thrush: builds a distinctive mud-lined cup nest. May be encountered from March through June.

Whitethroat and lesser whitethroat: both summer visitors to Kent. Whitethroat is common in dense scrubby hedgerows around Worth, Sandwich Bay and the margins of the Worth marshes. Nests low in the hedge or in bramble growth at the base.

Woodpigeon: not seasonal in the way smaller passerines are. Woodpigeons can and do nest throughout the year, including in winter. Their platform nests of twigs are relatively easy to spot. Active woodpigeon nests can be encountered even in October and November, so it is worth a glance before the last autumn cut.

A word on wasps

This is not a birds' nests issue, but it is worth checking for while you are inspecting: wasps build nests in hedges, typically from May onwards, and a nest disturbed mid-cut leads to a very unpleasant few minutes. Look for a consistent stream of wasps flying in and out of a single point in the hedge. If you find one, stop, retreat calmly and consider whether the job can be deferred until late autumn when the colony will have died back naturally.

Checking before we cut: how we handle it

Every job we do includes a walk of the hedge before tools come out. If we find a nest in use, we mark it and cut around it. If the nesting section is large enough to affect the finished look of the hedge, we let the customer know and return to complete it once the nest is clear. We never cut into an active nest. It is not just the law; it is not something we are prepared to do.

Can the whole job be delayed if nests are found?

Usually not. A single active nest in a 30-metre hedge is not a reason to postpone the whole job. Mark the occupied section, cut everything else, and return for the remaining section once the nest is empty. This approach is practical, keeps costs down and is entirely lawful.

If the nesting activity is very widespread (for example, a large established hedge that has multiple active nests throughout its length during April or May), postponement may genuinely be the only sensible option. In this case, September is usually the earliest practical start date for the main cut.
