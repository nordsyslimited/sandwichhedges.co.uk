Juniper hedge care: how to grow and maintain a juniper hedge in the UK

Juniper is one of only three native British conifers and it makes a genuinely distinguished garden hedge, but it has specific rules that differ from most conifers. Cut it wrong once and the damage is permanent.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Juniper hedges are less common than leylandii or laurel in East Kent gardens, but the species turns up occasionally in older gardens around Sandwich, Deal and along the chalk downland edge towards Eastry. They are distinctive: prickly, aromatic, slow growing and with a character that privet simply does not have. But the same prickliness that makes juniper a good nesting hedge makes careless trimming painful, and the species' one non-negotiable rule, that you must never cut back into old brown wood, means mistakes are hard to fix.

This guide covers common juniper (Juniperus communis) and its upright cultivars, which are the forms most suited to hedging. It also applies, with minor differences, to pencil-form cultivars such as J. communis 'Hibernica' (Irish juniper), and to Juniperus scopulorum varieties sold as hedging plants.

Nesting season: juniper is particularly valuable cover

Juniper's dense, prickly foliage is prized nesting habitat. Under the UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs roughly 1 March to 31 August. The safe cutting window for juniper is late April to June (spring trim) and late August to September (autumn tidy). Always check the hedge before cutting in either window.

Before you start

Juniper foliage has very sharp needle-like leaves that can pierce normal gardening gloves. Use thick leather or kevlar gloves. Wear eye protection: even light contact with juniper foliage can scratch. For tall hedges, a long-reach pole trimmer is safer than a ladder. Petrol or loud electric trimmers need ear defenders. Check for wasp nests before starting, particularly in late summer. If using a corded electric trimmer, plug into an RCD adapter.

Step 1: choose the right juniper variety for hedging

Not all junipers make good hedges. The low-growing horizontal forms, such as Juniperus horizontalis or J. x pfitzeriana, are ground-cover plants rather than hedging material. For a proper upright hedge, use:

  • Juniperus communis (common juniper): native, slower growing, distinctive blue-green needles, excellent wildlife value.
  • Juniperus communis 'Hibernica' (Irish juniper): strongly upright and columnar, works well as a formal screen in a row.
  • Juniperus scopulorum 'Skyrocket' or 'Blue Arrow': tighter, more uniform column than common juniper; suits a narrow formal screen.

Common juniper is the most natural-looking as a hedge and the most appropriate for a naturalistic East Kent garden. 'Skyrocket' and similar are better if you want a tight, formal screen in limited width.

Step 2: plant correctly

Juniper is not difficult to establish but it is particular about drainage. Waterlogged soil will kill it, often through Phytophthora root rot, which is hard to treat and effectively fatal once established. Before planting, assess drainage honestly. If the site holds water after rain, either improve drainage by incorporating grit and organic matter or choose a different species.

Planting is best done in autumn (October to November) or early spring (March to April), avoiding frozen ground. Space plants 60 to 80 centimetres apart for a hedge that fills in within three to four years. Stake on exposed sites: East Kent's coastal wind near Sandwich Bay and along the Deal seafront can rock newly planted specimens before root establishment. Water in well and apply a thick mulch to conserve moisture in the first summer, keeping the mulch clear of the stem base.

Step 3: formative trimming in the first two years

Juniper hedges need very little cutting in the first two growing seasons. The priority is to let the plants establish. If any stems are growing outwards and breaking the intended hedge line, remove them with secateurs. Do not make any attempt at a formal clipped shape until the hedge has reached close to the desired height: juniper dislikes heavy cutting while still young.

Step 4: the annual trim

Once the hedge has established (usually from year three or four), trim once or twice a year:

  • Spring trim (late April to early June): light cut to maintain shape and remove the previous season's new growth. This is the main shaping cut for most juniper hedges.
  • Autumn tidy (late August to September): optional but useful on faster-growing cultivars. A light pass to tighten the shape before winter. Do not cut after October, as late cuts leave fresh wounds exposed to frost.

The critical rule for all juniper cutting: stay in the green. Never cut back into growth that has no living green needles. Unlike yew, which regenerates well from old wood, juniper will not produce new growth from bare brown branches. Once a section is cut back to bare wood, it stays bare. Trim only the new season's growth, leaving the older structure intact.

Step 5: choose the right tool

For a small juniper hedge, sharp hand shears give the best result: they cut cleanly without snagging the prickly foliage, and you can feel exactly where you are cutting. A hedge trimmer works on larger hedges and established cultivars with relatively dense, even growth, but use it carefully: the vibration and the blade catching on needles makes it easier to accidentally cut deeper than intended. Always cut at an angle that favours the outside of the hedge, to maintain the battered taper profile that allows light to reach the lower portions.

Step 6: watch for common problems

Brown patches in summer

Possible causes include spider mite damage (look for fine webbing and a bronze, mottled texture on the foliage in dry summers), Phytophthora root rot (check for soggy soil at the base), Kabatina juniper blight (affecting shoot tips in late winter and early spring), or simply dog urine at the base. Near coastal roads, salt spray during winter is a known cause of browning on exposed hedges around Sandwich Bay and Deal seafront.

Sparse lower growth

Juniper hedges that are too dense at the top lose light to the lower portions and thin out at the base. Maintain the battered taper (slightly wider at the base than the top) to keep lower growth healthy. If the lower portion is already thinning, the only reliable remedy is patience: reduce the width of the top portion over two or three seasons to allow light back in.

Whole plants dying

Sudden die-back of one or more plants in an established hedge is most commonly Phytophthora root rot, particularly after a wet winter. There is no effective treatment: remove affected plants and, if possible, improve drainage at the site before replanting. Do not replant juniper in the same spot without addressing the drainage cause.

Juniper in East Kent conditions

East Kent's mild coastal winters, common across Sandwich Bay, Deal and Eastry, suit juniper well. The species is native to chalk grassland in the UK and the chalky or sandy soils common in parts of the area around Sandwich and Worth are close to its natural habitat. It tolerates dry summers better than most hedging species, which is increasingly useful during the dry late-summer periods that have become more common in the area.

The berries, which take two to three years to ripen and turn from green to blue-black, are eaten by fieldfares, redwings and other visiting thrushes over winter: species that feed along the hedgerows of the Stour valley and around Sandwich Bay during their autumn and winter stay. This makes an established juniper hedge a genuinely useful wildlife asset for an East Kent garden.
