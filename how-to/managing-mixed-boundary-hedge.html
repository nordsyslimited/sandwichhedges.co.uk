Managing a mixed boundary hedge: different species in the same run

Inherited a garden fence line where privet meets leylandii meets something you can't quite identify? Here is how to work with it rather than fight it.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

A lot of gardens in and around Sandwich, Deal and Ash have boundaries that grew up by accident. A section of privet from the 1970s, a run of leylandii a previous owner planted in the 1980s, a stretch of old hawthorn that was there before any of it. Three different plants, same fence line, and you have to cut the lot.

Mixed hedges like this are not the same as a deliberately designed tapestry hedge, where you choose species for visual effect. These are working boundaries, and the challenge is practical: the species have different growth rates, different ideal cutting windows, and different rules about how hard you can cut.

Nesting season applies to the whole run

The Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to disturb or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs roughly from 1 March to 31 August. This applies to every section of a mixed hedge, not just the sections with berries or cover. Check the entire run before you start, even the leylandii sections where nests are harder to spot.

The core problem: three different growth rates, one cut

When you cut a single-species hedge, life is straightforward: everything in that run is at the same stage, needs the same tool and responds the same way. A mixed run is messier. Here are the differences that actually matter.

Species Annual growth Best cutting window Cuts per year Hard reduction?
Privet 60–90 cm Jun (light), Aug–Oct 1–2 Yes, recovers well
Leylandii 60–90 cm May–Sep 2–3 No: stay in the green
Laurel 30–60 cm Late Aug–Oct 1 Yes, but one section at a time
Hawthorn 30–60 cm Aug–Nov 1 Yes, very tough
Holly 15–30 cm Late Jul–Sep 1 Yes, slowly
Beech 30–60 cm Late Jul–Aug 1 Yes, cut in winter

How to approach the cut

Choose a single scheduling window that suits most species

September is usually the answer. Nesting is over, leylandii is still taking cuts well, privet is in its second trim window, and laurel, hawthorn and holly are all fine. The species you miss is beech, which prefers late July or August, but it will survive a September cut without lasting harm if you are not cutting it hard.

Treat each section as its own hedge

Do not try to run the trimmer straight along the whole boundary and call it done. Walk the run first and mark where one species ends and another begins. A strip of coloured string or a handful of canes works fine. Then cut each section using the technique that species needs.

The hardest problem: leylandii that has grown too tall

This comes up in almost every mixed-boundary job we do in East Kent. A previous owner planted leylandii for screening, it has grown well beyond a sensible height, and now the neighbouring section of privet or laurel is struggling for light. You have two options.

  • Gradual reduction: Take 15–20 cm off the height of the leylandii each year over three or four seasons. Slower, but the hedge stays green throughout.
  • One-off reduction: Cut back hard in one season, accepting that the cut faces will show brown for a year or two before greening back in from the sides. This only works if you are cutting into green growth, not bare wood. Our leylandii guide covers the limits in full.

Height profile across the run

A mixed run will almost never be perfectly level because different species put on growth at different rates. Some variation is natural and nothing to worry about, but if you want a consistent line, the only real answer is to set a string line and trim to it, adjusting the height of each section independently.

When one section has a disease

A mixed hedge can stop a disease from spreading in the way a single-species run cannot. Box blight, for instance, stops at a privet section rather than running along the whole boundary. If one species is clearly diseased, treat or remove that section without touching the others. See our honey fungus guide and box blight treatment guide for the main disease scenarios.

DIY or professional?

A straightforward mixed run at a manageable height is a DIY job. Where things tip towards professional are:

  • Any leylandii over about 2.5 metres that needs a ladder or platform to reach.
  • A section that needs a significant hard reduction, where technique matters more than speed.
  • A run where the species are so different in habit that getting a straight line across all of them is genuinely difficult.
