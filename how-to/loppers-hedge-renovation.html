Loppers for hedge renovation: the right tool for thick stems

A hedge trimmer is for the face; loppers are for the skeleton. When a hedge needs renovating rather than trimming, loppers are often the tool that does most of the real work.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

When a hedge outgrows its annual trim, you are no longer trimming: you are renovating. That usually means cutting stems between 2 cm and 6 cm in diameter. A hedge trimmer cannot handle stems that thick; it bounces off and can damage the blade. A hand saw is accurate but slow. A reciprocating saw is powerful but hard to control in a dense thicket. Loppers sit neatly in the middle: they handle the range of stems most common in renovation work, they are manoeuvrable inside a dense hedge, and they cut cleanly if the blades are sharp and correctly chosen.

This guide compares the three main tools for renovation cutting, then goes into detail on how to use loppers effectively: which type to buy, the technique that keeps cuts clean, and the specific jobs they are best suited to in East Kent's most common hedge species (privet, laurel and hawthorn).

Safety note before starting renovation work

Wear eye protection and sturdy gloves throughout. A stem cut under tension by loppers can spring back sharply. Work from the outside of the hedge in and clear cut material as you go, so you are not stepping over or reaching across a pile of cut stems with sharp lopper blades in hand. Ear defenders are not needed for loppers specifically, but check for wasps before reaching deep into a hedge.

Three tools compared: loppers, hand saw, reciprocating saw

Bypass loppers

Stem diameter range: up to 4–5 cm with a good-quality pair; smaller loppers up to 2.5–3 cm.

What they do well: Clean, controlled cuts in dense growth. You can reach into the interior of a hedge to cut specific stems without disturbing nearby ones. Light enough to work with for a full session. Very quiet.

What they struggle with: Slow on volume. If a hedge needs 100 stems cut to 3 cm diameter, loppers alone will take several hours. They also need clean, fresh blades to cut rather than crush: a blunt lopper on green privet compresses the stem tissue instead of slicing it.

Kent renovation use: The workhorse tool for the first pass through a privet, hawthorn or laurel hedge in renovation. Use loppers to open up the structure, remove crossing stems and reduce the main framework before finishing with a trimmer.

Folding pruning saw

Stem diameter range: 4 cm and above, up to 10 cm or more. The limiting factor is usually access, not the saw's capacity.

What it does well: Clean, controlled cuts on thicker material. The pull stroke (teeth cut on the pull, not the push) makes it effective even in confined spaces. Very good for horizontal cuts near ground level where loppers are awkward. No power required.

What it struggles with: Slow on quantity. Each cut takes time. It is not the right tool for cutting fifty 3 cm stems; it is the right tool for cutting five 8 cm stems cleanly.

Kent renovation use: Used alongside loppers for base renovation on a mature privet or hawthorn hedge where the main structural stems have reached 6 to 10 cm and loppers can no longer close properly. The saw handles those stems; loppers take care of the smaller lateral growth.

Cordless reciprocating saw

Stem diameter range: Any diameter the blade length can accommodate, typically up to 15 cm with standard pruning blades.

What it does well: Speed. A cordless reciprocating saw can cut a renovation's worth of stems in roughly a third of the time loppers take. Very effective on open-access work, cutting along a hedge base or reducing tall stems at height.

What it struggles with: Control in dense growth. The saw's reciprocating action snags on surrounding stems and can tear rather than cut cleanly if the blade is even slightly blunt. It is a noisy, vibrating tool that is harder to place accurately in a thicket than loppers. Blunt blades are a real issue: a reciprocating saw with a dull blade tears stem tissue badly and can cause more die-back at the cut point than a hand saw.

Kent renovation use: Good for reducing the top of a tall hedge quickly, or for cutting long horizontal runs along the base of a wide hawthorn or laurel hedge where access is open. Less suited to close interior work.

When to use loppers specifically

Loppers are the right choice for four specific situations that come up repeatedly in renovation work on East Kent hedges:

  1. Opening up a dense interior: Where a privet or laurel hedge has become a thicket of crossing and overcrowded stems, loppers let you selectively remove stems from inside the hedge without disturbing the outer face. Work from one end to the other, removing the oldest and thickest crossing stems first.
  2. Removing old thick base stems: Over time, the base of a privet or hawthorn hedge accumulates very thick, old stems that have outgrown the hedge's intended width. Loppers cut these at or near ground level to open up the base, which encourages new growth from lower down.
  3. Reducing lateral framework branches: When reducing the width of a hedge hard, the main lateral branches (the ones growing horizontally outward from the main vertical stems) need to be cut back to a point that leaves at least some green growth beyond the cut. Loppers give you the control to place that cut accurately.
  4. Spot cutting after the main trimmer pass: After running the hedge trimmer over the face, there are always a few stems that are too thick for the trimmer but stick out beyond the new face line. Loppers handle these quickly.

Lopper technique: keeping cuts clean

Bypass, not anvil. This matters. An anvil lopper has one straight blade that closes against a flat plate; this compresses the stem tissue on the lower side before cutting. A bypass lopper has two curved blades that pass each other like scissors: the cut is clean on both sides. For hedge renovation, where the cut stem needs to callous over and regenerate, a bypass cut causes less tissue damage. Anvil loppers are cheaper and easier to sharpen but are the wrong tool for living wood you want to recover.

Open the blade fully before each cut on thicker material. Closing the lopper blade from halfway gives you less leverage and can cause the stem to slip sideways in the blade rather than cutting cleanly. Open the blades to their full extent, position the stem at the base of the blade (furthest from the tip), then close in a single deliberate movement.

Cut at a slight angle. A perfectly horizontal cut on a vertical stem leaves a flat surface where water pools and rot can establish. A slight downward angle of 10 to 15 degrees allows water to run off the cut surface. Not critical on a small stem that will callous over quickly, but good practice on thicker material.

Keep blades clean and lightly oiled. Laurel sap in particular is thick and sticky and gums up lopper blades quickly. After every half-hour of work on laurel or privet, wipe the blades with a rag, apply a light spray of WD-40 or similar, and wipe off again. It makes a noticeable difference to cut quality and to the effort needed.

When it goes beyond loppers

If the stems are consistently above 6 to 7 cm, the material is very dense, or the hedge is several metres tall, that is usually the point where the job becomes professional work: the volume of material to remove and handle, and the access equipment needed to work safely at height, make it a full day's job for a team.
