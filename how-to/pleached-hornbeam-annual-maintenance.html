Annual maintenance for a pleached hornbeam screen

A pleached hornbeam screen is relatively straightforward to maintain once the framework is established. Here is how the annual work compares with pleached beech, and what each species needs through the year.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Pleached hornbeam screens appear in increasing numbers across East Kent gardens, particularly in Sandwich, Ash and Woodnesborough, where people want privacy above a fence height without the bulk of a full hedge. The idea is a row of trees trained flat, like a hedge on stilts, with a clear leg below and a formal canopy above. Getting that framework established is the hard part; maintaining it once it is there is a job you can do yourself in a couple of hours each year with the right approach.

The two species most commonly used for pleaching in this part of Kent are hornbeam (Carpinus betulus) and beech (Fagus sylvatica). Visually they produce a similar result. The practical differences in maintenance are real but not dramatic, and are worth understanding if you are deciding between them or already have one planted.

Nesting season: check before you cut

Under the UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, damaging an active bird's nest is an offence. Nesting season runs roughly from 1 March to 31 August. A pleached screen holds its canopy close and tight, which makes excellent nest cover for sparrows, great tits and chaffinches. The safe window for the main annual cut is September to February. If you need to trim between March and August, check carefully for occupied nests first. A light tidy of stray new shoots in early summer is usually low risk, but inspect before you start.

Before you start: kit and safety

  • Eye protection: mandatory when cutting above head height; clippings fall toward you
  • Gloves: hornbeam and beech are not prickly but long sessions tire hands
  • Ladder or access platform: a pleached screen is typically cut at 2 to 4 metres; a stepladder and a pole trimmer often work better together than a tall ladder alone; always have someone footing the ladder
  • RCD adaptor for corded electric trimmers
  • Soft tree ties and new stakes: bring these on every visit; ties tighten over the season and need checking

Hornbeam versus beech: the key differences

Both hornbeam and beech produce a similar formal pleached screen and both are grown and maintained in essentially the same way. The comparison below draws out the differences that matter in practice.

Soil tolerance

Hornbeam is notably more tolerant of heavy clay and damp soils. In the Stour valley, where soil holds water for much of winter, hornbeam is the more reliable choice. Beech prefers well-drained ground: it performs well on chalk downland around Eastry and Worth, but can struggle on the heavier clay soils closer to Sandwich. If your site is on the wetter side, hornbeam is the safer option.

Winter leaf retention

Both species are marcescent: they hold their dead leaves through winter rather than dropping them in autumn. This is part of the appeal of pleached screens in both species, as the copper-brown winter canopy maintains some privacy and visual weight even after the season ends. In practice, beech holds its leaves slightly more reliably and they are a warmer reddish-brown; hornbeam leaves tend toward pale biscuit-brown and may drop a few more in a hard winter. Neither difference is dramatic from a few metres away.

Growth rate and cutting frequency

Hornbeam typically grows a little faster than beech once established. This means the annual cut on a hornbeam screen may produce more material to remove, particularly in the first few years after the main framework is complete. Some hornbeam screens in exposed positions or on rich soil also put on enough growth to benefit from a light supplementary tidy in June, before the main cut in August. Beech rarely needs this second pass.

Response to hard cutting

Both species respond well to being cut back hard. Hornbeam is probably the more forgiving of the two: it pushes new growth readily from old wood and recovers quickly from a renovation cut. Beech is slower to respond but still reliable, particularly on well-drained soils. If a pleached screen on either species has been left without cutting for several years and grown beyond its frame, the standard approach is to cut one side hard this year and the other the following year, leaving the untouched side to maintain the plant's energy while the cut side regrows.

The annual maintenance cycle

Late winter (January to early March)

Walk the screen and check the framework. Look for ties that have tightened against stems: these need cutting free and replacing with fresh soft ties. Any stems that have grown out of the frame or crossed badly can be removed now. Dead or damaged growth from autumn storms can also be cleared. This is not a full cut, just a tidy. Keep cuts clean and angled to shed rain. Mulch the root zone of each tree with a 7 to 10 cm layer of composted bark, keeping it clear of the stems themselves.

Late spring (April to June)

During nesting season, leave the canopy alone unless absolutely necessary. If a hornbeam screen is growing very vigorously, a few of the longest shoots can be pinched back or lightly trimmed in early June if you are certain no nests are occupied. For beech, leave it completely. This is a good time to check stakes: any that have shifted or rotted at the base should be replaced before summer growth adds weight to the canopy.

Late summer (August to early September)

This is the main annual cut. The nesting season has ended and the season's extension growth has hardened off enough to cut cleanly. Trim the top and both faces back to the frame, leaving 2 to 5 cm of new growth on the frame to thicken the canopy over time. Tie in any new lateral shoots that are growing along the frame in the direction you want; remove any that are growing at awkward angles or forward out of the plane. This tie-in work is as important as the cut itself: it is what keeps the canopy flat and the screen looking intentional.

Use a long-reach pole trimmer for the top of a tall screen, working from one end to the other in a single pass. Take the faces in separate passes. The finish should be flat and clean, like a vertical panel.

Autumn (October to November)

After leaf drop (or once leaves have browned in situ), do a final inspection of the framework. This is the best time to see the structure clearly without the canopy obscuring it. Any crossing stems or badly placed growth that was missed in August can be removed now. Apply a balanced fertiliser if growth looked thin this season.

Feeding and watering

An established pleached hornbeam or beech screen in reasonable soil rarely needs dedicated feeding. If the screen is on a poor, dry or very free-draining soil, as some gardens near the Sandwich Bay shoreline effectively are, a slow-release balanced fertiliser in April and watering through the first dry spell of summer will help maintain vigorous growth. Mulching the root zone matters more than feeding on most East Kent soils: it retains moisture, suppresses weeds and steadily improves the soil structure as it breaks down. Keep the mulch away from the stems themselves to avoid rot.
