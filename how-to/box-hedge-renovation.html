Box hedge renovation: renovate or replace?

An overgrown box hedge is a common problem in East Kent gardens. Whether to cut it back hard or start again depends on the health of the hedge. Here is how to make that call and, if renovation is right, how to do it.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Box hedges grow slowly compared to hornbeam or beech: around 15 to 20 cm per year for a healthy established hedge. A hedge that has been missed for a few seasons will not have the alarming volume of overgrowth you get with a neglected leylandii or privet, but it will have widened, softened and lost its defined lines. The more important question for box, more than it is for most other hedging species, is not how to renovate but whether to renovate at all. That depends largely on the health status of the hedge.

The nesting season check

The UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs roughly from 1 March to 31 August. The safe renovation window is September to February. See our guide on wildlife-friendly hedge trimming for the full legal detail.

First question: does the hedge have box blight?

Before deciding whether to renovate or replace, check for signs of box blight (Cylindrocladium buxicola): brown patches in the foliage, black streaking on younger stems, leaves falling from affected sections leaving bare patches. Box blight spreads in wet conditions and is now common in East Kent: the humid periods in late summer provide ideal conditions. See the separate guide on box blight treatment for diagnosis and management. The key point for a renovation decision is:

  • Light or localised blight: renovation is still a reasonable option, combined with hygiene measures and disinfecting tools between cuts.
  • Severe or widespread blight across most of the hedge: renovation will likely be followed by rapid reinfection. This is the strongest reason to consider replacement with a blight-tolerant alternative such as ilex crenata or pittosporum.

Renovate or replace: a comparison

Renovate Replace
Best for Healthy hedge that is overgrown but structurally sound Heavily blighted, gappy or structurally compromised hedge
Timescale to good appearance 1–2 seasons for sides; 2–3 seasons for full shape 3–5 years from new planting
Cost Lower upfront; mainly labour Higher upfront; plants plus preparation
Risk Low for healthy box; high for blighted material Low once new plants are in
Disease status Avoid if active widespread blight Opportunity to switch to a resistant species

If you renovate: hand shears versus power trimmer

Box renovation involves two distinct types of cut: the outer layer of lighter new growth, and the structural reduction into older wood. The right tool depends on what you are cutting.

Hand shears or loppers are better for:

  • Stems over 10 to 12 mm in diameter. A hedge trimmer blade will stall, skip or tear on thick stems, producing rough cuts that are slower to heal.
  • Detail work around corners and curves, where the angle of cut matters.
  • Working on a hedge you suspect has blight. Hand tools are easier to disinfect between plants (a 10% bleach solution or a dedicated disinfectant spray on the blades between cuts). Transmitting blight on contaminated blades from plant to plant is a real risk.

A battery or petrol hedge trimmer is better for:

  • Large volumes of lighter outer growth, where the speed advantage is significant.
  • Long straight sections where a consistent cutting line is easier to hold with a powered tool.
  • Faster clearance before a secondary pass with hand tools for the structural cuts.

For a typical overgrown garden box hedge, a combined approach works best: trimmer for the outer material, then loppers or shears for the main reduction cuts where the stems are thicker.

STIHL GB's demonstration of cutting back an overgrown hedge with a battery trimmer covers the main technique points for working on dense, structured hedges: approach angle, overlapping passes and managing the volume of arisings. The same principles apply to box renovation, with the addition of tool hygiene where blight is a concern.

When to do the renovation cut

October and November are the ideal months for box renovation in East Kent. The hedge is still in leaf, so you can see the full extent of overgrowth and the shape you are working toward. The coastal influence around Sandwich and Deal means mild enough temperatures to avoid frost damage to cut surfaces. February is also workable for the second year of a staged cut once the main winter frosts have passed.

If the hedge has had box blight, avoid cutting in wet weather: blight spores spread through water splash and wet foliage. A dry spell in October or November is the ideal window. Clean and disinfect all blades before you start and between sections if the hedge has had any blight history.

What to expect from regrowth

Box is slow to show regrowth after a hard renovation cut. Do not expect visible new growth until late April or May. By midsummer the cut surfaces will normally be showing new shoots, and by the second season the shape will be re-establishing. A box hedge that is genuinely healthy underneath, with a good root system and no ongoing blight infection, will recover well.

Hedges in the well-drained chalk soils east of Sandwich can dry out more quickly in summer: a mulch at the base in spring helps the recovering root system get enough moisture during the first summer after a renovation cut. Keep the mulch clear of the main stems to avoid moisture build-up at the base.
