Lavender hedge care and pruning

Lavender is one of the few hedging plants that does better the harder you cut it. Here is the checklist for getting it right, and the one mistake that kills hedges overnight.

Updated September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Lavender hedges are reliable in the right place and a mess in the wrong one. The right place is a well-drained, sunny spot, ideally on chalk or sandy soil with some shelter from the prevailing wind. East Kent delivers all of that in most gardens south of the A256. The wrong place is a shady corner on heavy clay that sits wet from November to March. Lavender roots rot fast in those conditions.

Pruning is where most people go wrong. Either they are too timid and end up with a hedge that sprawls open in the middle, or they cut into bare wood and watch half the plants die. Here is the checklist.

The annual lavender pruning checklist

  1. Know your variety before you cut. English lavender (Lavandula angustifolia: Hidcote, Munstead) flowers June to August and is cut in late July to early September. French or Spanish lavender (Lavandula stoechas: Papillon) flowers May to June and should be cut straight after those flowers fade, typically in late June or July. Hybrid lavender (Lavandula x intermedia: Grosso, Vera) flowers July to September and is cut in early September.
  2. Wait until flowering is genuinely finished. The flowers lose their colour and dry on the stalk. Cut too early and you lose weeks of pollinator value for no gain.
  3. Check for nesting birds in and around the hedge. See the WCA 1981 note below.
  4. Cut back by one-third to half of the current season's green growth. The target is a neat, rounded mound or a flat-topped edge, not a shaved flat.
  5. Stay in the green. Never cut into bare, woody stems. The moment your secateurs or shears are clicking on hard grey wood with no leaves, you have gone too deep.
  6. Remove all the old flower stalks completely. Dead flower stalks left on make the hedge look untidy through winter and attract disease.
  7. Collect all cuttings promptly. Lavender cuttings heap up fast and if left on the ground can encourage grey mould (Botrytis).
  8. Do not feed in autumn. A light dressing of a balanced fertiliser in April or May is enough to get the season going. Autumn feeding encourages soft growth that gets hit by winter.

Nesting season: check before you cut

Lavender hedges are rarely nesting sites themselves (too low, too open in structure), but garden birds often nest in adjacent shrubs and hedges that would be disturbed during cutting work. It is an offence under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 to damage or destroy an active nest. Take sixty seconds to check hedges, shrubs and climbers within a metre or two before starting work from late August onwards. If you find an active nest, wait until it is empty before trimming nearby.

Tools and safety

What you need

  • Sharp bypass secateurs for individual plants and small hedges.
  • Single-handed hedge shears or a lightweight cordless trimmer for a long run of lavender edging.
  • Garden gloves: lavender oils are generally harmless but some people find them irritating.
  • Keep blades clean and sharp. Ragged cuts through lavender stems heal slowly and are an entry point for Botrytis.

Varieties for East Kent

Lavandula angustifolia 'Hidcote'

The go-to for a low formal hedge. Compact, up to 50 cm, with very dark purple flowers. Hardy, reliable on chalk, and good-looking all winter with its silver-green foliage. Best for a defined path edge or border front.

Lavandula angustifolia 'Munstead'

Slightly taller than Hidcote, paler purple. A slightly more relaxed look. Very hardy and well-suited to East Kent's continental climate.

Lavandula x intermedia 'Grosso'

The tall, grey-leaved hybrid used commercially for oil production. Can reach 90 cm, with long elegant flower spikes. Less formally neat than Hidcote but more impactful as a loose screen. Less cold-hardy than the English varieties: protect the crown with a dry mulch in a hard winter.

Lavandula stoechas (French or Spanish lavender)

The one with the distinctive petal "ears" on top of each flower head. Flowers earlier, often from May, and needs pruning sooner. Less hardy than the English varieties: fine in a sheltered Kent garden but can be killed by a hard frost in an exposed spot.

Lavender in East Kent soils

The chalk downs and sandy coastal soils of the Sandwich and Deal area are almost ideal for lavender. Free-draining, alkaline, sunny: the plant has evolved in conditions not unlike the hillsides of southern France. If you garden on the chalk or sandy loam north-east of Sandwich, you can plant lavender with confidence.

On heavier clay soils further inland, drainage is the problem. Add horticultural grit to the planting hole, plant on a slight mound, and consider raised beds. Waterlogged roots in winter kill lavender reliably.

Planting spacing

For a continuous hedge or edging: plant at 30 to 45 cm centres for a dense, solid edge. Closer spacing fills in faster but gives each plant less airflow, which encourages disease. If you are willing to wait one extra season, 45 cm spacing gives healthier plants and costs less.

Plant in spring or early autumn. Avoid planting in a dry spell in July or August: lavender establishes much better when the soil has some moisture in it.

How long does a lavender hedge last?

A lavender hedge that is cut back properly every year typically gives you ten to fifteen years before the plants become too woody and uneven. There is no way to rejuvenate a plant that has gone badly woody: lavender does not regrow from old bare wood. When patches look exhausted, replace them. It is better to replant a section than to try to coax life from a hedge that has had its run.
