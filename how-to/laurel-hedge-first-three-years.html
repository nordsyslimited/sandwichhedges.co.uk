Laurel hedge: first three years

Cherry laurel is one of the fastest-establishing hedges you can plant in East Kent. Getting the first three years right means using the right tool at the right time, and not rushing the shape before the roots are ready.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Cherry laurel (Prunus laurocerasus) is the most popular evergreen hedging plant in Kent. Walk around any of the larger gardens near Sandwich, Eastry or Woodnesborough and you will almost certainly find a mature laurel hedge along at least one boundary. It is fast (30 to 60 cm per year once established), it tolerates the mild coastal winters well, it provides year-round screening, and it recovers strongly from hard renovation cuts if it is ever let go. What it does not tolerate well is being cut with the wrong tool, which is the single most common mistake in year two and three.

What follows is the year-by-year programme we use on new laurel hedges across East Kent.

Nesting season: 1 March to 31 August

Cherry laurel is a dense, sheltered evergreen and excellent nesting habitat. Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 it is an offence to intentionally damage or destroy an active nest. Nesting season runs approximately 1 March to 31 August. The safe cutting window for significant trimming is September to February. Only trim between March and August after checking carefully that the hedge is demonstrably empty. If there is any doubt, leave it.

Before any cutting work: PPE

Wear safety glasses (laurel sheds woody debris when cut), sturdy gloves and ear defenders with a powered trimmer. Prefer a pole trimmer to a ladder for hedges above chest height: overreaching sideways on a stepladder is a common cause of garden falls. Electric trimmer users: run the cable behind you and use an RCD adaptor. Check for wasps' nests at the base before starting.

At planting

Cherry laurel is almost always sold as pot-grown or root-balled stock rather than bare-root: unlike privet or hawthorn, it does not reliably survive being lifted without soil on its roots during the dormant season. This means it can go in year-round, as long as the ground is not frozen or waterlogged. The best time for new plantings in East Kent is September to November, when the soil is still warm enough to encourage root establishment before the first frosts, and the risk of summer drought in year one is avoided.

At planting, the formative cut is light, not severe. Privet benefits from being cut to 15 cm at planting; laurel does not. Instead, remove any physically damaged or crossing stems with secateurs. If the plant has very tall, leggy leaders that would look out of proportion with the other plants in the row, reduce them by roughly a third. Otherwise, leave the plant alone and let it concentrate on putting down roots.

Space pot-grown plants at 60 to 75 cm centres for a formal hedge, or 75 to 90 cm for a more informal boundary. Closer spacing fills faster but costs more. Apply a 7 to 10 cm mulch of composted bark along the full planting strip, keeping it clear of the stems.

Year 1: roots first

The priority in year one is water. Cherry laurel is not particularly drought-tolerant as a young plant, and the chalk downland around Sandwich Bay and the coastal strip near Deal dries out quickly in June and July. Water once a week in dry spells from April to September, aiming for a good soak at the base rather than a surface sprinkle. If the soil around the roots stays moist through the first summer, a well-sited laurel will put on 30 to 40 cm of new growth by autumn.

Keep the base strip weed-free for the entire first year. Competing grass and weeds draw water and nutrients at the critical establishment stage and significantly slow root development.

Do not cut the hedge at all during the main nesting season (1 March to 31 August). In late September or October of year one, once the growing season has ended and the nesting question is settled, give the hedge a light formative trim with secateurs. This is not about shaping at this stage: it is about encouraging the lower branches to thicken. Shorten any very long, unbranched leaders by about a quarter to a third, cutting to just above a leaf node. Leave shorter side shoots alone.

Year 2: beginning to shape

By spring of year two, a well-watered laurel hedge planted the previous autumn will be visibly thicker than it was at planting, with new side shoots branching from the main stems. Continue watering in dry spells through year two, though the established root system by this stage makes it more resilient than in year one.

The year-two cut is in late August or September. Use secateurs, not hedge shears or a powered trimmer. This is the rule that most laurel owners get wrong, and it matters. Hedge shears and trimmers slice through the large leaves of cherry laurel, leaving brown, shredded cut surfaces that are very slow to green up and look poor through winter. Secateurs cut cleanly through individual stems without damaging the remaining leaves. For a hedge at the size it reaches in year two, working with secateurs is not impractical: it takes longer, but the finished result is significantly better.

At the year-two cut, begin establishing the finished profile. Trim the sides to a slight batter (wider at the base, narrowing slightly toward the top). This angle allows light to reach the lower face and maintains a fuller base over time. If the top leaders have reached the target height, cut them back to that height. If not, leave the top alone for another season.

Year 3: full maintenance begins

By year three, a well-established cherry laurel hedge is typically growing at close to its full rate. In a good growing season in Kent's mild climate, this can mean 30 to 60 cm of new growth in a single year on the sides and top combined. One main cut per year (late August to September) is usually sufficient to keep it tidy, though fast-growing hedges on fertile soils may need a second, lighter pass in late June to avoid an unkempt appearance through summer.

By this point, the hedge should have a consistent height and width, a dense, even face with no significant gaps, and a well-established profile. The transition from the formative stage to the annual maintenance stage is essentially complete.

Secateurs remain the preferred tool for the finished surface of an established laurel hedge if appearance matters. For long runs where secateurs are impractical, a hedge trimmer used on a light pass in late summer (taking no more than the current season's growth) gives an acceptable finish. Use loppers on any thick woody stems at the back.

At any point in the first three years, if the hedge has a significant gap at the base from the boundary side, check whether the neighbouring property has shade or root competition that could be drawing the laurel. For general guidance on managing gaps, see fixing bare patches in a hedge.
