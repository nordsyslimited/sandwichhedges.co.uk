Managing a native hedgerow on a rotational cycle

A practical guide to cutting a mixed native hedge in sections rather than all at once, with the benefits that brings for birds, small mammals and pollinators across East Kent.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

A native mixed hedge, the kind with hawthorn, blackthorn, hazel, field maple, elder and dog rose all jostling for space, is one of the most productive wildlife habitats you can have in a garden. But the way most people manage it, cutting it back on the same sides every year to a neat rectangular profile, quietly chips away at most of what makes it valuable.

Rotational cutting is the alternative. You divide the hedge into sections and cut a different section each year, so at any point part of the hedge is in full berry and another part is thickening up after its cut. It takes no more time than normal hedge cutting. It just requires a bit of planning first.

Nesting season: the law first

The Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to damage or destroy the nest of any wild bird while it is in use. Nesting season runs from 1 March to 31 August. Any cutting, trimming or coppicing of a native hedgerow that could disturb nesting birds must wait until the season ends. The safe cutting window is October to February. November and December are particularly good months: berries have largely been taken, birds are not nesting, and the hedge is fully dormant.

Before you start: tool and personal safety

Native hedges contain thorns. Hawthorn and blackthorn both draw blood through most gloves, so wear thick leather gauntlets rather than thin nitrile. Eye protection is essential when using a powered trimmer near dense thorny growth. For electric trimmers, use an RCD adaptor. If you need height to reach the top of the hedge, use a secure step platform rather than a ladder wherever possible, and never lean sideways over thorny growth without somebody footing below. Check the hedge base for wasp nests before starting in autumn.

Step 1: map the hedge and mark your sections

Walk the length of your hedge and count the metres. A typical garden boundary hedge of 20 to 40 metres divides easily into two halves (for a 2-year rotation) or three sections (for a 3-year rotation). Longer farm-boundary hedges of 80 metres or more work well in thirds.

Mark the section boundaries with a short bamboo cane pushed into the hedge base, or a spray of brightly coloured paint on the ground. Write the sections down: something as simple as "Section A, metres 0-12. Section B, metres 12-25" in a notebook or a phone note is enough to keep the rotation on track year after year.

For most garden hedges in Sandwich, Worth, Ash and the villages around East Kent, a 2-year rotation is the right starting point. Farms and longer boundaries benefit from a 3-year cycle, which leaves more of the hedge in berry at any given point.

Step 2: decide what each year's cut includes

Rotational management means cutting the sides and top of one section hard, whilst leaving other sections largely untouched. You are not trying to keep the whole hedge at an identical height: you want variation in height, age and density across the hedge's length.

For a 2-year rotation: cut Section A hard this winter, leave Section B. Next winter, cut Section B, leave Section A. The A sections never grow for more than two years before being cut back; Section B never goes more than two years either. The hedge stays manageable whilst never having its entire berry and nesting resource removed at once.

For a 3-year rotation: cut one third each winter, leaving the other two-thirds. By the time you return to cut Section A again, it will have grown for three years, producing abundant haws, sloes and hips in its second and third years before the cut.

Step 3: understand which species benefit most from the rest period

Hawthorn produces the best berry crop in its second and third years after a cut. Blackthorn sloes ripen in September and October and are most prolific on 2- to 3-year growth. Elder berries are produced on young wood from the current season, so elder in a rotational hedge rarely lacks fruit.

Dog rose produces hips from late September into January in Kent, which is exactly when fieldfares and redwings arrive on the coast near Sandwich Bay and Deal looking for food. Hips form on the previous year's wood, so an uncut section always has the best supply. Hazel nuts form on 1- to 3-year wood; leaving a section uncut for two years dramatically improves nut yield.

For nesting, the dense, thorny interior of an uncut hawthorn or blackthorn section is exactly what a dunnock, whitethroat or song thrush chooses. A freshly cut section will be used again the following spring once it has some regrowth, but the most complex and protected nesting sites are in 2- to 3-year growth.

Step 4: time the cut correctly within the safe window

Once nesting season ends on 31 August, you have a wide window. November is often the best month in East Kent: the hedge has finished fruiting, most migratory birds have moved through, and the weather is mild enough to work in comfortably. Cutting in December and January is also fine for dormant species.

Avoid cutting during frost or when the cut faces strong east winds off the sea: fresh cut ends on hawthorn and blackthorn can suffer die-back if frozen within a few days of cutting. If a cold snap is forecast, wait.

Do not cut the top growth of blackthorn in wet weather: the cut ends are an entry point for fungal disease. A dry November day is better than a damp December one.

Step 5: what "leaving a section" actually means

Leaving an uncut section does not mean complete neglect. You can and should:

  • Remove any dead wood that is clearly diseased rather than naturally dying (honey fungus and canker spread from dead wood to living stems).
  • Cut any overhanging branches that shade the path or road below the hedge.
  • Clear brambles that are climbing through the base of the uncut section and pulling the hedge sideways.
  • Tidy the hedge base if it is encroaching on a lawn or border.

What you do not do is cut the sides and top to the previous year's profile. Leave the top and sides alone and let the section grow out freely. Some sections of a mature rotational hedgerow will develop into a broad, slightly billowing shape as opposed to the flat-faced rectangle of an annually clipped garden hedge. That is by design.

Step 6: record and review each year

Keep a note of which section you cut each winter, and roughly when. After 3 to 5 years you will start to see real differences between sections: the uncut years will visibly carry more berries and denser internal structure. In practice, most people who start a rotational cycle become reluctant to cut their "berry section" when the time comes, which is exactly the right instinct.

Review the rotation every 3 years. If one section has become too wide and is encroaching badly on a path or neighbouring boundary, you can cut it harder in its cutting year and bring it back. A rotational hedge is not rigid: the principle is to always leave some of the hedge in berry, not to follow a timetable precisely.
