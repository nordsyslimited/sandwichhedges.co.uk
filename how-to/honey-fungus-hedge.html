Honey fungus in garden hedges

The most common cause of woody plant death in UK gardens, and one of the hardest to deal with. What to look for, how to confirm it, and what to do once you have.

Updated August 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Five warning signs of honey fungus in a hedge

  • Honey-coloured toadstools appearing at the base of one or more hedge plants in late summer or autumn (September to November). They grow in clusters and have a pale ring on the stem. Not always present, but diagnostic when they are.
  • White fan-shaped mycelium visible under the bark at ground level when you peel back a small section of bark at the base. Smells strongly of mushrooms. This is one of the clearest signs of honey fungus specifically.
  • Black bootlace-like rhizomorphs in the soil around the roots when you dig down. These are the spreading strands the fungus uses to travel through the soil from plant to plant.
  • Progressive die-back along a hedge run: one plant dies, then the next, then the next in sequence. This pattern reflects the rhizomorphs travelling along the root line underground.
  • Sudden collapse with no obvious cause: a plant that seemed healthy wilts and dies in a matter of weeks, despite adequate water and no sign of drought stress. The roots are being killed from below before the above-ground symptoms appear.

Honey fungus is the common name for several related species of Armillaria fungi. Of the species found in UK gardens, A. mellea (the most aggressive) and A. gallica are the ones most likely to cause problems in hedges. The RHS consistently lists honey fungus as the single most commonly reported cause of plant death in UK gardens, above drought, pests and all other diseases combined.

For a garden hedge, the consequences can be severe. Unlike a border plant that dies and leaves a gap in a bed, a section of hedge dying takes privacy screening, windbreak and wildlife habitat with it. And because honey fungus spreads through underground rhizomorphs, the plants either side of the first casualty are often already infected before you notice anything wrong.

Why honey fungus is particularly hard on hedges

A hedge is a row of plants growing in close proximity with their root systems overlapping or intertwined. That is ideal habitat for rhizomorphs, which spread through the soil in straight lines, attacking the next root system they encounter. In a mature established hedge, adjacent plants' roots may be in contact, making the path of spread direct and fast.

Kent gardens with old tree stumps in or near a hedge are at particular risk. Honey fungus lives in dead woody material (stumps and buried roots), using it as a food source while sending out rhizomorphs. A stump from a felled tree left in place for years can sustain a colony that eventually spreads laterally into a nearby hedge. We see this fairly regularly in properties in Ash, Eastry and Woodnesborough, where older gardens have existing large trees and established hedging growing close together.

Confirming the diagnosis

The bark test

The most reliable single check is to take a penknife or small tool and carefully lift a small section of bark from the base of a dying or recently dead plant, at or just below ground level. If honey fungus is present, you will see white, fan-shaped sheets of mycelium between the bark and the wood. The smell is distinctive: strong, earthy mushroom. This mycelium is not always visible at the stem surface, but it is almost always present under the bark at ground level when the plant has been killed by honey fungus.

Digging for rhizomorphs

In autumn and early winter, dig carefully around the base and roots of an affected plant. Honey fungus produces black or dark brown rhizomorphs (sometimes called "bootlaces") that run through the soil. They are round in cross-section, typically 2 to 3 mm across, and have a firm outer coat. These are distinct from ordinary fungal mycelium strands and are a clear sign of Armillaria activity.

The toadstools

Honey-coloured toadstools appear in autumn at the base of infected plants, sometimes in large clusters. They have a pale ring on the stem and a slightly sticky cap. However, several other fungi also produce similar-looking toadstools near dead wood, so the toadstools alone are not a definitive diagnosis without the mycelium or rhizomorphs to confirm. The RHS's video "Honey Fungus: What you need to know" (Royal Horticultural Society, YouTube) gives a clear visual guide to distinguishing honey fungus toadstools from common lookalikes, which is helpful before you take drastic action on the basis of toadstools alone.

What to do once confirmed

Remove infected plants and roots promptly

There is no chemical treatment for honey fungus available to UK gardeners. The management approach is physical: remove the infected plant along with as much root material as you can excavate. This deprives the fungus of its food source in that location. Do not shred or compost the infected material; bag it for general waste or burn it if your local authority allows garden bonfires.

The difficulty is that leaving even a small section of root behind can sustain the colony. For an established hedge plant with a large root system, total removal is genuinely hard work. At minimum, remove the main root ball and all visible large laterals you can reach.

Remove one plant either side

If a plant is confirmed dead from honey fungus, the plants immediately adjacent on each side are very likely already infected even if they show no symptoms yet. The RHS recommends removing at least one plant either side of a confirmed case before replanting. This is a hard call when those plants look healthy, but it prevents the cycle from repeating immediately after you have replanted the gap.

Install a physical barrier before replanting

Before putting new plants in, install a vertical barrier in the soil around the replanting area. A butyl pond liner or heavy-duty polythene sheet at least 0.5 mm thick, sunk to at least 45 cm depth, will block rhizomorphs from re-entering from the surrounding soil. Leave 2 to 3 cm of the barrier above the soil surface to stop rhizomorphs going over the top. This is particularly important if the source is likely in a neighbouring garden (old stumps across a fence line) or in part of your own garden you cannot fully clear.

Let the soil rest, or replant with resistant species

If you can leave the area fallow for a season or two, the fungus in that patch will die back without a food source. If replanting sooner, choose species with better tolerance. Box (Buxus sempervirens) is a practical choice for formal sections of a Kent hedge, and it performs well in the mild coastal conditions along Sandwich Bay and Deal seafront. For mixed or wildlife sections, hawthorn and blackthorn are both worth considering. Avoid replanting the same species (privet, laurel, beech, leylandii) into an area with known honey fungus without the physical barrier.

What not to do

Do not leave old stumps in or near the hedge. Stumps are the main reservoir. If you have recently felled a tree near the hedge, have the stump ground out rather than leaving it to decay in place.

Do not pour bleach or other chemicals onto the area. No fungicide is registered for honey fungus control in UK garden use, and bleach or other caustic treatments will damage the soil and the plants either side without helping the underlying problem.

Do not assume the toadstools alone are confirmation. Several innocuous species produce similar-looking toadstools near dead wood. Check for the white mycelium under the bark or the black rhizomorphs in the soil before removing healthy plants.

A note on Kent gardens specifically

The older, more established gardens in and around Sandwich, Ash and Woodnesborough often have large mature trees growing close to hedging that was planted later. When those trees eventually come down, the stumps left in place can quietly sustain honey fungus colonies for a decade or more. If you have had a tree removed and your hedge starts dying in sections a few years later, honey fungus from the old stump is one of the first things to check.

Soil conditions also play a role. The well-drained sandy soils near Sandwich Bay and Deal seafront are less likely to harbour the related Phytophthora root rot (which prefers waterlogged conditions) but are not immune to honey fungus. Heavy clay soils further inland, where drainage is slower, can favour both.
