Protecting tender hedges from frost in Kent

East Kent's coastal position helps, but inland gardens around Ash, Woodnesborough and Eastry can hit -5°C in January. Here is what we do with the vulnerable species before the cold arrives.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Kent gets away with a lot. Mild sea air, free-draining soils, sheltered spots near the coast. People plant things here that would be risky further inland, and usually they get away with it. But usually is not always, and a cold snap in February is enough to set back a bay hedge by two or three years if it was planted on a frost pocket site and nobody thought to mulch the roots.

This guide covers the species most likely to suffer, and what you can realistically do in October and November before the worst of the cold arrives.

Winter is the main trimming window, not frost protection time

Nesting season runs from 1 March to 31 August. The proper cutting window for most hedges is September to February. If your tender hedge needs shaping before you protect it for winter, do that first, in September or October. Avoid cutting tender species hard after the end of October, as fresh wounds going into frost can allow cold into the stem. Renovation cuts on tender hedges belong in spring, once temperatures have settled.

Step 1: Know which species need help

Hardy hedging species, yew, beech, hornbeam, hawthorn, privet, most hollies, do not need frost protection. They are perfectly at home in a Kent winter. The ones that benefit from some care are:

Bay laurel (Laurus nobilis)

Bay is the one we see frost damage on most often in East Kent. The large leaves scorch badly in a sharp frost combined with a cold wind, and established hedges can drop half their leaves in a cold January. Young plants or recently planted hedges are most at risk. The roots usually survive but the regrowth can be patchy. A mulch around the base, plus a windbreak fleece on the exposed side in hard winters, makes a real difference.

Pittosporum tenuifolium

Pittosporum is widely planted for its compact growth and glossy foliage, particularly in Sandwich Bay and coastal gardens where it copes well with salt air. It is reliably hardy in a mild Kent winter but will drop leaves and suffer stem dieback if temperatures stay below -5°C for more than a couple of nights. Young plants under two years old should be fleece-protected until they have put on a good root system.

Ceanothus (California lilac)

Ceanothus grown as a hedge is borderline-hardy in Kent. It survives most winters on a south-facing boundary or against a wall but can be killed back to the ground in a cold, wet winter inland. The root system often survives and re-sprouts from the base, but this takes the hedge back several years. Mulching heavily and protecting young plants with fleece in the first two or three winters reduces the risk considerably.

Escallonia

Escallonia is tougher than it looks and established plants generally cope well with East Kent winters. It is the new plantings, and hedges in exposed positions, that need care. Brown or bronze foliage in late winter is a sign of frost scorch rather than death; most established escallonia hedges will regrow from the lower branches even after significant dieback above.

Eucalyptus

Eucalyptus grown as screening is marginal in exposed Kent positions. E. gunnii is the hardiest commonly grown species and tolerates short periods below -10°C once established, but young trees in their first three winters need protection. Coppicing eucalyptus in late spring, cutting it back hard each year, is often practised as much to keep the size manageable as for hardiness, but a heavily coppiced plant going into its first winter is very vulnerable.

Step 2: Mulch the root zone in October

The single most useful thing you can do for a tender hedge before winter is put down a mulch. A layer of 7 to 10 cm of composted bark, well-rotted garden compost, or straw placed around the base of the hedge in October or November does two things: it insulates the soil against rapid temperature swings, and it retains moisture that evergreen hedges continue to need through winter.

On East Kent's sandy and chalk-based soils around Sandwich, Worth and Ash, moisture retention during dry winter spells matters. Evergreens lose water through their leaves all winter; if the soil freezes or dries out the roots cannot replace it, and you get scorch that looks like frost damage but is partly drought damage.

Keep the mulch a few centimetres back from the main stems. Mulch piled up against the bark traps moisture and encourages collar rot, particularly in a wet winter.

Step 3: Stop feeding by September

Do not apply nitrogen-rich fertiliser after the end of August. Late-season feeding produces soft, sappy growth that is much more vulnerable to frost than growth that has had time to harden off. If your hedge needs feeding at all, the time is early spring, as growth is starting, not in the summer when it looks like it needs a boost.

Potassium-rich feeds (tomato fertiliser, sulphate of potash) in August can help the new season's growth harden off before winter but are not essential for established hedges on decent soil.

Step 4: Decide whether fleece is needed

Fleece is worth considering for young plants, recently planted hedges (under two years), and species in known frost pockets. It provides around 2 to 3 degrees of frost protection, which can make the difference in a moderate Kent winter.

Horticultural fleece (not builder's fleece or shade netting) is what you want. Secure it over the top and down the sides but leave some air circulation if you can, and do not leave it on permanently. Condensation under a sealed fleece can cause fungal problems almost as damaging as the frost itself. Take it off when the cold spell passes, check the plant, and put it back when another cold period is forecast.

For a large established bay hedge or a wall-trained ceanothus, temporary windbreak netting fixed to posts on the exposed side provides enough shelter to prevent wind-chill scorch without the condensation risk of wrapping.

Step 5: Know when to intervene after frost

After a hard frost, resist the urge to cut out the apparently dead material immediately. Stems that look dead in February often have green cambium underneath and will re-shoot from lower down in April and May. The old rule is to wait until growth restarts before deciding what to remove. Scratch the bark with a fingernail: green underneath means alive, brown or dry means dead. Work inward from the tips until you find green, then cut just above the lowest green bud or pair of leaves.

For bay, the new growth tends to come from the base and from lateral buds rather than from the tip of frost-killed stems. Left unpruned until May, a frost-hit bay hedge often looks better than you expected.

East Kent frost pockets: where to be more careful

Cold air drains downhill and collects in low-lying ground. The villages of Ash, Woodnesborough, Eastry and the lower ground around Worth can be significantly colder on a still, clear night than Sandwich or Deal, even though they are only a few miles apart. If your garden sits at the bottom of a slope, in a hollow or in a walled enclosure where cold air cannot escape, treat your tender hedges as though they are in a colder climate zone than the coastal average suggests.

The coastal belt of Sandwich Bay, Pegwell Bay and the beaches around Deal benefits most from the maritime influence. Hedges there, particularly pittosporum and escallonia, often need no protection at all in a typical winter. But even there, the occasional polar air mass in January or February can surprise people who have grown complacent after a string of mild winters.
