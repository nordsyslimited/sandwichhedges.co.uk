How hedgerows help manage water and reduce flooding

A hedgerow is not just a boundary. Managed well, it is one of the cheapest and most effective tools for slowing surface water, filtering runoff and reducing the peak flood levels that affect farms, gardens and roads in East Kent.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

In many parts of East Kent, including the Stour valley between Sandwich and Canterbury and the low-lying ground around Worth and Ash, surface water flooding is a recurring problem. Roads flood after heavy autumn rain. Fields pond for days. Garden drainage runs off onto paths and drives.

Hedgerows are part of the answer, though rarely the whole answer. What they do well is slow the speed at which water moves across the land, buy time for it to soak into the soil rather than run off to the nearest road or ditch, and filter out the sediment and nutrients that cause problems downstream. This guide explains the mechanisms, and what that means for how you manage your hedge.

How a hedgerow slows water

The canopy intercepts rainfall

A dense hedge canopy catches a meaningful fraction of rainfall directly. The water either evaporates from the leaves or runs slowly down the stems to the base. Either way, it reaches the ground more slowly and spread over a larger area than falling rain. A thin, regularly scalped hedge loses most of this interception capacity.

Roots create water channels in the soil

This is the bigger effect. A mixed native hedgerow develops a dense, varied root system that penetrates to depths a garden border or grass strip rarely matches. Hawthorn, blackthorn and field maple send roots down 1.5 metres or more. Those roots, as they grow and die, create channels through the soil that let water move downward rather than across the surface. Once the channel network is established, even heavy rain in a short period can soak away faster than you would expect from the soil type alone.

The grass buffer strips water from the surface

The grassy base of a hedgerow is often undervalued. A strip of rough, uncut grass and low herbs on the field or garden side of the hedge slows water sheeting off an adjacent hard surface or cropped area, giving it time to soak in before reaching the hedge roots themselves. A mown-to-the-base or sprayed-off hedge bottom removes this buffer almost entirely.

The hedge line acts as a miniature berm

The slight raised bank that builds up under an established hedgerow, the result of decades of leaf litter, root action and undisturbed soil, provides a low physical barrier to lateral flow. This is particularly noticeable on gentle slopes, where water running downhill is slowed by each boundary hedge it crosses.

What this means for management

The water management benefits of a hedgerow depend on keeping the three components above (canopy, root system and grass buffer) in reasonable health. Several common management practices reduce them significantly:

  • Hard annual scalping, where the hedge is cut back to a fraction of its natural width every year, reduces canopy density and stresses the root system over time. Cutting to the same line every year also tends to push the hedge progressively inward, thinning the base.
  • Clearing the hedge base, whether by mowing, strimmers or herbicide, removes the grass buffer and often damages the surface root network.
  • Filling gaps with soil or hard material rather than new plants removes sections of the root network entirely.

Alternatives that preserve or improve the water management function:

  • Cut the hedge on a two or three year rotation rather than annually. This allows the canopy to recover density and gives the root system time to extend.
  • Leave the hedge base vegetation (grass and low herbs within 0.5-1 metre of the hedge line) uncut for the growing season. It can be mown in late autumn if neatness matters.
  • Cut to an A or batter profile: slightly wider at the base than the top. This keeps light reaching the lower stem and base vegetation, maintains a denser base, and is more stable in wind than a rectangular profile.
  • Where there are gaps in the hedge line, fill them with bare-root native species in winter rather than leaving them open.

Nesting season: the one rule that overrides everything

Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs roughly from 1 March to 31 August. No water management urgency changes this. The safe cutting window for hedges is September to February. If you need to do hedge work outside this window, the hedge must be checked carefully by a competent person before cutting begins.

Ditch and drainage work alongside a hedgerow

In East Kent, many boundary hedges sit beside drainage ditches, particularly around the lower-lying parishes of Worth, Ash, Woodnesborough and Eastry. The ditch and the hedge work together: the hedge slows the water before it reaches the ditch, and the ditch carries away what the soil cannot absorb.

Ditch clearance (removing accumulated silt, vegetation and debris) is maintenance work on the watercourse, separate from hedge cutting. In many cases it falls to the riparian owner, the person whose land the ditch adjoins. If the ditch is part of a formal drainage network, the relevant Internal Drainage Board (in this area, Southern Water or the Environment Agency) may have an interest. Check before undertaking significant ditch work: changing how water drains across a catchment can affect neighbours and watercourses downstream.

East Kent context

Kent's coastal plain around Sandwich Bay and the Stour estuary is low-lying and has a long history of drainage management. The hedgerow network here is sparser than inland, but the remaining boundary hedges, particularly those running across the grain of the slope, play a disproportionate role in managing the speed of runoff after heavy rain. The mild coastal winters (rarely hard frost before January on the coast between Deal and Sandwich Bay) mean hedges remain in active growth later into autumn and restart earlier in spring than inland equivalents, which extends the period during which root water uptake is significant.
