Phormium (New Zealand flax) as garden screening in Kent

A decision guide to using phormium for year-round garden privacy in East Kent: where it works, where it does not, and how to care for it once planted.

Updated September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Phormium (New Zealand flax) turns up a great deal in coastal East Kent gardens, and for good reason. The bold, upright leaves give year-round structure, they handle salt-laden Channel wind without flinching, and the plants are surprisingly tough once settled in. You see them regularly along the seafront gardens at Deal and in the exposed plots at Sandwich Bay where a conventional trimmed hedge would struggle.

Whether phormium is the right choice for your specific garden, though, depends on a few questions. Work through the decision points below before committing to a row of plants.

Is phormium right for your garden? A decision guide

Question 1: Is your garden position coastal or inland?

Coastal or exposed (Sandwich Bay, Deal seafront, Pegwell Bay area): Phormium is one of the best choices available. The tough, strap-like leaves are wind-firm in a way that many softer-leaved plants are not, and the salt air tolerance is genuine. Privet and laurel are the alternatives, but phormium gives a very different, more architectural look.

Inland and sheltered (Ash, Worth, Woodnesborough, Eastry): Phormium still works but you need to choose a sheltered, sunny position and a frost-hardy cultivar. Phormium tenax is the tougher species. If you are in a frost pocket, consider bamboo (Fargesia rufa) or hebe as alternatives. A severe prolonged freeze can damage or kill phormium at temperatures below -10 to -12 degrees Celsius.

Question 2: Do you want a formal or informal screen?

Informal, naturalistic, architectural: Phormium excels. A row of phormium planted at 1.2 metres apart gives a flowing, year-round screen with bold vertical lines. It suits a contemporary garden or a coastal plot where a more natural feel fits the setting.

Formal, clipped edge, neat geometric shape: Phormium is not the right plant. It cannot be clipped into a crisp formal shape. For a formal hedge on a boundary where you want a flat top and straight sides, choose privet, laurel, yew or box instead.

Question 3: Do you want wildlife value from the screen?

Wildlife is a priority: Phormium provides limited wildlife value compared to native hedge species. The tall flower spikes (on mature plants in summer) attract some pollinators, but there is no significant berry crop and the dense leaf bases are rarely used for nesting. If wildlife value matters, consider elder, hawthorn or a mixed native hedge alongside or instead.

Structure, privacy and low maintenance are the priority: Phormium is hard to beat. Once established it is virtually self-sufficient, needing only an annual tidy in spring and occasional division every four to five years if it outgrows its space.

Question 4: How much width can the screen take?

Very narrow strip (under 60 cm): Phormium will eventually outgrow a very narrow border. Compact cultivars like 'Yellow Wave' or 'Sundowner' help, but even these reach 1 to 1.5 metres across. For a genuinely narrow screen, a trellis with climbers or a formal clipped hedge is more practical.

60 cm to 1.5 metres of width available: Phormium works well. Allow 1.2 to 1.5 metres between plants and choose a compact cultivar unless you need maximum height.

Plenty of space: Full-size Phormium tenax or one of the larger cultivars like 'Purpureum' gives a bold statement. The leaf fans of a mature plant are impressive at 2 to 3 metres across.

Nesting season and phormium

Check before you cut: the legal position

Though phormium is less commonly used for nesting than hedgerow shrubs, birds do occasionally nest at the base of large established clumps, particularly where the leaf bases form a sheltered cavity at ground level. Under the UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 it is an offence to damage or destroy an active nest. Nesting season runs roughly from 1 March to 31 August. The safe window for any significant tidying or division work is September to February. In March or later, check carefully at the base of the plant before working on it.

Before you start any cutting or division work

Safety before you cut

  • Eye protection: phormium leaves taper to a sharp point and can flick into an eye during cutting. Wear safety glasses or goggles.
  • Sturdy gloves: the leaf edges are not sharp enough to cut like a blade, but the rough surface can cause friction burns. Leather or heavy-duty work gloves.
  • Ear defenders for any power tool use.
  • Prefer long-handled tools where possible. A pair of long-handled bypass loppers reaches into the centre of the plant more safely than reaching in with both arms. For large clumps, a long-handled serrated knife or pruning saw works well for cutting out old leaf fans at the base.
  • Stable footing: phormium clumps create uneven ground around the base. Check where you are standing before reaching in to cut.
  • Wasp check: in summer, the base of a phormium can shelter a wasp nest.

How to care for phormium

Pruning and tidying (spring, March to April)

Phormium does not need clipping. The annual maintenance is removing dead, brown or very old leaves. Identify each leaf to remove and cut it off at the base of the plant, as close to the ground as you can reach. Do not cut leaves partway down: the stub left behind turns brown and looks worse than the original dead leaf. Old flower stems can be removed at the same time once the flowers have finished.

Dividing an overgrown clump

Every four or five years, a large phormium may need dividing if it outgrows its space or becomes congested in the centre. This is best done in April or May. Use a sharp spade to cut through the clump, lift sections and replant them at the required spacing. Divisions taken from the outer edge of the clump are the most vigorous. Water the divisions in well and keep them from drying out through their first summer.

Feeding

Phormium on the free-draining chalk and sandy soils around Sandwich and Sandwich Bay will benefit from a general-purpose slow-release fertiliser in spring. This encourages strong, healthy leaf growth and maintains the colour of variegated cultivars.

Winter protection in a severe frost

In most East Kent winters, established phormium needs no winter protection. If a severe frost is forecast (below -8 degrees Celsius for more than a day or two, which is rare at the coast), you can wrap the crown of a young or smaller-leaved cultivar loosely in fleece. Remove it promptly once the frost passes. Large established plants of Phormium tenax generally shrug off any winter Kent is likely to throw at them.

Cultivar notes

The range of phormium cultivars in UK garden centres is wide, and the colours are genuinely varied. A few worth knowing:

  • Phormium tenax 'Purpureum': large, dark bronze-purple leaves, very striking, RHS Award of Garden Merit. Can reach 2 metres or more.
  • Phormium 'Yellow Wave': compact, bright yellow-green striped, around 1 metre tall. One of the better choices for a smaller garden or a narrower border.
  • Phormium 'Sundowner': bronze with pink edges, medium-sized. Good for the garden where you want a little colour rather than just green or bronze.
  • Phormium cookianum 'Tricolor': green, white and red-striped, arching rather than upright, around 1 to 1.2 metres. Less frost-hardy than P. tenax varieties.

Phormium is not for every garden. But for a coastal East Kent plot that needs year-round structure without constant maintenance, it is one of the most reliable choices you can make.
