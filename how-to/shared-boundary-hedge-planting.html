Planting a hedge on a shared boundary: what to sort out before you start

A hedge on a shared boundary is entirely legal and rarely needs planning permission. But a few practical checks before you order the plants can prevent a lot of difficulty later, whether that is a neighbour dispute in year three or a hedge that turns out to be on the wrong side of the boundary altogether.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

The pre-planting checklist at a glance

  1. Check your title deeds and boundary position
  2. Confirm no planning permission is needed (it rarely is)
  3. Check for restrictive covenants in your deeds
  4. Tell your neighbour what you are doing (not legally required, but practical)
  5. Choose species with mature size and maintenance in mind
  6. Plan the hedge position: set back from the boundary line
  7. Check the soil and underground services
  8. Time the planting to avoid nesting season for site preparation work
  9. Agree maintenance access arrangements if needed
  10. Plant, mulch and establish

1. Check your title deeds and boundary position

Before anything else, be clear about where your boundary actually is. Title register plans from the Land Registry show the general boundary, but they are not precise surveys: the red line on the plan is indicative and typically drawn to a scale where a millimetre on the plan represents a significant ground distance. If there is any doubt about where the boundary falls, a measured survey by a chartered surveyor is the reliable way to find out.

The Land Registry register for your property is available to download at a modest cost from the gov.uk website. If you do not have current copies of your title deeds, this is a good moment to get them.

2. Planning permission: you almost certainly do not need it

In England, planting a hedge does not require planning permission. A hedge is not a structure, so the rules that apply to fences and walls (permitted development heights of 2 metres, or 1 metre adjacent to a highway) do not apply. You can plant a hedge of any height on your land without needing permission in most circumstances.

Two situations that could change this: if your property is in a conservation area, there may be additional controls, so check with your local authority (Dover District Council covers most of East Kent). If your property is subject to an agricultural condition that restricts new planting, this would show in your title deeds. In practice, these restrictions are uncommon for ordinary residential gardens in the Sandwich and Deal area.

3. Check for restrictive covenants

Some residential plots, particularly on newer housing developments in East Kent, carry restrictive covenants in the title deeds that limit boundary treatments. These are private contractual obligations rather than planning rules, but they are enforceable. Read the charges register section of your title register carefully. If there are covenants, check whether they specify permitted boundary treatments or prohibit certain species or structures.

4. Tell your neighbour

There is no legal obligation to notify your neighbour before planting a hedge on your own land. But it is worth having a brief conversation first, particularly if the hedge will affect the light to their garden or windows as it matures. A conversation before planting, when the hedge is hypothetical, is easier than one three years later when a leylandii is six metres tall.

If your neighbour has any concerns about species choice, height or position, it is far simpler to address these at the planning stage than after the plants are in the ground. Most boundary hedge disputes that reach councils or solicitors have their roots in a conversation that never happened at the start.

5. Choose species with the future in mind

Species choice matters more on a shared boundary than anywhere else in the garden, because the hedge will grow whether or not you trim it on time, and a neighbour who ends up in shade from a fast-growing evergreen hedge they had no say in is unlikely to be pleased. A few points to work through:

  • Mature height: if you want a hedge of 1.5 to 2 metres, choose species that grow to that height naturally or that respond well to trimming at that height. Leylandii and laurel can be kept at 2 metres, but they require regular cutting to do so. A privet or hornbeam hedge at 1.5 to 2 metres is much easier to maintain at that height.
  • Evergreen versus deciduous: evergreen species (laurel, leylandii, Griselinia) give year-round privacy but also year-round shadow. A deciduous beech or hornbeam hedge provides more light in winter when the sun is already low.
  • High Hedges Act: if you plant an evergreen or semi-evergreen hedge of two or more plants that grows over 2 metres high, and it unreasonably affects your neighbour's enjoyment of their property, they can make a complaint to the council under Part 8 of the Anti-social Behaviour Act 2003. The council can issue a remedial notice requiring reduction. This is not a reason to avoid planting a hedge, but it is a reason to plan for a realistic maintained height. See our guide to high hedges and the law for the detailed position.
  • Wildlife value: a native mixed species hedgerow (hawthorn, blackthorn, field maple, dog rose) has significant wildlife value and is a hedge that neighbours tend to view more positively than a wall of leylandii. In parts of East Kent near the coast and the Sandwich Bay nature reserve, biodiversity-friendly planting is particularly worthwhile.

6. Position: set back from the line

Plant the hedge at least 30 to 60 centimetres inside your boundary, measured from the boundary line to the nearest edge of the planting holes. This setback means that when the hedge reaches its mature spread, the stems are within your property. It also means the hedge can be trimmed on your side without you needing to stand on the boundary line, and that roots grow into your ground rather than into your neighbour's.

The appropriate setback depends on species and how wide the mature hedge will be. A closely trimmed privet at 30cm offset is fine. A wide informal Rosa rugosa hedge might need 60 to 90cm setback to be comfortably within your land at maturity.

Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981: nesting birds

If you are clearing existing vegetation or an old hedge to prepare the planting site, check for active bird nests first. It is an offence to damage or destroy any wild bird's nest while it is in use or being built. Nesting season runs roughly 1 March to 31 August. Site clearance work and ground preparation is best done in the September to February window. New planting itself does not disturb nests but site preparation can.

7. Check the soil and underground services

Before digging, establish whether there are any underground services (gas, water, electricity, drainage) running along the boundary line. Your utility providers or a utility search service can advise. In older East Kent properties, buried drainage runs are not always where you might expect them, and a spade through a drain or cable is a more expensive problem than a few phone calls beforehand.

Sandy coastal soils along the Sandwich Bay and Deal seafront areas will benefit from improved planting preparation. See our guide to planting a hedge in sandy soil for the specific steps.

8. Timing: when to plant

Autumn (mid-October to late November) is the practical planting window for most hedging species in East Kent. Bare-root plants, available from autumn onwards, are cheaper and establish at least as well as pot-grown stock for most hedging species. Autumn rain handles establishment watering, roots develop over winter, and the hedge has the best chance of a strong first growing season.

If planting in spring (March to April), use pot-grown stock and be prepared to water in any dry spells through May and June. Avoid planting into frozen or waterlogged ground.

9. Maintenance access

If the hedge will need trimming on both sides when mature, think now about how you will access the neighbour's side. You have no automatic right to enter your neighbour's property to trim the hedge, even if the hedge is yours. The Access to Neighbouring Land Act 1992 gives a route to apply to court for access for essential works, but it is rarely invoked and is not a substitute for a simple prior agreement.

A brief conversation with your neighbour along the lines of "we will need to come onto your side once or twice a year to trim the hedge, is that acceptable?" costs nothing and removes a practical problem before it arises. If the hedge will not need access from the neighbour's side (for example, if it is a species that only needs trimming from your side, or if the boundary is a fence and the hedge runs along your side), this is less of a concern.

The planting itself

Once the checklist above is clear, planting a boundary hedge is straightforward. Dig a trench along the planting line, improve the soil with well-rotted compost, plant at the correct spacing for the species, firm in well, water at planting and mulch the full hedge line.
