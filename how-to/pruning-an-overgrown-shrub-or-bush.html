Pruning an overgrown shrub or bush: how it differs from hedge trimming

A single overgrown shrub asks for a different set of cuts to a hedge, and a hedge trimmer is often the wrong tool entirely. Here is how the two jobs actually differ, and how to bring a neglected bush back into shape.

Updated September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Hedge trimming vs shrub pruning: what actually differs

  Hedge trimming Specimen shrub pruning
Tool Shears or a powered hedge trimmer Secateurs and loppers, occasionally a pruning saw
What gets cut A shallow layer of this year's growth, across the whole face Whole stems, removed at the base or back to a strong bud
Goal A flat, even, formal surface The plant's natural rounded or arching shape, with light and air reaching the centre
Frequency One to three times a year Usually once a year, some shrubs only every two to three years
Common mistake n/a Shearing the outside with a hedge trimmer, which leaves a dense twiggy shell and a bare, congested middle

The mistake we see most often in Kent gardens is a single specimen shrub, a large viburnum, philadelphus or forsythia grown as a stand-alone feature, being clipped over the outside with a hedge trimmer every year because that is the tool to hand. It looks tidy for a few weeks, then regrows as a dense fuzz of thin twigs with no flowers, because a shrub trimmed this way never gets the selective, stem-by-stem cutting it actually needs. If the plant has a name, not just a species used as hedging, it almost certainly wants secateurs, not shears.

Nesting season: 1 March to 31 August

Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Dense, well-established shrubs, especially evergreens like viburnum, choisya and holly, are prime nesting sites for blackbirds, robins and wrens. Check thoroughly for an active nest before cutting anything between 1 March and 31 August. Hard renovation work is best scheduled for late winter, well clear of the nesting season.

How hard can you cut an overgrown shrub?

One-go renovation

Many tough deciduous shrubs, including philadelphus, deutzia, forsythia, weigela and buddleia, tolerate being cut back hard in a single winter: down to 30 to 60 cm from ground level, removing all the old top growth. They regrow strongly from the base the same season and can look reasonable again within a year, fully recovered within two. This is the fastest way to deal with a genuinely overgrown shrub that has outgrown its space.

Staged renovation over two to three years

Slower-growing or more sensitive shrubs, including many evergreens such as camellia, rhododendron and some viburnums, respond better to a gentler approach: remove around a third of the oldest, thickest stems at ground level each year, cutting the newest growth back by about a third at the same time. Over two or three winters the whole shrub is renewed without the shock, and the risk, of taking it all back at once. If you are unsure which category a particular shrub falls into, stage it. A shrub that would have tolerated hard cutting can always be finished off next winter; one that does not respond well to hard cutting cannot be un-cut.

Timing for common Kent garden shrubs

  • Spring-flowering shrubs (forsythia, flowering currant, early viburnums): prune immediately after flowering, not in winter, or you remove the coming year's flower buds.
  • Summer-flowering shrubs on new wood (buddleia, hardy fuchsia, some hydrangeas): hard-prune in late winter, February into early March, before growth starts.
  • Evergreens (viburnum tinus, choisya, laurel grown as a specimen rather than a hedge): late spring, once the last sharp frost has passed, given Kent's coastal winters can still deliver a cold snap into April.
  • Anything you are unsure about: renovate in late winter and watch how it responds before deciding whether to repeat next year.

Step by step: renovating an overgrown shrub

Step 1: Remove dead, damaged and crossing wood first

Before shaping anything, cut out any dead, diseased or damaged stems, and any that cross and rub against another. This alone often opens the plant up considerably and makes the remaining structure much easier to read.

Step 2: Take out the oldest stems at the base

Using loppers or a pruning saw, cut the thickest, darkest, most congested stems right down to ground level, or to a low, strong outward-facing bud if the shrub is one that does not regrow well from bare ground. This is the cut that actually renews the plant, not the shearing of the outer twigs.

Step 3: Reduce remaining growth for shape

Once the oldest wood is out, stand back and assess the shrub's natural outline. Shorten remaining stems selectively to bring the shape back under control, cutting to just above an outward-facing bud so new growth heads away from the centre rather than into it.

Step 4: Clear the centre

Thin out any remaining weak, thin or inward-growing shoots from the middle of the plant. Good light and airflow through the centre reduces fungal problems and encourages flowering wood next season, rather than a thicket of unproductive twigs.

Step 5: Feed and mulch after hard renovation

A shrub that has just lost a third or more of its growth benefits from a general-purpose feed in spring and a mulch of well-rotted compost or bark around the base, keeping it clear of the stems themselves. This supports the vigorous regrowth you are asking the plant to produce.
