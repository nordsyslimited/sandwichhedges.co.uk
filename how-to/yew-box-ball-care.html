Yew and box topiary balls: keeping container topiary sharp in Kent

Which plant is easier to keep as a container ball, how to clip each one and when, and how to keep them alive through the dry East Kent summers. An honest comparison from the people who maintain these plants on doorsteps across Sandwich and Deal.

Published September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

A pair of clipped balls in matching pots is one of the most satisfying things you can put by a front door. Formal, low-maintenance and evergreen through the greyest February. But box and yew behave quite differently in a container, and the wrong choice for the situation ends up being more work, not less.

We maintain both across East Kent, from period properties in Sandwich with mature yew specimens in lead planters to modern doorsteps in Deal with box balls in reconstituted stone pots. Here is what we actually see.

Yew vs box at a glance

Factor Yew (Taxus baccata) Box (Buxus sempervirens)
Growth rate in a pot Moderate; fills shape steadily Slow; holds shape for longer
Cuts per year needed 1 (late August) 1 (late May/June); 2nd tidy optional
Disease risk in Kent Very low Box blight and vine weevil common
Drought tolerance in pot Good Poor; needs consistent watering
Foliage texture Medium; deep green Fine; bright to mid-green
Toxicity All parts toxic All parts toxic
Frost hardiness in pot Hardy; protect pot, not plant Hardy; protect pot, not plant
Verdict Lower maintenance; better for Kent Finer finish; higher vigilance needed

The practical upshot: if you want something you can more or less leave alone between annual clips, yew is easier in Kent. If you want the finest finish and are happy to keep a close eye on it, box is worth the extra attention.

Clipping yew topiary balls

When to clip

Late August to mid-September is the main window. The plant has finished its main growth flush, nesting season has ended under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, and the weather is still warm enough for cut surfaces to harden before winter. A light tidy in late May is acceptable if the new growth has become particularly shaggy.

Nesting season (Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981)

Even container topiary near a wall or fence can shelter a nesting bird. Check for active nests before cutting from 1 March to 31 August. The safe cutting window is September to February. Always glance into the top and sides of the ball before you start.

How to clip

Use a sharp pair of one-handed topiary shears or lightweight hand shears. Start at the top and work around the ball in horizontal passes, rotating the plant if it is in a moveable pot. Keep the cuts light: remove the new season's growth only, which is the paler, softer foliage. Do not dig back into the darker old wood unless you are correcting a specific bulge.

Step back every few passes and check the ball shape from eye level. A round ball requires you to keep adjusting your angle as you move around it; it is very easy to cut one face flat if you are not stepping back regularly.

Safety note

Yew foliage and berries are toxic. Wear gloves. Keep clippings away from children and livestock. The red berries, which appear in autumn on female plants, are the most palatable part to animals, so bag all autumn clippings rather than leaving them on the ground.

Clipping box topiary balls

When to clip

Late May to early June is the traditional time for a box ball. The plant has pushed its main flush of bright new growth, and cutting then encourages a second, smaller flush that fills the surface back in before summer ends. A second light tidy in August is optional. Never clip box in wet weather; damp conditions spread box blight fungal spores on the shears from one plant to the next.

How to clip

The technique is the same as yew: small, light passes from the top downward, stepping back to check the shape. Box responds very well to being rotated in its pot every few passes. The finer foliage means a neat finish is easier to achieve but also that any imprecision is more visible.

Disease hygiene

Wipe or dip your shears in a dilute disinfectant solution between plants if you are clipping more than one box specimen. Box blight travels on contaminated blades. At the end of the session, clean the blades thoroughly before storing.

Feeding container topiary

Nutrients wash out of pots quickly, especially in Kent's chalky soils where containers leach fast. Feed container topiary twice a year:

  • March to April: a balanced slow-release granular fertiliser to support the spring growth flush.
  • September: a low-nitrogen fertiliser (higher potassium) to harden the plant before winter without encouraging soft new growth.

Do not over-feed. A single application of a granular fertiliser at the manufacturer's rate is enough; more does not produce a denser ball and risks scorching roots in the confined space of a pot.

Watering

Container topiary is far more vulnerable to drought stress than the same plant in the ground. In a Kent summer with no rain for a week, check the pot weight every two days. Water deeply when needed, until water runs freely from the drainage holes, rather than adding a little each day.

Both yew and box prefer a well-drained compost. Sitting in waterlogged compost through a wet winter is as harmful as drought in summer. Check that drainage holes are not blocked, and raise pots on feet if they sit directly on a paved surface.

Repotting

Container topiary needs repotting every three to five years or when roots begin circling visibly around the base of the rootball when you ease it out. Move up one pot size at a time. Use a gritty compost mix (equal parts John Innes No. 3 and horticultural grit works well). Repot in March before the growing season starts.

Overwintering

Both yew and box are cold-hardy in East Kent and do not need frost protection for the plant itself. The pot is the vulnerable part. Terracotta and ceramic pots can crack if saturated compost freezes solid. Wrap pots with bubble wrap or hessian during prolonged frost periods, raise them on pot feet and reduce watering to a minimum from November to February.
