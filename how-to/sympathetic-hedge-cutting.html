Sympathetic hedge cutting: hard annual cuts vs rotational management

Most UK garden and field hedges are cut hard to the same frame every year. It keeps them neat. It is also, in terms of wildlife value and long-term hedge health, close to the worst way to do it. This is what the alternative looks like.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Safety note before you start

Hedge cutting with power tools carries risks. Always wear eye protection, ear protection, heavy gloves and non-slip footwear. Keep third parties and children clear of the cutting area. Ensure blades are sharp: a blunt cutter drags and twists, increasing kickback risk. This guide describes cutting technique, not tool operation; see our guide to choosing a hedge trimmer for equipment advice.

Nesting season: 1 March to 31 August

Under the UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting typically runs 1 March to 31 August. Check any section of hedge for active nests before cutting. See our guide to checking for birds' nests.

Two ways of cutting the same hedge

There is nothing wrong with cutting a hedge to keep it a defined size and shape. The question is how much growth you take off each time, how often you cut, and whether you always cut both sides in the same season.

Hard annual cutting: both sides, back to the frame, every August. The hedge is always the same size. It always looks the same. The outer surface is always new wood, never old enough to have berries, rarely dense enough for nesting.

Sympathetic cutting: same target size, but with one or more of these changes in approach:

  • Leave 5-10 cm of new growth on the outer face rather than cutting back to bare wood.
  • Cut every two or three years rather than every year.
  • Alternate sides: cut the road-facing side one year, the garden-facing side the next.
  • Shift timing slightly later: September rather than August, allowing late berries to set.

The hedge does not look significantly different. The wildlife value is substantially higher.

Comparison: hard annual cut vs sympathetic rotational management

Factor Hard annual cut Sympathetic rotational

Growth retained Cut to same frame 5-10 cm of new growth left on outer face

What the research says

The UK Centre for Ecology and Hydrology (UKCEH) and the National Association of Agricultural Contractors (NAAC) published a joint film and guidance note in 2024/25 featuring Chris Gardner, a hedge contractor and vice-chair of the NAAC, on practical technique changes that benefit both wildlife and hedge longevity. The key findings from that work align with what field ecologists have been saying for some time: the timing of cuts, the height to which you cut, and whether you alternate sides all have measurable effects on the wildlife value a hedge provides in the 12 months following each cut.

Specifically:

  • Hedges cut every two to three years carry significantly more berry-bearing wood than those cut annually, because hawthorn, blackthorn and field maple need at least two seasons of growth before they produce a reasonable berry crop.
  • Alternate-side cutting means one side of the hedge is always at least one season's growth beyond the frame: it provides cover and food in the off year.
  • Cutting in mid-September rather than late August (where the hedge size permits) allows haws and sloes to colour up before they are removed, providing a brief but significant late-season food source for migrating and resident birds.
  • Not cutting back to the bare frame, but leaving a few centimetres of the current year's growth, builds a progressively denser outer shell over several seasons.

The NAAC contractor perspective

What makes Chris Gardner's contribution in the UKCEH film useful is that it comes from a working contractor rather than a conservation charity. The constraints are real: clients expect a tidy boundary, the work has to be done safely and efficiently, and changes in technique have to be practical from the top of a tractor cab or holding a petrol trimmer.

His conclusions are consistent with sympathetic management being compatible with professional hedge maintenance. The changes are not dramatic: slightly later timing, slightly less aggressive cutting height, alternating sides. None of these require special equipment or significantly more time. The difference in outcome, over three to five years, is a noticeably denser hedge with visible berry and seed production.

Applying this in East Kent gardens

Most garden hedges in Sandwich, Deal and the surrounding villages are managed to a tight profile: laurel in the older properties along the Richborough Road, privet in the post-war streets, leylandii screening plot boundaries in Worth and Ash, hawthorn and field maple on rural boundaries toward Eastry and Woodnesborough.

The sympathetic approach applies differently across these species:

Hawthorn and mixed native hedges: these respond best to rotational management. Even a single missed cut year produces a significant increase in berry production. Alternate-side cutting is the most practical change to introduce. Hawthorn can be cut very hard (renovation cutting) and will regenerate from old wood; see our guide to overgrown hawthorn.

Privet: privet grows fast and, in a small garden, annual cutting is often necessary to keep it within bounds. The sympathetic adjustment here is the height: leaving 5 cm of growth rather than cutting to the old wood, and timing cuts to allow the white flowers to appear before the midsummer cut if possible.

Laurel: laurel's large leaves mean it responds to cuts differently from small-leaved species. It is also slower-growing, so many laurel hedges need cutting only every 12-18 months anyway. The key sympathetic adjustment for laurel is to use secateurs or a saw for thick stems rather than a flail cutter: clean cuts on laurel heal better and produce less brown, ragged regrowth.

Leylandii: leylandii will not regenerate from brown wood, which makes the "leave some growth" principle essential rather than optional. Always cut leylandii leaving green growth on the outer face. Rotational cutting is possible but must not let the hedge outgrow its position, since hard renovation cuts are not recoverable on mature leylandii.

When sympathetic cutting is not appropriate

There are situations where hard annual cutting remains the right approach:

  • A formal box or yew hedge where tight geometry is the point. Both species grow slowly enough that skipping a year changes the shape noticeably.
  • A boundary hedge over a right-of-way or pavement where overhang creates a safety or legal obligation to cut back to a specific line every year.
  • A hedge that is already showing gapping at the base due to years of shading from the top: in this case a sympathetic approach needs to be combined with a programme of base restoration first.

For most garden and rural boundary hedges in East Kent, however, the sympathetic approach is applicable with minimal practical compromise.
