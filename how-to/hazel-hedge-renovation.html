Renovating an overgrown hazel hedge in Kent

Hazel is one of the most forgiving native hedgerow plants for a renovation cut. Here is the checklist we work through before, during and after bringing an overgrown hazel hedge back.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Hazel (Corylus avellana) has been managed as a coppiced hedgerow plant in Kent for centuries. The practice of cutting it back hard, sometimes to near ground level, and allowing it to regrow is called coppicing, and hazel is so well adapted to this treatment that it barely registers the shock. Left unmanaged for a decade or more, a hazel hedge typically sends up multiple tall, arching stems with thin, whippy growth at the top and bare, open growth at the base. That is the standard picture we see in older East Kent gardens, particularly around Ash, Woodnesborough and Worth, where native hedgerows are common.

Renovation is very achievable, and because hazel regrows so quickly, the results are visible within a single growing season.

Nesting season: the most important check

Under the UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, damaging an active bird's nest is an offence. Nesting season runs from roughly 1 March to 31 August. Hazel is a valuable nesting plant for dormice (a protected species in Kent), as well as blackbirds, robins and long-tailed tits. The safe renovation window is September to February, with January to early March the most popular. If you need to cut between March and August, check carefully and thoroughly for occupied nests and dormouse presence before starting.

Before you start: kit and safety

  • Eye protection: mandatory; hazel stems and clippings are springy and unpredictable
  • Gloves: thick leather or similar; hazel stems can be sharp at freshly cut ends
  • Ear defenders if using a petrol chainsaw or reciprocating saw for thick stems
  • Ladder safety: overgrown hazel can be tall; a pole saw is safer than a ladder for high stems; have someone present at all times when using ladders
  • RCD adaptor for corded electric tools
  • Check for wasp nests before starting, particularly in August and September

Renovation checklist

Work through these steps in order. Skipping ahead, particularly on the nesting check and the assessment of standing growth, is the most common cause of problems.

Step 1: Confirm the cutting window

Is it September to February? If not, stop. Come back in late autumn or January. The only exception is a small tidy job outside this window where you can confirm no active nests are present.

Step 2: Check for protected species

Dormice are protected under the Conservation of Habitats and Species Regulations 2017. They are present in parts of East Kent, particularly near ancient woodland edges and old hedgerows. If your hazel hedge is old, connects to woodland or is on a rural boundary, consider whether a dormice check is warranted before major work. English Nature (Natural England) can advise. For garden hazel hedges in town or village settings, the risk is lower but still worth considering.

Step 3: Decide: renovation cut or full coppice?

A renovation cut reduces the height and width significantly but leaves the main framework stems at a usable height, typically 1.0 to 1.8 m for a garden hedge. This is appropriate when the hedge has a good base and the owner wants a formal or semi-formal boundary.

A coppice cut takes all stems back to 15 to 30 cm above ground, leaving only the stool. This is the traditional method and produces the most vigorous regrowth, but leaves the hedge looking bare for one full season. It is the right choice for a hedgerow or rural boundary where the aim is long-term wildlife habitat, or for a garden hedge where the owner has decided to start again from scratch.

Step 4: Gather the right tools

For stems up to 4 cm diameter: loppers. For stems 4 to 15 cm: a folding pruning saw or reciprocating saw. For stems over 15 cm at the base: a bow saw or chainsaw (chainsaw requires appropriate training and PPE). Bring a good supply of bags or a skip for the arisings: hazel produces a lot of material and it is a nuisance to leave on site.

Step 5: Work systematically, one section at a time

Start at one end of the hedge and work along it, clearing the full depth and height of each section before moving on. This is better than trying to do the whole top, then the whole face: it keeps the working area clear and makes it easier to judge the final line. Clear brambles and ivy from the base before cutting the main stems.

Step 6: Make clean cuts at the right height

For a renovation cut: take each stem back to the desired height, cutting just above an outward-facing bud where possible. For a coppice cut: take all stems back to 15 to 30 cm, cutting at a slight angle to shed rain. All cuts should be clean, not ragged: a sharp saw or loppers makes a noticeable difference to healing speed.

Step 7: Clear and remove all arisings

Hazel clippings and cut stems can be used as pea sticks or woven into dead hedge material (see our guide to making a dead hedge). Finer material can go on a compost heap. Do not leave large volumes of cut material against the base of the hedge as this can harbour slugs and harbour fungal problems.

Step 8: Post-cut care

Apply a mulch of composted bark 7 to 10 cm deep along the base of the hedge, kept clear of the cut stumps. Water if renovation takes place in a dry spell. A balanced fertiliser applied in spring, watered in, helps speed recovery on thinner soils. No other special care is needed.

What to expect during recovery

Hazel regrows fast. Within 4 to 8 weeks of the first warm days in spring, new shoots will push vigorously from dormant buds at the base and from the cut stumps. By mid-summer after a winter renovation, the hedge will be visibly leafy and well covered. By year two it will typically have enough growth to begin trimming normally. This is significantly faster than holly or yew renovation, and comparable with hawthorn.

Catkins (the yellow male lamb's tails that appear in January and February) grow on second-year wood. They will not appear in the first winter after a full renovation. By year two, catkins should begin to return. Nuts take three or four years to appear on new growth, as they form on three-year-old wood. If nut production matters to you, consider a staged renovation over two years, leaving part of the hedge to continue producing while the first section regrows.
