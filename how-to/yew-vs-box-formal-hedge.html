Yew vs box: which formal hedge for your Kent garden?

Both give you the same dark, fine-textured formality. The difference is what happens in year five, ten and twenty. Here is an honest comparison for East Kent conditions.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Walk around the older gardens of Sandwich, Deal and the surrounding villages and you will find both. Yew (Taxus baccata) forming the dark backbone of walled kitchen gardens and estate boundaries. Box (Buxus sempervirens) edging parterres and clipped into balls on either side of paths. For centuries, the choice came down to scale, budget and patience. That calculation has shifted in the last decade.

Box blight (Cylindrocladium buxicola) arrived in the UK in the mid-1990s. Box tree moth (Cydalima perspectalis) followed in the 2010s and had established in every English county by the mid-2020s. We now see badly damaged or dead box hedges in gardens across East Kent almost every summer. It has changed the terms of this comparison significantly.

The case for yew

Yew is slower to establish than box, but once it is growing it is essentially immune to the diseases now threatening box. It regenerates from bare wood (unusual among conifers), so you can reduce an overgrown yew hedge hard and it will come back. It tolerates shade, it tolerates the chalk soils underlying most of the Deal, Sandwich and Ash area, and it is long-lived enough to outlast the gardener who planted it by decades.

A single cut per year, in late summer, is enough to keep a formal yew hedge sharp. The growth rate works in your favour here: yew does not bolt the way leylandii does, so a tidy August cut holds its shape through winter and into the following June.

The main objections to yew are cost and time. Young plants are more expensive than box, and they take longer to reach a useful height. At 15 to 20 cm per year in reasonable conditions, a yew planted at 60 cm will reach 1.2 metres in roughly three to four years. Box is faster at the same stage: closer to 20 to 30 cm per year in a good growing season.

Yew is also toxic: berries, leaves and bark contain taxine alkaloids. This is a genuine consideration if young children or animals will be in the garden. The berries ripen in autumn to a bright red and are attractive to children. Box is not edible either, but it is not in the same risk category as yew.

The case for box

Box remains the right choice for topiary and low edging where its fine texture and dense, controllable growth is exactly what you need. At the scale of a knot garden, a low parterre edge or a clipped ball in a container, nothing matches box for precision. Yew is excellent for a larger formal hedge but harder to manage at a small scale with hand shears.

Box is faster to establish and cheaper to buy, which matters if you need a lot of plants. And despite the disease pressure, box grown in good conditions with good airflow and clean tools is not automatically going to be lost to blight. The risk is real, but it is manageable with discipline.

The key is to avoid planting a hedge of solid box at close centres in a humid, sheltered spot and then giving it no attention until problems appear. Box blight thrives in still, damp conditions. If you trim in dry weather, sterilise blades between plants and avoid overhead watering, the risk reduces significantly.

Side by side

Yew (Taxus baccata) Box (Buxus sempervirens)
Growth rate 15–20 cm/year once established 20–30 cm/year (young plants)
Cuts per year 1 (August–September) 1–2 (May–June, then August if needed)
Disease risk None from box blight or moth Moderate to high (blight + moth in Kent)
Hard renovation Yes, recovers from bare wood Yes, but blight risk increases post-cut
Wildlife value Good (birds eat berries, nesting cover) Limited (dense but low food value)
Toxicity Highly toxic (all parts except the flesh of the berry) Toxic if eaten in quantity
Cost (60 cm plants) Higher (£3–6 per plant) Lower (£1.50–3 per plant)
Best use Formal boundary hedge, tall screening, topiary Low edging, parterre, small topiary, containers

What we see in East Kent

In the older walled gardens around Sandwich and in the more sheltered cottage gardens in Worth and Eastry, we still see intact, healthy box hedges in good condition. These tend to be established plants that have been managed carefully for decades and are not under pressure from crowded replanting.

What we also see, with increasing frequency, is box stripped back to bare stems by blight following a wet summer, or heavily damaged by box tree moth caterpillars. Many of these gardens are replacing their box with yew, Ilex crenata (Japanese holly) or osmanthus, all of which give a similar formal look without the disease risk.

If you are starting from scratch and want a formal hedge that will look good in twenty years without annual anxiety about disease, yew is the stronger choice for East Kent. If you want a low parterre edge, a knot garden or a small piece of topiary work, box remains the right tool for the job.

Nesting season reminder

Both yew and box provide nesting habitat. Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs approximately 1 March to 31 August. The safe cutting window for formal hedges is September to February. A quick check before any summer trim is good practice and legally important.
