Cutting tall hedges safely: which access method for your height

The hedge is 3m of solid leylandii and you can't reach the top. Before you drag out the ladder, here's the safer sequence: long-reach trimmer first, then platform steps, then scaffold. And when it's worth making a call.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Working at height causes more garden injuries than almost any other activity. Most of them involve ladders. A hedge is not a wall: a standard leaning ladder against a hedge has nothing solid to lean against, the person on it is holding a moving blade, and the natural instinct is to reach sideways to get the last bit. That combination is how accidents happen.

The good news is that for most tall hedges in East Kent, there is a better option than a ladder, and often a better option than working at height at all. This guide works through the decision in order: start from the ground up, and only go higher when you have ruled out the simpler option.

Before you start: safety at every height

  • Eye protection: essential at any height. Flying debris falls onto your face when cutting above head height.
  • Ear protection: for petrol or electric trimmers.
  • Gloves: sturdy gardening or work gloves throughout.
  • RCD: if using an electric trimmer, fit a residual-current device in the circuit before starting.
  • Ladder rule: never use a ladder as a working platform for hedge cutting. If a ladder must be used to reach a section, have someone footing it at the base, never overreach sideways, and use a pole trimmer from that position rather than holding a short-blade tool at arm's length.
  • Soft ground: platform steps and scaffold towers on soft or uneven ground are unsafe. Firm up or board out the ground surface first.
  • Wasps: inspect the hedge from a distance before starting. Disturbing a wasp nest at height is a lot more serious than at ground level.

Nesting season applies at every height

The UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs roughly from 1 March to 31 August. Tall hedges, particularly leylandii and laurel, are popular nesting sites. The safe cutting window for most hedges is September to February. If a check reveals an active nest, leave that section until the birds have fledged.

Which access method: work through this in order

The right question is not "how do I get up there?" but "do I need to get up there at all?" Work through the options below from the top. Stop when you find the first one that covers your situation.

Decision sequence

Question 1: Can you comfortably reach the top of the hedge with arms raised while standing on the ground?
Yes: standard ground-level trimming. No special access needed. See our hedge trimming safety checklist.
No: continue to Question 2.

Question 2: Is the hedge top within about 2.5-3m from the ground (roughly within reach of a long-reach pole trimmer)?
Yes: a long-reach trimmer is likely your best option. See below.
No: continue to Question 3.

Question 3: Is the hedge top under about 3.5m from the ground, and is the run under 15m?
Yes: consider a hop-up platform or platform steps. See below.
No: continue to Question 4.

Question 4: Is the hedge under 20m of run?
Yes: a mobile scaffold tower may be cost-effective to hire. See below.
No, or the hedge is over 4m tall: at this scale, a professional with the right access equipment is worth a call. The cost is usually less than you'd expect.

Option 1: long-reach (telescopic pole) trimmer

This is the safest option for hedges up to about 3-3.5m because you stay on the ground the entire time. A long-reach or pole hedge trimmer has a head on a telescopic shaft, typically extending to give a reach of 2.5-3m above your hands. The head is usually angled.

What it does well:

  • Top of the hedge from the ground, without a ladder in sight.
  • Reaching over into the hedge top from one side where the access is awkward.
  • Sections where the ground beside the hedge is soft, uneven or sloped.

What it doesn't do well: cutting the face of a very tall hedge cleanly, or working a hedge where you need to cut both face and top at awkward angles simultaneously. For the face, you are still better approaching at ground level with a standard trimmer, then finishing the top with the pole.

See our full guide to using a long-reach trimmer safely for setup, blade angle and technique.

Option 2: platform steps or hop-up platform

When the long-reach trimmer genuinely can't get to the top (the hedge is too wide to reach across, or the top is over 3.5m), a hop-up platform or platform steps are safer than a ladder for hedge work. The key difference: you have a flat standing surface and both hands are free to hold the trimmer properly.

Requirements for safe use:

  • Firm, level ground. If the ground is soft, lay boards under the feet first.
  • No overreaching: position the platform, cut what you can reach safely, then reposition. Reaching sideways from a platform is how falls happen.
  • Someone present: not mandatory, but strongly advised. A person who can steady the platform and raise the alarm if something goes wrong makes a real difference.
  • Weight limit: check the platform's rated load and don't forget the weight of the tool and yourself.

Domestic hop-ups (single step, about 50cm high) are useful for hedges just out of reach. For hedges in the 3-4m range, a two-step or three-step platform gives more working height with a broader standing surface than a standard stepladder, which narrows at the top.

Option 3: mobile scaffold tower

A mobile scaffold tower (aluminium tower on castors) is worth considering for tall hedges with a long run, typically over 10-15m, where moving a platform every few steps becomes impractical. Hire cost is roughly £50-80 per day from tool hire companies serving East Kent, including Sandwich and Deal.

Practical notes:

  • The tower needs firm, level ground at every position. On soft lawn or uneven surfaces, boards under each castor foot are essential.
  • Lock the castors before climbing. Every time, before every climb.
  • Use the outriggers if the tower supplies them. They are there for a reason.
  • Don't move the tower with anyone on it.
  • Allow time for setup and repositioning: for a 20m hedge at 3.5m height, budget most of a day.

When to call a professional

There is no shame in deciding that a hedge is beyond what can be done safely from the ground or with domestic access equipment. The cases where a call makes sense:

  • The hedge is over 4m tall, particularly a mature leylandii or laurel boundary.
  • The ground on one or both sides is soft, steeply sloped or otherwise unsafe for a tower.
  • The hedge runs along a boundary where working near a fence, wall or neighbour's property adds complexity.
  • The hedgerow is a tall mixed native boundary that needs careful species-by-species management alongside the height reduction.

Professionals carry the right equipment (including access platforms designed for hedge work rather than adapted for it), have the experience to judge when a hedge can be renovated and when it can't, and are fully insured for work at height. For most boundary leylandii in Sandwich, Worth, Ash and Eastry, the cost of a quote is nothing and the job is usually done in a day.
