Beech hedge: first three years

Bare-root beech planted in January and container-grown beech planted in April follow different paths in the first three years. Here is what to do and expect from each, in an East Kent garden.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Beech (Fagus sylvatica) is not an especially difficult hedge to establish, but it is unforgiving of a few specific mistakes. Skip the formative cut, plant a container-grown hedge in July without watering it in, or expect it to look like a hedge by the end of year one, and you will be disappointed. Get those things right and beech rewards you with one of the best garden hedges going: good structure, that well-known winter leaf colour, and an easy annual cut from year three onwards.

The question of whether to plant bare-root or container-grown plants affects what you do in years one to three more than people realise. The two routes differ in timing, the planting cut, watering needs and how quickly the hedge starts to look like a hedge. This guide compares them side by side.

Nesting season: 1 March to 31 August

Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 it is an offence to damage or destroy a nest that is in active use. Check the hedge carefully before any trim between March and August. Beech planted in winter will have its first growing season in spring and early summer, which is also peak nesting season. The safe window for young beech is late August through to February.

Bare-root beech: year by year

Year 1: plant and cut hard

Bare-root beech whips are available from nurseries between late October and mid-March, when the plant is fully dormant. This is the moment to plant them. For a formal garden hedge at 1.5 metres, three plants per metre (one every 33 cm) in a single row is standard. If you want density faster, go two rows staggered at 50 cm spacing.

At planting, do the formative cut: take the main leader down by one-third, and cut all the side shoots back to 10 cm from the main stem. This looks severe, but it stimulates the multiple shoots from low down that will form the dense base of the hedge. Bare-root whips planted without the formative cut often grow straight up with a bare base that takes years to fill in. After cutting, firm the soil, water in and mulch.

Through the first summer, the main job is watering during dry spells. The chalk downland between Sandwich and Deal dries out quickly. In late August, once you are confident the hedge contains no active nests, you can take a first light pass over the sides with hedge shears. Leave the top alone. The goal is to trigger a second flush of lateral growth before winter.

Year 2: building density

The second year is when a bare-root beech hedge really starts to look like something. Clip the sides two or three times: late June (only if the hedge is clearly empty of nests), late August and late September. Each clip should take off roughly half the new side growth. Hold the top at 10 to 15 cm below the final target height: resist the urge to let it shoot upward, since energy that goes into the leaders comes at the expense of the lower face.

By the end of year two, a well-managed bare-root beech hedge in the right conditions (not on pure chalk within 20 cm of the surface, watered in its first summer) should have a solid framework with no major gaps at the base. The copper-brown winter leaves will be holding, which gives a sense of privacy even when the hedge is not yet fully dense.

Year 3: establishing the final profile

Let the top reach its target height this year. Beech cuts well with a slight trapezoid profile, wider at the base. Two cuts: late June if clear of nests, then the main cut in late August or early September. By the end of year three, the hedge should be on the standard annual maintenance cycle.

Container-grown beech: year by year

Year 1: establish the roots

Container-grown beech planted in spring or autumn needs one full season of root establishment before you start clipping. The plant has been growing in a pot, and the roots may be circling or pot-bound. Tease them out gently before planting, water well and mulch. Do not apply the hard formative cut you would use on bare-root whips. Instead, trim only any broken or very untidy shoots with secateurs. Then leave the hedge completely alone through the first growing season.

Watering is more critical here than with bare-root. A container-grown beech planted in spring will face its first summer with limited root spread in what may be dry soil. Water once a week during dry spells through June, July and August. If the hedge shows yellowing or leaf scorch, water more. The coastal strip around Deal and Sandwich Bay is reliably drier than inland Kent in summer.

Year 2: first clip and density work

By late August of year two, the container-grown beech will have had one full growing season in the ground and should be visibly thickened. This is when you start clipping: a light pass over the sides in late August, taking off no more than 5 to 8 cm. Leave the top alone. If you have the time, a second light side clip in late September will push another flush of lateral branching before the hedge goes dormant.

Year 3: catch up with the bare-root hedge

Year three for a container-grown beech corresponds roughly to year two for bare-root: clip the sides two or three times (June, August, September), hold the top slightly below the target height, and start establishing the batter angle. By the end of year three the two hedges will be close in density, with the bare-root still slightly ahead. Both will be on the annual maintenance cycle by year four.

What goes wrong

The most common failure across both types is a bare base, and it usually comes from one of two causes. The first is skipping or softening the formative cut on bare-root whips: a hedge that is not cut at planting develops a few strong vertical leaders with minimal side growth, and the lower third never fills in. The second is letting the top grow ahead of the sides in year two: once the hedge has shot up, it is very difficult to thicken the base without hard renovation.

The second most common failure is poor establishment on chalk. Beech tolerates chalk downland, but it does not thrive in thin soil over solid chalk. If you are planting on the lighter downland north of Sandwich or Woodnesborough, dig in organic matter at planting, mulch generously, and water through the first two summers.
