Hedge trimmer noise: what the law actually says

Your neighbour has knocked on the door, or you have received a noise complaint, or you simply want to avoid one. Here is a plain-English guide to the law on garden machinery noise in England and what councils can realistically do about it.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Hedge trimming generates noise. Petrol trimmers in particular can produce 95 decibels or more at the operator's ear, audible well beyond the garden boundary. Most of the time this is not a legal problem. But when does it become one, what framework applies, and what can a local council actually do?

The answer matters both ways: whether you are the person cutting, or the neighbour who cannot sleep because someone is revving a petrol hedger at 7:30am on a Sunday morning.

The key question: is there a fixed legal time limit?

No. There is no single piece of national legislation in England that sets a fixed start or stop time for using a hedge trimmer on private residential property. No law says "you cannot use a hedge trimmer before 8am" in the same way that planning law might restrict certain activities on a development site. What exists instead is a framework for assessing whether noise rises to the level of a statutory nuisance.

Councils often publish their own guidance suggesting acceptable hours for power tools on residential property (commonly 8am to 8pm Monday to Saturday, and 10am to 5pm on Sundays), but these are practical recommendations, not criminal prohibitions. Breaching the guidance is not, by itself, an offence.

The decision tree: is the noise a statutory nuisance?

Work through these questions to understand how a council would assess a noise complaint about hedge cutting:

Is the noise "unreasonable in the circumstances"?

Start here. Two hours of hedge cutting on a Saturday afternoon in a residential area is unlikely to be found unreasonable. Forty-five minutes of a petrol trimmer at 6:45am on a Sunday, repeated weekly, very likely would be. Councils weigh intensity, timing, frequency and duration together.

Does the noise substantially interfere with the use and enjoyment of the complainant's property? This is the legal test for statutory nuisance under Section 79 of the Environmental Protection Act 1990. "Substantial" means material, not trivial. Courts have consistently held that temporary noise from normal residential activity does not meet this threshold even if the neighbour finds it irritating. The noise must genuinely prevent or substantially impair the ordinary use of the property, not merely be unpleasant.

Is the complainant unusually sensitive? If a neighbour has a medical condition that makes them more sensitive to noise, a council must consider this, but the standard remains objective: would the noise substantially interfere with an ordinary person in the same circumstances?

How close are the properties? In Sandwich's historic town centre, terraced properties, and in the tightly spaced gardens common across Deal and Woodnesborough, gardens are often very close together. A petrol trimmer running two metres from a neighbour's bedroom window has a materially different impact from the same machine in a large rural plot. This matters to the assessment.

What happens if someone complains to the council?

A council's environmental health team will investigate the complaint. The usual progression:

  1. Initial enquiry: the council contacts the person who has been complained about, explains the concern and asks them to consider the impact. Most cases stop here.
  2. Monitoring: if the noise continues after initial contact, the council may ask the complainant to keep a noise diary or install recording equipment.
  3. Abatement Notice: if the council decides a statutory nuisance exists or is likely to recur, they can issue an Abatement Notice requiring the person to stop or limit the noise. The notice must specify what is required and when.
  4. Enforcement: failing to comply with an Abatement Notice without reasonable excuse is a criminal offence under the Environmental Protection Act 1990, carrying a fine. For domestic premises, the fine can be up to £5,000 on summary conviction.

In practice, formal enforcement action over ordinary garden machinery is rare. Most disputes are resolved at the initial enquiry stage, or by the neighbours reaching an informal understanding about timing.

What is and is not covered

Scenario Likely outcome
Two hours of hedge cutting on a Saturday afternoon Unlikely to constitute statutory nuisance; complaint very unlikely to proceed
Petrol trimmer at 7am on a weekday Borderline: council guidance usually suggests 8am; repeated very early starts could attract investigation
Petrol trimmer at 7am on a Sunday morning Likely to be found unreasonable; could result in an Abatement Notice if repeated
Battery trimmer at 9am on a Sunday Unlikely to attract a successful complaint; noise level substantially lower than petrol
Professional contractor working at 7:30am for a client Contractor may have obligations under the Code of Practice on Noise; client should agree hours with neighbours

Practical guidance: staying out of trouble

For most householders in East Kent, the risk of a formal noise complaint over hedge cutting is low if you follow reasonable practice:

  • Start no earlier than 8am on weekdays and Saturdays, 10am on Sundays. This matches most councils' informal guidance and will deflect the large majority of complaints.
  • Keep it brief. A single hedge cutting session of one to three hours, once or twice a year, is categorically different from running powered equipment continuously over multiple weekends.
  • Consider the trimmer type. Battery and corded electric trimmers produce roughly 10-15 dB less noise than petrol equivalents. In densely built-up areas, such as the streets around Sandwich's town centre or terraced housing in Deal, switching from petrol to battery for routine maintenance is worth considering regardless of the legal question.
  • Tell your neighbour. A one-sentence warning the day before ("I'm planning to cut the hedge tomorrow morning, should be about two hours") prevents most complaints before they start.
  • For contractors: if you are hiring a professional to cut your hedge, ask them to agree the working hours with you and brief your neighbours. The same standard applies to professional work, and your neighbours are more likely to accept short-term noise from an agreed professional visit than from repeated early-morning DIY sessions.

Do not forget the nesting season rule

The noise question is entirely separate from the nesting season law, but they often come up together. Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs roughly 1 March to 31 August. The fact that your trimmer noise is legally acceptable does not help if you damage a live nest. Always check for nests before cutting between March and August. The safe cutting window for most hedges is September to February.

What about hedge height disputes?

The noise question is different from the hedge height question. High hedges legislation (Anti-social Behaviour Act 2003, Part 8) deals with evergreen or semi-evergreen hedges over 2m that obstruct light or access to a neighbouring property. See our hedge heights and neighbour law guide for that topic.

I am the neighbour. What can I do?

If the noise is genuinely unreasonable, this is the recommended sequence:

  1. Speak to the person first. Most people do not realise how far their hedge trimmer carries. A polite conversation resolves the majority of disputes.
  2. Keep a diary. If the problem continues, note the date, time, duration and your description of the impact. This is evidence a council will ask for.
  3. Contact your local council's environmental health team. For East Kent properties in Sandwich, Deal, Worth, Ash and surrounding villages, the relevant authority is Dover District Council (environmental health).
  4. Consider mediation. Councils and organisations such as the Community Mediation Service Kent offer free or low-cost mediation as an alternative to formal enforcement, and it tends to produce better long-term outcomes between neighbours who will continue living next to each other.
In most cases, a quick conversation about timing is all it takes. The law is a backstop for genuine disputes, not an everyday tool for settling normal garden noise.
