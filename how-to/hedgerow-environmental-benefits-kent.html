Environmental benefits of hedgerows in Kent: carbon, wildlife and water

The hedge at the bottom of your garden is quietly doing a great deal of work. A guide to what hedgerows contribute environmentally in East Kent, and how management choices change the picture.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

England has lost around half its hedgerow length since the Second World War, most of it removed to enlarge agricultural fields. What remains, something in the region of 400,000 kilometres of field hedgerows plus an unmeasured but very large network of garden hedges, is now recognised as one of the most important habitats in the lowland British countryside. East Kent sits at the edge of that network: the downland north of Woodnesborough, the fields around Ash and Eastry, and the coastal grazing marshes around Sandwich Bay all depend on hedgerow corridors to hold their wildlife together.

This guide looks at the four main environmental functions a hedgerow performs, and how management approach affects each one. It is structured as a decision guide: pick the benefit you most want to maximise, and you will find the management approach that best delivers it.

Nesting season is the foundation of everything

Nesting season runs from 1 March to 31 August. It is a legal requirement under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 not to damage an active nest. It is also the period when most of a hedgerow's wildlife value is concentrated: birds nesting, bumblebees foraging, insects hatching. The proper cutting window is September to February. Everything in this guide rests on that.

What do you want to achieve?

The four main environmental functions hedgerows provide are carbon storage, biodiversity and wildlife corridors, water management, and erosion and wind control. They are not mutually exclusive, and a well-managed hedgerow of native mixed species delivers all four. But if you are making management decisions, it helps to know which approach tilts the balance in which direction.

If your priority is carbon storage

Hedgerows store carbon in their woody biomass and in the soil beneath them. The older and larger a hedge, the more carbon it has accumulated. Research by the UK Centre for Ecology and Hydrology (UKCEH) estimates that England's hedgerows collectively store around 9 to 10 million tonnes of carbon in above-ground biomass, with similar amounts in the soil beneath.

To maximise carbon storage:

  • Maintain height and width. A taller, wider hedge holds more woody biomass than a close-clipped low boundary.
  • Allow standard trees within the hedgerow. A mature oak or ash growing out of a field hedge holds vastly more carbon than the hedge itself, and even a garden boundary can accommodate a standard tree if the space allows.
  • Avoid cutting every year on the same line. Each annual clip removes some of the outer biomass. Cutting every two or three years on a rotational basis allows more woody growth to accumulate before the next trim.
  • Leave the base unmanaged or lightly managed. Grass, bramble and ivy along the hedge base contribute significant organic matter to the soil as they decompose.

On the chalk downland and free-draining sandy soils around Sandwich, Worth and Ash, hedgerow soils tend to have lower organic matter than heavier clay soils further north. A mature hedge boundary that has been in place for decades may have built up substantially richer soil than the adjacent field, and this soil carbon is part of the total hedgerow carbon store.

If your priority is biodiversity and wildlife corridors

Hedgerows function as linear habitats and as corridors connecting isolated patches of woodland, wetland and grassland. In a fragmented agricultural landscape like East Kent's, where field boundaries may be the only semi-natural vegetation between one woodland and another, the connectivity of hedgerows matters enormously for mammals, amphibians and invertebrates that cannot cross open ground easily.

To maximise biodiversity value:

  • Plant or retain mixed native species rather than a single species. A hawthorn and blackthorn hedge with elder, field rose and hazel provides food and cover through a much longer season than a privet or leylandii boundary.
  • Allow flowering and fruiting. Clipping before flowers open removes the food source. For native species hedges, allowing one section to flower and fruit by cutting it every two or three years rather than annually makes a significant difference. In a formal garden where the whole hedge needs to stay neat, even allowing a small section at one end to run is worthwhile.
  • Maintain a dense base. Ground-nesting birds such as yellowhammer and whitethroat, and small mammals including hedgehogs and common shrews, use the base of hedges for nesting and foraging. A hedge with gappy bases is far less valuable than one that is dense to the ground.
  • Connect to other habitats at both ends. A hedge that runs from a garden to a field boundary to a wood edge, however indirect the route, is worth incomparably more than an isolated island of vegetation.

The coastal position of East Kent adds an extra dimension. Hedgerows along the inland edge of Sandwich Bay, around Pegwell Bay and along the approach to Worth and Ash lie on a migration corridor for birds moving along the English Channel coast in autumn. A thick, berry-laden hedgerow in October is a critical fuelling stop for redwings, fieldfares, blackcaps and goldcrests arriving exhausted from the Continent.

If your priority is water management

Hedgerows intercept rainfall, slow surface run-off and help water percolate into the soil rather than running off the land. This is particularly significant on the light, free-draining chalk and sandy soils of East Kent, where winter rain can move quickly across compacted or frozen ground towards watercourses.

To maximise water management benefit:

  • Maintain an uncompacted soil beneath the hedge. Regular mowing right up to the hedge base compacts the soil and reduces percolation. Leaving a strip of rough vegetation along the base allows the soil structure to remain open.
  • Allow a hedge bank or berm to develop. Traditional Kent field hedges often sit on a low bank, which slows water movement and collects run-off from adjacent land. A garden hedge can achieve a similar effect by allowing the soil level to build up slightly at the base over time.
  • Keep the hedge base vegetated. Bare soil beneath a hedge is more prone to surface run-off in heavy rain than a base covered by grass, dead leaves and rough vegetation.
  • Use the hedge as one element in a wider drainage strategy. Combined with a swale (a shallow, grassed drainage channel) or a rain garden, a hedgerow boundary can direct and slow water movement across a garden or smallholding effectively.

If your priority is erosion and wind control

East Kent is exposed. The low-lying ground around Sandwich and Pegwell Bay, and the exposed downland north of Woodnesborough, can be subject to considerable wind in winter and spring. A hedgerow provides shelter for livestock, crops and garden plants, reduces soil erosion on the windward side, and creates a microclimate that is several degrees warmer in winter and several degrees cooler in summer than unprotected open ground.

To maximise shelter and erosion control:

  • Maintain height on the windward side. A hedge that is allowed to grow taller on the side facing the prevailing south-westerly provides significantly more shelter than a low-clipped boundary.
  • Keep the hedge dense and gapless. Gaps at the base create wind tunnels that can actually increase erosion on the sheltered side. A dense, solid hedge face is more effective than a tall, gappy one.
  • Consider a double hedge or hedge plus bank on very exposed sites. The traditional Kent hedgerow on a bank, with a ditch on the windward side, provides layered protection and is worth recreating on new planting on exposed sites.
  • Allow a rough base. The same undisturbed base that benefits water management also anchors soil against both wind and water erosion.

The management approach that delivers most across all four

If you want to do well across all four functions simultaneously, the management pattern to aim for is:

  • Mixed native species (hawthorn, blackthorn, hazel, field rose, elder, hornbeam or beech as the main components).
  • Maintained height of at least 1.5 to 2 metres, with 3 or more metres on exposed sites.
  • A rotation of cuts rather than annual trimming of the whole hedge. Each section cut once every two to three years, with sections staggered so part of the hedge is always older growth.
  • Trimming in September to October, after berry season has begun but before the worst winter weather.
  • An unmanaged or lightly managed base strip of at least 50 cm.
  • Standard trees within or beside the hedge wherever space allows.

This is not always compatible with a formal garden boundary, and there is no point pretending otherwise. A privet or beech hedge in a residential garden in Sandwich serves its function as a boundary and delivers real environmental value, but it cannot replicate the ecological complexity of a mature native hedgerow. The best approach for a garden is to keep the formal hedge well managed and to consider whether one boundary or part of a boundary could be managed in a more naturalistic way.
