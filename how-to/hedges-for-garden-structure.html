Hedges for garden structure: creating rooms and defined spaces

A hedge does not have to be a boundary marker. Used internally, it divides a garden into rooms, creates enclosed walks, frames a view or hides the vegetable patch from the terrace. Here is how to think about species, height and layout.

Updated September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Kent has a long tradition of hedged garden design. The great gardens of the county, from Leeds Castle to Goodnestone Park near Ash, use clipped hedging to give structure, enclosure and seasonal interest that no fence or wall can replicate. In smaller gardens in Sandwich, Deal and the villages inland, the same principle applies at a different scale.

Using a hedge internally (to divide the garden rather than just bound it) involves different decisions from a boundary hedge: height matters differently, width matters, how long establishment takes matters, and how the hedge looks from close range and from above matters. The checklist below covers the main species worth considering; the sections after it explain when each one is the right choice.

Gardening With Alan Titchmarsh covers structural planting and garden design in detail on his YouTube channel (185,000 subscribers), including establishing formal hedged spaces: Gardening With Alan Titchmarsh on creating garden structure (YouTube).

Quick-reference checklist: species for structural hedging in Kent

Best choices for structural and room-dividing hedges in East Kent

  • Yew (Taxus baccata) - the finest formal hedge plant; dense, dark, clips to a sharp edge; very long-lived
  • Hornbeam (Carpinus betulus) - the best deciduous room-divider; tolerates clay; holds winter leaves for year-round structure
  • Beech (Fagus sylvatica) - similar to hornbeam; better on chalk; same winter leaf retention
  • Privet (Ligustrum ovalifolium) - fastest-growing formal hedge; best for a quick internal screen; needs two cuts per year
  • Box (Buxus sempervirens) - for low edging (30-60cm); classic parterre and path edging; slow; blight-risk now requires careful management
  • Holly (Ilex aquifolium) - evergreen, excellent structure and wildlife value; slower but very effective once established

Nesting season applies to all structural hedges

Yew, hornbeam, beech, holly and privet are all used as nesting sites by garden birds. The nesting season runs 1 March to 31 August. It is an offence under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 to damage or destroy an active nest. Wait until late July or August to cut, or delay until September to February. Always check before cutting.

Yew (Taxus baccata)

Yew is the finest structural hedge plant available in Britain. It clips to a harder, denser edge than any other species, holds its shape through the winter, and on chalk soils around Sandwich and the North Downs it establishes and grows steadily without any particular difficulty. The dark green colour provides a better backdrop for other planting than any other hedge: herbaceous borders in front of yew look significantly better than they do in front of a paler or sparser hedge.

The main objection to yew for structural hedging is speed. Yew grows at roughly 20-30cm per year in good conditions on free-draining chalk: a 2m garden-room hedge from bare-root whips will take seven to ten years to reach height. On heavier soils it is slower. For a garden designed for the next generation rather than the next five years, yew is the obvious choice. For a garden you want to see take shape in five years, it probably is not.

Yew is also toxic. Every part of the plant, berries included, is poisonous to most mammals. This is not usually a problem for a clipped hedge in a managed garden, but it is worth being aware of if horses, cattle or dogs have access to the area.

Hornbeam (Carpinus betulus)

Hornbeam is the best deciduous structural hedge for East Kent gardens that sit on heavier soils. In the gardens of Worth, Ash and Woodnesborough, where clay is the norm, hornbeam outperforms yew (which can struggle on poorly drained sites) and beech (which also dislikes clay). It grows faster than yew, clips tidily and holds its buff-brown dead leaves through winter, giving more or less year-round structure.

For tall room-dividing hedges at 1.8m or more, hornbeam is often the most practical choice for an inland Kent garden. It can be maintained at the same height year after year without thinning or die-back. It is also the most affordable option: bare-root hornbeam whips are available from October at similar prices to bare-root hawthorn, making it economical for longer runs.

Hornbeam is slightly less formally crisp than yew. The deciduous leaf gives a softer texture. If the aim is a flat-topped, geometrically precise hedge at close range, yew wins on texture; if the aim is a tall division that gives seasonal interest as well as structure, hornbeam is usually the better choice.

Beech (Fagus sylvatica)

Beech behaves similarly to hornbeam as a structural hedge and is often a better choice on the chalk and sandy soils of Sandwich, Kingsdown and the North Downs villages. It holds its copper-brown dead leaves through winter and clips to a neat, flat-topped shape. On free-draining chalk it often grows more vigorously than hornbeam.

For a garden room hedge on chalk soil, beech and hornbeam are near equals in terms of function: the choice usually comes down to soil and personal preference for colour (beech leaves are a slightly richer copper in autumn; hornbeam is more buff). Both look striking as a tall enclosing hedge in winter when the dead leaves are backlit by low sun.

Privet (Ligustrum ovalifolium)

Privet is the fastest option for an internal structural hedge and the only species here that can provide meaningful height and density within two to three years of planting. Semi-evergreen (it holds most of its leaves in mild Kent winters), it clips into a formal rectangular shape easily and responds well to repeated trimming.

The trade-off is frequency. Privet grows fast enough that it needs two cuts per year to look neat: typically in May or June and again in August or September. In a large garden where you are managing multiple runs of structural hedging, that is a significant commitment. It also has a somewhat suburban reputation in formal design contexts.

Where privet is genuinely useful is as a fast screen while slower species establish. Planting privet now and slower yew alongside it, then gradually removing the privet over ten years as the yew matures, is a practical approach for gardeners who want both speed and longevity.

Box (Buxus sempervirens)

Box is the traditional low-edging and parterre plant, at its best as a 30-60cm clipped edge rather than a tall room-divider. It holds a precise, formal edge better than any other species at that height, and it is genuinely evergreen.

The difficulty with box in recent years is box blight (the fungal diseases Cylindrocladium buxicola and Volutella buxi) and, increasingly, box tree caterpillar (Cydalima perspectalis), an invasive moth whose larvae defoliate box plants entirely. Both have become widespread across East Kent, including gardens in Sandwich, Deal and the surrounding villages. Box can still be grown, but it requires regular inspections and prompt action when disease or caterpillar appears.

For low hedging where box blight is a serious concern, alternatives include Ilex crenata (Japanese holly, very similar appearance), Euonymus japonicus, or clipped Lonicera nitida. None of these quite matches box for formal precision, but all are lower-maintenance in a garden where box blight has already appeared.

Holly (Ilex aquifolium)

Holly as a structural hedge combines year-round evergreen cover with significant substance. A 2m holly hedge is genuinely opaque, visually and practically. It is slower than hornbeam or privet but faster than people often assume if planted well and kept watered through the first two summers. A decent holly hedge can be achieved in eight to ten years from bare-root whips.

The spiny leaves make holly less pleasant to work next to than yew or hornbeam, and the texture is coarser. For an interior hedge you pass close to daily, a smoother species is usually preferable; for a hedge that is seen from a distance and needs to be solid and evergreen, holly is very effective.

Heights and proportions

For garden rooms to feel properly enclosed, the hedge height should be at least half the width of the space. A 3m wide path enclosed by 1.5m hedges feels like a hedged walk. The same hedges around a 15m lawn feel more like low dividers. Some practical rules of thumb:

  • Low edging (30-60cm): box, Ilex crenata, clipped Lonicera nitida. Suitable for path edging, parterres, kitchen garden compartments.
  • Medium internal screen (1-1.5m): privet, hornbeam, beech. Divides a garden visually without creating complete enclosure. Allows borrowed views over the top.
  • Full room-divider (1.8-2.5m): yew, hornbeam, holly. Creates genuine enclosure. Affects light and wind across the garden significantly. Plan the siting carefully in relation to the sun.
  • Tall backdrop hedge (2.5m+): yew or hornbeam maintained tall. More a wall than a room divider; best used at the end of a garden or as a backdrop to a border.

Planning the layout

Before planting structural hedging, the most important thing is to walk the space at different times of day and different seasons. A tall hedge running north-south casts a substantial shadow on the east-facing side. In a Sandwich or Deal garden where the house already limits light to the rear, an additional room-divider hedge can make areas permanently shaded that were previously acceptable.

Gaps (openings, archways) are part of good hedged garden design. An enclosed space needs an entrance: either a gap left in the planting (which can be given a topiary arch over time) or a deliberate opening that frames a view. Leaving the gap in the original planting plan is much easier than cutting one later. A gap of 0.9-1.2m is a practical passage; a gap of 2m or more reads as a visual opening rather than an entrance.
