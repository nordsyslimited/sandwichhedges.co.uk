Who owns the boundary hedge? How to find out and what it means

Before you cut back a boundary hedge, hire someone to renovate it, or ask your neighbour to deal with it, you need to know who owns it. Here is how to find out, in plain English, without involving a solicitor.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Most hedge disputes begin the same way: one person assumes the hedge belongs to the other, nothing gets done, and after a few years the hedge is 3m wide, 4m tall and blocking everyone's light. Or someone cuts back a hedge they do not own, a neighbour is furious, and the conversation goes downhill from there.

The short version: ownership is in the title deeds. Responsibility for maintenance usually follows ownership. But neither point is as simple as it sounds, particularly in older East Kent properties where boundaries predate Land Registry records by decades.

Step 1: Check the title register

The starting point is HM Land Registry. Most registered properties in England and Wales have a title plan that shows the extent of the land. You can get a copy online for £3 (as of 2026) at gov.uk/search-property-information-land-registry. Get both the title plan and the title register for your property. Also get the register for your neighbour's property if it is registered: the comparison of both plans sometimes resolves the question.

What you are looking for are T-marks. These are small T-shaped symbols on the boundary lines of the title plan. The T points towards the owner who is responsible for that boundary feature. If the T is on your side of the boundary line, the hedge is yours to maintain. If it is on your neighbour's side, it is theirs.

What T-marks mean (and do not mean)

A T-mark is a conventional indicator, not a legal guarantee, unless it is backed up by a specific covenant in the transfer deed. In practice, courts treat T-marks as evidence of intent, but they can be challenged if there is conflicting evidence elsewhere in the title.

An H-mark (two T-marks facing each other, forming an H across the boundary line) indicates a party boundary: both owners are responsible. This is less common on hedges than on fences and walls, but it does occur.

No marks at all is the most common situation in older properties, particularly village houses in Sandwich, Woodnesborough, Eastry and the surrounding parishes, where the land was never formally marked up in this way. In this case, you move to the next steps.

When there are no T-marks

If the title plan has no T-marks, look at the transfer deed (the document that conveyed the property to the previous owner, then to you). These sometimes contain express covenants such as "the buyer shall maintain the northern boundary hedge in good repair and condition." If there is a covenant, it is binding.

If neither the plan nor the deed resolves it, the position is genuinely ambiguous. The traditional common law presumption is that a hedge belongs to the owner on whose side the ditch runs, but this is an agricultural rule from a period when every boundary had a ditch. It applies only rarely in modern domestic gardens.

In practice, where ownership is unclear, the hedge is maintained by whoever is most motivated to maintain it, and disputes are resolved either by agreement with the neighbour or, if that fails, by a solicitor and ultimately a court.

The difference between ownership and maintenance

Owning the boundary hedge does not automatically mean you are legally obliged to maintain it, unless a covenant says so. English law does not impose a general duty to keep a private hedge tidy. What ownership does mean is:

  • You can cut and manage it as you choose, within the usual planning and nesting-season constraints
  • Your neighbour cannot cut it without your permission
  • You are responsible for any damage it causes (for example, if it falls and damages a neighbour's property, or if roots damage their drains)
  • You need your neighbour's consent to enter their land to maintain it from their side

What your neighbour can do if the hedge overhangs their garden

Regardless of who owns the hedge, your neighbour has the common law right of abatement. This means they can cut back any branches or roots that cross the boundary line into their property, up to the boundary, without needing your permission. They must offer you the cuttings rather than disposing of them on your behalf. They cannot cut further than the boundary, and they cannot damage the roots in a way that kills the plant.

This right is limited to growth that has actually crossed the boundary. They cannot cut your hedge from their side simply because it is large. The right only applies to the encroaching part.

When you want to renovate a boundary hedge

Before commissioning any major renovation work on a boundary hedge, confirm ownership. If the hedge is yours, you can cut it as hard as the species allows. If it is shared or its ownership is unclear, cutting hard without your neighbour's knowledge risks a dispute. The practical approach is to talk to them first: most people are glad to agree a maintenance arrangement, particularly if the hedge has been neglected for years.

If the hedge is categorically not yours, you can still cut growth that has crossed the boundary. You can also ask your neighbour to maintain it, and if it blocks light, the High Hedges Act 2005 may give you a route to force them to reduce it (see our guide to the High Hedges Act).

RJL Gardens, a UK professional landscaping channel, has a practical guide to assessing and renovating a neglected boundary hedge. The renovation steps they walk through (assessment, species identification, deciding how hard to cut) apply whether the hedge is yours or jointly managed.

A quick decision guide

Work through these in order:

  1. Check your title plan at Land Registry. Look for T-marks on the boundary in question.
  2. If there is a T-mark on your side: the hedge is yours to maintain. You can proceed.
  3. If there is a T-mark on their side: the hedge is your neighbour's. You can cut back what overhangs into your garden. For anything else, ask them.
  4. If there is an H-mark: it is a shared boundary. Talk to your neighbour about a shared maintenance arrangement.
  5. If there are no marks: check the transfer deed for any express covenant. If there is one, follow it.
  6. If the deed is also silent: the position is ambiguous. Agree with your neighbour informally if possible. If the hedge is blocking light or causing damage, take legal advice.
