What's wrong with my hedge? A troubleshooting guide

Start with the symptom you can see. Work through the questions below and you should be able to narrow down the cause, then follow the link to the right detailed guide for that specific problem.

Updated September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

A hedge that has something wrong with it tends to make itself known gradually: a brown patch here, a gap there, sticky leaves, or a whole section that just never looks right. The difficulty is that the same visual symptom, say a section of browning, can have three or four different causes, each with a different fix. Treating box blight the same way you would treat drought damage, for example, makes neither problem better.

Martin Fish of Pots and Trowels, who has been advising on garden problems for over 40 years and judges for the RHS, demonstrates the importance of reading the hedge carefully before reaching for any remedy: his video "Hedge Cutting Explained and Demonstrated" (youtube.com/watch?v=pbLr1l7uNrs) shows the inspection process that should come before any cutting or treatment. The same principle applies here: look first, diagnose second, then act.

This guide is structured as a decision tree. Start with the symptom column that matches what you are seeing, answer the questions, and follow the link to the detailed guide for that problem.

Before any cutting or treatment

If you are cutting as part of diagnosing or treating the hedge, the nesting season rules still apply. Under the UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. The safe cutting window is September to February. Check the hedge for active nests before any work. For neighbour hedge-height disputes arising from an overgrown hedge, see hedge heights and neighbour law.

Branch A: Browning or die-back

Is it a conifer (leylandii, thuja, lawson cypress)?

Brown patches on conifers are one of the most common problems we see across East Kent. The first question is whether the browning appeared after a cut, or without any trimming.

  • Browning after a cut: almost certainly caused by cutting back into old wood beyond the green growing layer. Most conifers cannot regenerate from bare brown stems. The brown area will not recover. See our detailed guide: conifer hedge going brown.
  • Browning without a recent cut (patches all over, or internal browning): could be drought stress, spider mite, or a disease such as phytophthora root rot. Check for webbing on the inner stems (spider mite) and look at the roots for black discolouration (phytophthora).
  • Brown only on windward side in late winter/early spring: likely cold wind scorch. Common on lawson cypress and western red cedar in exposed East Kent plots. The affected growth should regenerate once the weather warms, if the damage is not severe.

For the full conifer diagnosis: conifer hedge going brown.

Is it a box hedge?

Box problems in East Kent have become almost unavoidable. Two distinct causes account for most of them:

  • Brown patches with clear boundaries, dark-streaked stems, leaf drop in wet weather: box blight. A fungal disease that has spread widely across Kent over the past decade. Full guide: box blight treatment.
  • Stripped bare stems, webbing, pale green caterpillars (May to October): box tree moth caterpillar. Now present throughout East Kent. Full guide: box tree moth caterpillar damage.

Is it a section of a non-conifer, non-box hedge that has died back suddenly?

Sudden die-back in one section of an otherwise healthy established hedge is serious. The most likely causes:

  • Honey fungus: look at the base of the dead section. Peel back a small area of bark from the collar near the roots. White, fan-shaped mycelium with a faint mushroom smell is honey fungus. Bootlace-like strands (rhizomorphs) in the soil nearby confirm it. The affected section will need to be removed entirely. Full guide: honey fungus in hedges.
  • Waterlogging: a section that died off after a very wet winter, especially on clay soils (common in the low-lying areas around Ash, Worth and the Stour Valley), may have suffered root rot from waterlogging. Check if the base soil is persistently wet.
  • Drought stress: in the dry summers that are increasingly common in East Kent, newly planted sections or hedges on free-draining soils can die back if not watered. Full guide: hedge care in a dry summer.

Branch B: Sticky leaves, deposits or discolouration

Sticky leaves with black, sooty deposits

This combination is almost always caused by sap-sucking insects: aphids, scale insects or whitefly. The sticky substance is honeydew they excrete; the black coating is sooty mould growing on the honeydew. The mould is unsightly but does not directly harm the plant. The infestation does. Full guide: hedge aphids and scale insects.

Orange, rust-coloured spots on leaves

Rust diseases affect several hedging species. Hawthorn rust is one of the most visible, producing orange-yellow spore masses on the leaves in summer. Most rust infections on established hedges are not serious, but severe infections on young stock can check growth. A hard pruning after the season ends, plus good air circulation, usually keeps rust manageable.

White, powdery coating on new growth

Powdery mildew. Common on privet, beech and many other hedging species in warm, dry summers followed by humid conditions, which is a pattern Kent gardens see regularly. The plant rarely suffers lasting harm from mildew alone. Remove and bin (do not compost) affected growth when you cut, and avoid overhead watering in the evening.

Branch C: Gaps or missing sections

Is the hedge newly planted (under two years old)?

Sparse growth, slow filling-in and a few gaps are normal in the first one to two years, particularly on bare-root hedging planted in autumn or winter. As long as the stems are still green and the root zone is not waterlogged or bone dry, patience and correct aftercare are usually all that is needed. Full guide: newly planted hedge care.

Is the hedge established, with a specific section that has failed?

Gaps in an established hedge nearly always have a cause. Look first at the soil and roots in the failed section: if the ground is compacted, waterlogged or has old tree stumps nearby (possible honey fungus source), fix the underlying problem before replanting. Full guide: filling gaps in hedges.

Branch D: The whole hedge looks tired, sparse or overgrown

A hedge that has had several years without proper cutting, or has been cut too hard repeatedly, often reaches a point where it looks sparse, gappy and shapeless without any single identifiable cause. The good news is that most hedging species are very resilient, and a properly managed renovation programme over two to three years can transform the worst-looking specimens.

Key questions before starting:

  • Does the species tolerate a hard cut? (Beech, privet, hornbeam, yew, hawthorn and most native species do. Leylandii and most other conifers do not.)
  • Is the root system still healthy? (Test by cutting a few stems close to the base: green wood and white roots indicate a viable plant.)
  • Has it been cut at the right time of year? (September to February for most species.)

If the answers are positive, a renovation programme is realistic. Full guide: renovating a neglected hedge. If the hedge is a conifer that has been cut beyond the green, or if honey fungus is confirmed in the roots, removal and replanting is usually the better option.
