Bird cherry hedges in Kent: planting Prunus padus

A native species with spring blossom and autumn berries that birds genuinely queue up for. Here is how to get it into your hedge and keep it there.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Bird cherry (Prunus padus) is one of those species that earns its place quickly. The name is not poetic licence: blackbirds, thrushes, and in good winters waxwings, strip the small black berries by September. The white raceme flowers in April and May are early nectar for bees and hoverflies. And on the chalky, free-draining soils around Deal, Worth, and the lanes east of Sandwich, it copes with summer drought better than many alternatives.

It is not a formal boundary hedge in the way privet or leylandii are. Bird cherry does best as an infill species in a mixed native hedge alongside hawthorn, blackthorn, and field maple. Left unchecked it will reach 10-15m, so it needs managing. With a single annual cut in winter it stays at whatever height suits the site.

Before you start

  • Bare-root plants only arrive November to March. You cannot impulse-buy bird cherry in June. Plan your order in autumn.
  • Gloves and eye protection when cutting any established hedge nearby. Flying debris is the main hazard from powered trimmers.
  • RCD protection on any electric trimmer; keep the cable behind you and in sight.
  • Check for nests in any existing nearby hedges before starting work. See the nesting callout below.

Planting guide

When to plant

Bare-root bird cherry is available from October through to March while the plant is dormant. This is the right window: the roots establish over winter and the plant is ready to push on in spring. Container-grown plants can go in almost any time the soil is workable, but autumn is still the most reliable slot. Avoid planting when the ground is frozen solid or baked dry.

Spacing and row layout

For a mixed native hedge, plant bird cherry whips 30-45cm apart. A staggered double row gives a thicker result: set the two rows about 30cm apart, offset so each plant in the second row sits between two in the first. If you are adding bird cherry as infill into an existing hedge with gaps, single plants spaced into those gaps is fine.

Soil and site

Bird cherry tolerates most soils. It prefers moisture-retentive ground but will not sit happily in waterlogged conditions. The chalk and loam soils of Thanet, the Deal coast, and Worth perform well: free-draining but not parched. Avoid ground that holds standing water through winter.

It grows in full sun or partial shade. South-facing banks are fine; heavy shade under mature trees is not ideal. Coastal exposure is manageable with windbreak planting behind it in the first year.

Planting

  1. Dig a hole wide enough to spread the roots without bending them, and deep enough to match the soil mark on the stem.
  2. Place the plant, spread the roots, and backfill with the original soil. No compost or fertiliser is needed.
  3. Firm gently with your foot to remove air pockets.
  4. Stake young plants in exposed positions. A short 60cm stake and tree tie just below the first side branches is enough; remove it after the second winter once the roots are anchored.
  5. Water in well, even in autumn. The roots need moisture to begin establishing.
  6. Mulch with a 7-10cm layer of bark around the base, kept clear of the stem itself, to retain moisture and suppress weeds.

First-year care

Keep weeds away from the base for the first two seasons. Competition from grass and nettles is the main reason new whips fail. Water during any dry spells in the first spring and summer. No hard pruning in year one: let the plant establish a root system before you start shaping it.

Ongoing pruning

Once the hedge is established, bird cherry tolerates fairly hard cuts. The best window is September to February, well outside the nesting season. A single annual cut in late autumn or winter is usually enough for a managed hedge at 1.5-2m.

If the hedge has been left and reached an unmanageable height, a hard renovation cut back to 60-80cm in winter will usually produce vigorous re-growth the following spring. Expect a scrappy year while it recovers, but it will come back.

Remember that bird cherry can reach 10-15m unmanaged. In a Kent lane or garden hedge context, an annual cut is not optional if you want it to stay at hedge height.

Wildlife value

The white drooping flower clusters (racemes) appear in April and May, among the first native trees to flower after hawthorn blossom. They are visited by early bees and hoverflies when other nectar sources are sparse.

The berries ripen from late July through August and are small, black, and intensely attractive to birds. Blackbirds and thrushes take them first. In years when Scandinavian waxwings irrupt into the UK (roughly every five to seven years), bird cherry is one of the species that draws them into garden hedges. Near the marshes around Worth and Sandwich Bay, where migrants stop over, a fruiting bird cherry hedge in September can be worth watching.

The foliage supports a range of moth and butterfly larvae, adding to the insect value that in turn feeds nesting birds.

Nesting season: check before you cut

Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs roughly from 1 March to 31 August. Bird cherry and mixed native hedges are actively used as nesting sites. The safe cutting window is September to February. A light summer trim is only acceptable once you have checked the hedge is demonstrably empty.
