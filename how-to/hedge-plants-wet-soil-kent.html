Hedge plants for wet and waterlogged soil in Kent

Most popular hedging species are indifferent or actively hostile to waterlogged conditions. Here's which ones actually work on a wet site, and which to steer clear of.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Kent's soil is far more varied than people assume. The free-draining sand and chalk of the coastal strip around Sandwich Bay is one thing: see our guide on planting hedges in sandy coastal soil for that. But the low-lying areas around Ash, Worth and the Stour valley have heavy clay that pools in winter. Parts of the Sandwich Bay hinterland and the areas around Eastry have a perched water table that can keep root zones wet from November through to April.

Habitat Aid, a UK specialist in native plants for habitat creation, covers the principles of native hedge planting on their YouTube channel, including species choice for different site conditions. Their approach emphasises matching species to what the site actually offers rather than forcing unsuitable plants into improved soil.

"How to plant a native hedge" by Habitat Aid (YouTube). Habitat Aid are a UK B Corp and specialist native plant nursery, listed by HedgeLink as a source of native hedging stock.

Plant outside nesting season

Planting and ground preparation in wet areas can disturb wildlife. Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, disturbing an active bird's nest is an offence. The nesting season runs from 1 March to 31 August. For bare-root planting, the window of late October to late March falls naturally within the safe period and also coincides with the wet season, when new plants establish most easily on a poorly drained site.

Wet soil in Kent: what it actually looks like

The practical categories worth knowing:

  • Seasonally wet: the ground is boggy or flooded from November to March, then dries out by late spring. Most native species handle this well.
  • Persistently wet: the water table stays high, or the site is a natural sump with no outlet. This is where species choice becomes critical: only a handful of plants tolerate year-round root saturation.
  • Heavy clay that drains slowly: not the same as waterlogging. Clay holds moisture but does eventually drain. Most hedging species manage on seasonally wet clay provided the surface doesn't pool for months.

Species to avoid on wet sites

These are the species we frequently see failing on low-lying or poorly drained plots in East Kent:

Leylandii and conifers

The roots of conifers in general, and leylandii in particular, rot in persistently waterlogged conditions. A leylandii hedge that was looking fine in year two can start dropping sections in year three if the site floods reliably over winter. If drainage cannot be improved, plant something else entirely.

Yew

Tolerates drought and chalk extremely well but is genuinely vulnerable to waterlogging. A yew hedge on a wet site will establish slowly, develop yellow patches and eventually die back from the roots. On anything other than occasionally wet clay, choose a different species.

Beech

Beech does best on free-draining chalk and sandy soils. It will grow on heavier soils but a persistently wet water table causes it to die back progressively. Hornbeam is nearly always the better choice on a heavier, wetter site in Kent.

Cherry laurel and Portuguese laurel

Both will survive occasional flooding but perform poorly on persistently damp or waterlogged ground. They are woodland-edge plants that like moisture-retentive soil but not saturation: the distinction matters on a Kent clay site that pools from December to March.

What about improving drainage instead?

For a short garden hedge, a French drain or rubble-filled sump alongside the planting trench can help enough to expand species choice. It's worth doing if you want a formal beech or hornbeam hedge and the site is only seasonally wet. For a field boundary or a long run alongside a ditch that floods regularly, drainage is usually impractical: match the species to the conditions instead.

The one trap to avoid: improving the soil in the planting trench alone without addressing drainage can make things worse. A well-improved, free-draining trench in waterlogged clay acts as a sump, drawing water from the surrounding soil into the root zone. It's better to accept the conditions and plant accordingly.

The species that struggles the most on a wet site isn't always the one that dies first. Sometimes it just grows slowly and thinly for five years before you replace it. Alder would have given you a dense hedge by then.
