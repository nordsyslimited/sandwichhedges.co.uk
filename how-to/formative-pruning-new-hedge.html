Formative pruning for a new hedge: what to cut in the first two years

Many gardeners plant a hedge and leave it alone for two or three years, waiting for height. For deciduous species this is the most common mistake in hedge establishment. The initial cuts determine the shape of the hedge for the next twenty years.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

When bare-root hedge whips arrive in a bundle of twenty or fifty, they look like little more than sticks with roots attached. The instinct is to plant them and leave them alone to grow. This is correct for some species, and quite wrong for others.

Formative pruning is the term for the deliberate shaping cuts made in a hedge's first one to three years. Done well, it produces a hedge that is dense from the base, well-clothed all the way down, and structurally sound. Left out, it produces a hedge that is bushy at the top and open at the bottom, or straight-stemmed and leggy rather than branching and thick. Correcting a badly established hedge takes years and sometimes requires a renovation hard cut that sets the whole thing back by two seasons.

The good news is that the work is not extensive. Formative pruning on a new hedge typically takes half an hour per twenty metres. The decisions are the harder part.

Nesting season: check before you cut

Under the UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs roughly from 1 March to 31 August. Even newly planted hedge whips can be used by birds from the second growing season onwards if they have put on enough growth. The safe formative pruning window is September to February for deciduous species, and early March for a quick spring formative cut before nesting starts. If work is needed between March and August, check the hedge carefully first.

Tools for formative pruning

For small whips you need only secateurs or hand shears for most formative work. Good-quality bypass secateurs make a cleaner cut on young stems than anvil types, which can crush rather than cut on thin green growth. For slightly older plants or thicker stems, loppers give more leverage. A hedge trimmer is not ideal for formative work: the goal is selective cuts to leaders and side-shoots, not a uniform clip across the full profile.

The key rule: treat deciduous and coniferous species differently

The single most important principle in formative pruning is that deciduous hardwood hedging species and coniferous species require opposite treatment in their early years.

Deciduous hardwoods, including beech, hornbeam, hawthorn, blackthorn, hazel and field maple, respond to hard pruning by producing multiple lateral shoots from low on the stem. Cutting them back hard early is precisely what creates the dense, bushy, basal growth that makes a good hedge. Leaving them to grow uncut produces a row of single-stemmed plants that fill the top but are open at the base.

Conifers, including leylandii, thuja and lawson cypress, do not regenerate freely from old wood after a hard cut. The goal in the early years is to build height and establish a healthy root system. Cutting back the leading shoots delays height establishment significantly and can damage the plant's natural form in a way that takes years to recover. The correct approach is to leave conifers entirely uncut until they near the desired height, then begin trimming.

Broadleaf evergreens, including privet, laurel, photinia and lonicera nitida, sit between the two extremes. They respond reasonably well to hard cuts but do not need the same drastic initial cut as deciduous hardwoods. The goal in year one is to tip-prune to encourage lateral branching, with progressively firmer cuts in years two and three.

Deciduous hardwoods: year one and two

Plant bare-root whips in late autumn or early winter, from October to February. As soon as they are planted and the ground is firm around the roots, cut all growth back by one-third to one-half. This sounds drastic on a 60–90 cm whip, but it is correct. The cut forces the plant to put energy into multiple basal shoots rather than extending the single leader.

By spring the whips will produce new shoots from several points along the stem, including low down near the base. This basal growth is what you are trying to encourage. Without the initial hard cut, the plant grows strongly from the top and the base remains open and sparsely branched.

At the end of year one's growing season (autumn), assess the structure. The leading shoots will have extended by 20–40 cm. Cut back all growth by one-third again, and cut any particularly long lateral shoots that are extending significantly beyond the target line. The aim at this stage is not a flat, even profile, but a compact, well-branched structure with active growth from the base to the top.

By the end of year two, with a second hard cutback behind it, a beech or hornbeam hedge planted as 60 cm whips will typically be producing a dense, multi-stemmed structure to 90–120 cm, with growth from low on the stem. This is the foundation for a good hedge. Year three onwards is maintenance trimming: keeping the faces flat and the top level, with an annual cut in summer.

Specific notes by species:

  • Beech and copper beech: Cut to 30–40 cm above ground level after planting. Cut back by one-third in autumn of year one. Beech retains dead leaves through winter (marcescence), which provides winter interest but means the hedge looks cluttered in year one and two: this is normal.
  • Hornbeam: Same approach as beech. Hornbeam is slightly more vigorous and may need a heavier cut in year one to keep lateral growth balanced.
  • Hawthorn and blackthorn: Can be cut back to 30 cm above ground immediately after planting. Very vigorous: will produce dense lateral growth quickly. Year two cut can be heavier, shaping toward the target profile.
  • Hazel: Similar to hawthorn. Can tolerate a hard initial cut. Year two, begin shaping toward the intended profile width and height.
  • Field maple: Cut back to 40–50 cm after planting. Vigorous grower; responds well to formative pruning.

Broadleaf evergreens: year one and two

Broadleaf evergreens are usually planted as pot-grown plants rather than bare-root whips, which means they already have an established root system and can tolerate a more considered approach to formative pruning.

In year one, the priority is establishment rather than pruning. Allow the plant to settle through its first growing season. In late spring or early summer of year one, once new growth is showing actively, tip-prune the leading shoots by 5–10 cm to encourage branching. This is a light intervention, not a hard cut: the goal is to stimulate lateral growth without stressing a plant that is still establishing its root system.

In year two, begin more assertive formative shaping. The plant should by now be growing with reasonable vigour. Cut all three faces lightly, taking 10–15 cm off the leading shoots and beginning to train the faces toward the intended profile. For a formal hedge with a slight taper (wider at the base than the top), begin establishing that profile from year two.

Specific notes:

  • Privet: Vigorous and forgiving. Can be cut more firmly than most evergreens. In year one, tip-prune; in year two, begin proper formative shaping toward the intended height and width.
  • Cherry laurel and Portuguese laurel: Larger-leafed; use secateurs rather than a hedge trimmer to avoid unsightly cut leaves on the face. Year one tip pruning; year two begin full formative shaping.
  • Photinia: Year one tip-prune to encourage lateral branching. The characteristic red tips appear on new growth, so each formative cut produces the decorative effect the plant is known for. Year two begin formal shaping.
  • Lonicera nitida: Very responsive to pruning. Can be cut back more firmly than most broadleaf evergreens from year one. Grows quickly and may need two formative cuts in year one alone if growth is vigorous.

Conifers: what not to do in years one and two

The correct formative approach for conifers is deliberate restraint. Do not cut the leading shoots. Do not cut back the main framework. The goal in years one, two and three is height and root establishment.

Leylandii in particular is frequently planted and then cut hard in year one or two in an attempt to control size from the start. This is counterproductive: cutting the leading shoot delays the hedge reaching the desired height by one to two full growing seasons, and the plant recovers its vigour and continues growing at the same rate once the leader re-establishes.

What you can do with conifers in year two or three is lightly trim the sideshoots, reducing their length by 5–10 cm to encourage density on the faces. Keep the leader untouched. Once the hedge has reached 20–30 cm above the desired final height, cut the leader back to that height and begin maintaining the top. From that point, maintenance trimming (once or twice per year on the faces) keeps the hedge at size.

Yew (Taxus baccata) is an exception among conifers and closer in behaviour to a broadleaf evergreen: it will regenerate from old wood if necessary, tolerates harder cuts, and can receive light formative pruning from year two. Even so, the same principle applies in year one: establish height first, then begin shaping.

Year three: establishing the maintenance routine

By the end of year two on a well-managed hedge, the basic structure should be in place. Year three is when you establish the maintenance trimming routine that will continue annually from that point. For deciduous species, one trim in mid-summer (July or August) keeps the shape clean through the growing season. For many evergreens, two trims per year produce a neater result. For conifers, once or twice per year on the faces, keeping the leader controlled.
