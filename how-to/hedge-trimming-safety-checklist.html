Hedge trimming safety checklist

Ten things to run through before you pick up a hedge trimmer. From PPE and nesting season to wasp nests and overhead cables, based on what we check every single time we start a job in Kent.

Updated September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Most hedge trimming injuries and most legal problems with hedges come from the same place: somebody just got on with it without a quick look first. The checklist below takes under five minutes. It's what we run through on every job, whether it's a small privet in Sandwich or a two-metre laurel run in Eastry.

The quick checklist

  1. Check for nesting birds (Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 applies)
  2. Look for wasp or hornet nests
  3. Inspect for overhead cables and buried lines
  4. Clear the area of furniture, toys, hoses and trip hazards
  5. Put on full PPE: gloves, glasses, ear defenders, sturdy footwear
  6. Inspect the trimmer blade for damage, tightness and sharpness
  7. Check fuel, charge or cable condition
  8. Confirm the blade guard is in place
  9. Lay a groundsheet at the hedge base
  10. Tell a neighbour if the hedge is a shared boundary

Nesting season: the legal check

The Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it a criminal offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs roughly from 1 March to 31 August. Before touching any hedge between those dates, you must check for active nests. If birds are nesting, you wait until they have fledged. There is no exception for a hedge that is getting unruly.

1. Check for nesting birds

Stand quietly beside the hedge for three to five minutes. Watch for birds flying in and out of the same spot, listen for cheeping. Then, gently part the foliage in several places and look inside, particularly in the lower two thirds where most nests sit. If you find a nest with eggs or chicks, mark the spot with canes and wait. The whole hedge does not need to stop, just the section within about a metre of the nest.

For more on what to look for and what the law actually says, see our guide to wildlife-friendly hedge trimming.

2. Check for wasp and hornet nests

Wasps nest in hedges from around June through to October, most heavily in August and September, which is exactly peak trimming season. Look for a steady stream of wasps going in and out of the same gap. Do not probe or poke. Back away calmly. Arrange for a pest controller to remove the nest before you cut. Hitting a wasp nest with a spinning blade is not something you want to experience.

Hornets are less common in East Kent but are more defensive and have a more painful sting. Treat any hornets' nest with the same caution.

3. Check for overhead cables

Look up before you start. In Kent's older villages and streets, overhead service cables running between poles and houses are still common. These are not dead wires. Do not allow a trimmer blade, ladder or falling branch to come within three metres of an overhead power line. If the hedge has grown into overhead cables, contact the network operator before you do anything. For the service cable running from the pole to your house, maintain a sensible clearance and do not use the hedge trimmer above head height anywhere near it.

Also check for any low-level cables, irrigation pipes or lighting cables in the ground around the base of the hedge. A probe or a careful look at the soil surface usually tells you if something's been laid.

4. Clear the area

Garden furniture, children's toys, hoses, pots and anything else around the base of the hedge needs to go. A hedge trimmer throws debris, sometimes sharp sticks, several metres. Anything on the ground becomes a trip hazard once you start moving along the hedge with your attention on the blades. Give yourself a clear run on both sides.

5. Put on full PPE before touching the trimmer

This is not optional. The kit you need:

  • Cut-resistant gloves. Standard garden gloves will not stop a hedge trimmer blade. Wear gloves rated to EN 388.
  • Safety glasses or a face shield. Twigs and small stones come off at speed. Glasses protect your eyes, a face shield also protects your face. Either is fine for most domestic work.
  • Ear defenders. Petrol trimmers are consistently above 85 dB. Even cordless trimmers run at 75 dB or more. Prolonged exposure damages hearing. Wear them.
  • Sturdy closed-toe footwear. No sandals, no canvas trainers. Boots or heavy shoes protect your feet from dropped equipment and debris on the ground.
  • Long sleeves and long trousers. Not mandatory but recommended. They protect you from scratches from the hedge itself and from flying material.

If you are working from a ladder, add a hard hat. Falling sections of hedge are heavier than they look.

6. Inspect the trimmer blade

Before each use, look at the blade with the trimmer off and unplugged or with the battery out. Check for:

  • Bent or chipped teeth. A bent tooth will catch and throw material unpredictably.
  • Loose blade bolts. Shake the blade gently. Any movement means it needs tightening before use.
  • Sap, resin or old plant material clogging the teeth. Clean it off with a rag and a little blade oil before you start.
  • Sharpness. A blunt blade tears rather than cuts, which is harder on the trimmer and harder on the hedge. If the blade is dragging rather than cutting cleanly, it needs sharpening. See our sharpening guide or send the blade off to a sharpener before the season.

7. Check fuel, charge or cable

  • Petrol: Check the fuel level. Top up away from open flames and wipe up any spill before starting. Check the oil level if your trimmer has a separate oil reservoir.
  • Cordless: Confirm the battery is charged. A half-charged battery dying mid-job on a tall hedge is an inconvenience. On a ladder it is a safety risk.
  • Corded: Inspect the cable for any nicks, abrasions or repairs with tape. If the cable is damaged, do not use the trimmer. Inspect the extension lead and its connections too. The cable should be run behind you and away from the hedge you are cutting, not across in front of you.

8. Confirm the blade guard is in place

The blade guard on the end of a hedge trimmer is there to stop the tip of the moving blade from catching on you as you sweep along. Do not remove it. Check it is properly fixed before you start. On petrol trimmers, also check the throttle trigger and the safety lock-off switch work as they should: the blade should not run unless both are engaged.

9. Lay a groundsheet

A tarpaulin or old bed sheet at the base of the hedge catches clippings and makes clean-up a fraction of the time it would otherwise take. For a long hedge, run several overlapping sheets. When you are done, roll them up from the ends and empty into the brown bin or your compost heap. The sheet also reveals wasp nests, cables and anything else hidden in the base that you missed in step 2.

10. Tell the neighbour if it's a shared boundary

If the hedge sits on or near a boundary, a quick word with the neighbour before you start avoids any surprise and any legal difficulty if debris crosses into their garden. If you intend to cut the neighbour's side as well, you need their permission. Our boundary hedge law guide covers the detail.

Staying safe while you work

The checklist gets you to a safe start. A few things worth keeping in mind while the trimmer is running:

  • Always keep two hands on the trimmer. Never hold a branch with one hand and trim with the other.
  • Cut away from your body. The blade moves toward the hedge, not toward you.
  • Never reach above shoulder height with a standard trimmer. Use a long-reach pole trimmer for anything higher, and only from the ground.
  • Switch off and wait for the blade to stop completely before moving the trimmer, adjusting your position, clearing a blockage or putting it down.
  • Take regular breaks. Fatigue is a cause of accidents with power tools.
