Pollinator-friendly hedges in Kent: how to plant and manage for bees

A hedge is not just a boundary. Managed well, it is a multi-season nectar source, a nesting corridor and one of the most useful things you can do for pollinators in an East Kent garden. Here is how to make yours count.

Updated September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

UK hedgerow coverage has declined significantly over the last century, and what remains is the backbone of our insect ecology. A single mature hedgerow of mixed native species can support thousands of invertebrate species. In a garden context, even a short stretch of the right plants, managed at the right time, makes a genuine difference.

East Kent has its own ecological character worth leaning into: the chalk soils around Eastry, Woodnesborough and Ash support a slightly different wildflower and insect community than the sandy strip at Sandwich Bay; the coastal mildness around Deal and Sandwich extends the active season at both ends. The plant choices and timing advice below reflect those local conditions.

Step 1: Choose species that flower, not just fill space

Most garden hedges are planted for structure and privacy. That is reasonable, but a hedge where every plant is leylandii or laurel provides almost nothing for pollinators, because neither produces accessible nectar flowers. Swapping even a quarter of the plants to flowering species transforms the ecological value.

The best native species for pollinators in East Kent, in rough order of value:

  • Hawthorn (Crataegus monogyna): the single highest-value flowering hedging plant for bees in the UK. May blossom feeds queen bumblebees and honeybees at a critical colony-building period. Also supports around 150 invertebrate species on its leaves. Spiny enough to make an effective stock-proof barrier. Fast-growing and unfussy on chalk or loam.
  • Blackthorn (Prunus spinosa): flowers in March, before almost anything else, when the earliest queen bumblebees emerge. Provides nectar at a point in the season when other sources are scarce. Produces sloe berries, the late-autumn food source for thrushes and waxwings passing through the Kent coast.
  • Dog rose (Rosa canina): the pale pink flowers in June are popular with bumblebees and hoverflies. Produces hips that feed birds through autumn and winter. Scrambling habit can be incorporated into a mixed native hedge rather than used as a formal clipped element.
  • Elder (Sambucus nigra): flat-topped cream flowers in June are excellent for hoverflies, some bee species and various small flies. Fast-growing and ideal for filling gaps quickly. Elderberries in late summer and autumn feed blackbirds and thrushes.
  • Hazel (Corylus avellana): catkins in February and March are wind-pollinated but the pollen is gathered by early solitary bees. Dense hazel provides nesting sites as well as food. Tolerates fairly hard cutting without complaint and responds well to coppicing.
  • Field maple (Acer campestre): small greenish-yellow flowers in May are underrated for bees, particularly hoverflies. Excellent autumn colour. Grows well on the chalk soils around Sandwich and Eastry.
  • Escallonia (for coastal gardens): the long summer flowering season, from June to September, makes escallonia an exceptional choice for the coastal gardens around Deal, Sandwich Bay and Kingsdown where it thrives in the salt air. Bees, hoverflies and butterflies all visit the flowers. Not native but high nectar value and tough coastal performance justify it for pollinator planting in the right location.

When to cut a hedge that supports nesting birds

The UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 protects active bird nests. A hedge rich in hawthorn, blackthorn and dog rose is exactly the habitat that blackbirds, dunnocks, whitethroats and many other species choose to nest in. The main nesting season runs from 1 March to 31 August. Cut or renovate your hedge outside this window (September to February) and you avoid disturbing nesting birds. If you need to cut during the season, check carefully for active nests first and do not proceed if any are found in use.

Step 2: Mix species for a longer season

A single-species hawthorn hedge is excellent, but peaks in May. A mixed hedge that includes blackthorn (March), hazel (February), hawthorn (May), dog rose (June), elder (June), field maple (May) and escallonia (June to September) provides a near-continuous nectar supply from February to September. That covers the entire active season for UK pollinators.

The classic mixed native hedge ratio for wildlife in Kent is roughly: 40% hawthorn, 20% blackthorn, 15% hazel, 10% field maple, 10% dog rose, 5% elder. Adjust proportions based on how much structure and how much "informal wildlife" character you want: more hawthorn and blackthorn for structure; more dog rose and elder for a looser, wildlife-focused hedge.

For a more formal garden where a purely native hedge feels too rough, a hedge of photinia or hornbeam as the backbone, with a strip of mixed native planting alongside it, gives privacy and wildlife value in parallel without one compromising the other.

Step 3: Plant at the right time

For native species bought as bare-root stock, November to March is the planting window. Bare-root plants are cheaper, generally establish faster and are the normal choice for mixed native hedges. They should be planted as soon as they arrive from the nursery, or at most heel-in temporarily if the ground is frozen or waterlogged.

Spacing for a wildlife-focused single-row hedge: 25 to 30 cm between plants for a tight, stock-proof result. For a double-staggered row (richer habitat, better insulation, more nesting opportunities): 30 cm between plants in each row, with a 30 to 40 cm gap between rows, with the rows offset so plants do not sit directly opposite each other. A double-row native hedge with long grass at its base is the closest a small garden can get to a genuine hedgerow habitat.

Step 4: Manage the base

The ground at the base of a hedge, if left uncut or only cut once a year, becomes some of the most valuable invertebrate habitat in a garden. A strip of long grass and wildflowers, 30 to 60 cm wide, along the hedge base provides shelter for ground-nesting bees and beetles, overwintering sites for chrysalises, and hunting ground for hedgehogs. Cutting it once in late autumn rather than keeping it mown through the summer makes a significant difference. Dead stems from the previous season, left standing through winter, provide overwintering habitat for solitary bees.

On the chalk soils around Eastry, Woodnesborough and Ash, a hedge base strip sown with chalk-grassland wildflowers (wild marjoram, kidney vetch, bird's-foot trefoil, cowslip) produces outstanding results for pollinators from May through to September.

Step 5: Cut at the right time

Cutting timing is where most well-intentioned pollinator hedges fall short. The typical garden schedule of "cut in May and August" removes hawthorn and blackthorn blossom in May before bees can use it. The better schedule for a pollinator hedge:

  • One cut per year, late August to September: all flowering has finished, most insects have moved on from the hedge to other foraging grounds, berries are beginning to set, and the hedge can be shaped before autumn growth slows. This single late-summer cut is the most pollinator-friendly possible for a formal hedge.
  • Informal hedge, two cuts: a light cut in late June or early July, after hawthorn and blackthorn have flowered, and a second in late August or September. This keeps the hedge from getting too large while protecting most of the spring flowering period.
  • For escallonia specifically: cut after the main flowering flush ends in September. Cutting in June or July removes months of summer flowers.
