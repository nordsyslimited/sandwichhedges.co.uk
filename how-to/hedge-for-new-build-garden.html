Choosing a hedge for a new build garden in Kent

New build gardens in East Kent tend to arrive with a fence panel and very little else. Here is how to decide whether a hedge beats the fence, and which species to plant first.

Updated September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

We cut a lot of hedges on the newer estates around Sandwich, Worth, Ash and Woodnesborough. Many were planted by the first owners of houses built in the 2010s and 2020s, and in almost every case, the hedge outlasts two or three sets of fence panels and adds far more to the property than a screen of timber ever would. The people who planted them early are consistently glad they did.

That said, the choice of species matters more than the decision to plant at all. A species that suits the soil and position will be low-maintenance and look good for decades. The wrong species will demand attention every few months or limp along badly.

Hedge versus fence: the practical comparison

The RHS covered the case for choosing a hedge over a fence directly in their planting guide, emphasising the ecological and long-term practical benefits. Watch: How to plant a hedge and why it's better than a fence (RHS on YouTube, 78,000+ subscribers, official channel).

Factor Fence Hedge
Privacy Immediate from day one 2 to 5 years to full coverage
Lifespan 10 to 20 years before replacement 100+ years with good care
Annual cost Low initially, high at replacement One annual trim; low long-term
Storm resilience Panels blow down in high winds Windbreak effect; rarely damaged
Wildlife Low: nesting only on surfaces High: nesting, food, cover, insects
Noise/pollution Negligible effect Mild but measurable reduction
Effort to establish None: erected by contractor Soil prep + watering first 2 years
Property value No measurable uplift Broadly positive, especially at maturity

The practical conclusion for most new build gardens in East Kent is: if you can tolerate a few years of lower privacy whilst the hedge establishes, a hedge will outperform the fence on almost every dimension beyond the first two years. Many people run both: leave the fence in place whilst the hedge grows, then remove the panels once the hedge is self-supporting.

What the soil does to species choice in East Kent

Most of East Kent sits on chalk or free-draining loam, particularly inland from Sandwich, Deal and Ash. The soils tend to be alkaline (pH 7.5 to 8.0 is common) and dry out quickly in summer. A few spots around Sandwich Bay and close to the river Stour have sandier, less fertile conditions. The coastal zone around Deal and Kingsdown also gets significant salt-laden wind.

This matters because some popular hedge species struggle in these conditions:

  • Leylandii is often planted on new builds by developers for its speed, but it has a shallow root system and suffers on thin chalk. It also needs cutting two or three times a year to stay presentable, and the maintenance cost mounts up over time.
  • Laurel (cherry laurel) is one of the more reliable performers on chalky soils and copes well with a certain amount of shade. It is not the most exciting plant but it is genuinely unfussy.
  • Native mixes (hawthorn, blackthorn, field maple, hazel, dog rose) thrive on East Kent chalk and are almost entirely self-managing once established beyond the first two years.

Species comparison for new build gardens in East Kent

Fast-growing options (privacy in 2 to 4 years)

Photinia 'Red Robin' is one of the better choices for a new build wanting quick, tidy results. The red new growth is a bonus, it tolerates chalk and light shade, and it takes formal clipping well. Annual trimming in late summer keeps it at the right height and stimulates the red flush of new leaves. It will reach 1.5 to 2 m height within four to five years on reasonable soil.

Griselinia littoralis is exceptional on the coastal strip around Deal and Kingsdown where salt wind rules out many evergreens. It is not reliably hardy in hard Kent winters (late 2025 to early 2026 caused some browning), but it recovers readily and its very dense, apple-green foliage is excellent. Not the right choice for inland gardens where a hard frost will set it back significantly.

Privet (Ligustrum ovalifolium) is fast, forgiving, and extremely tolerant of East Kent's chalk soils. Its weakness is the trimming frequency: formal privet needs cutting two or three times a year to look neat. Semi-wild privet needs only once but becomes quite lax. The golden privet (Ligustrum ovalifolium 'Aureum') is no more difficult than the green form and considerably more ornamental.

Medium speed (privacy in 4 to 7 years, very low maintenance)

Beech (Fagus sylvatica) is our most consistent recommendation for new builds in the inland East Kent villages. It is slower than privet but not as slow as some guides suggest: on prepared chalk soil with a good first-season watering routine, you can expect 30 to 40 cm of growth per year from bare-root whips. Beech holds its russet dead leaves through winter, maintaining screening when it matters most. It needs cutting only once a year in late summer. Copper beech (the purple-leaved form) does exactly the same job with considerably more visual interest.

Hornbeam (Carpinus betulus) is closely comparable to beech in growth and maintenance but generally tolerates wetter, heavier soils somewhat better. If the new build site has any heavy clay or ground where water sits after rain, hornbeam over beech. On the well-drained chalk soils of Worth, Ash, Woodnesborough and Eastry, the two are interchangeable in practice.

Yew (Taxus baccata) is often dismissed as slow, but on a chalk soil with good drainage (which describes most of East Kent) yew grows faster than it gets credit for: 20 to 30 cm per year from a good start is achievable. It is essentially maintenance-free once established beyond the need for an annual trim, and it is the finest formal hedging plant in temperate gardens. Toxic berries mean it should not be planted where children or animals have unsupervised access, though the hazard is often overstated.

Informal or wildlife-priority options

Native hedge mix (hawthorn 40-50%, blackthorn 20%, field maple 15%, hazel 10%, dog rose 5-10%) is the right choice where the objective is wildlife and the garden backs onto open land or farmland, which describes many new build sites on the edges of Sandwich, Ash and Worth. A double-staggered row of bare-root whips planted in autumn will become an almost impenetrable native hedge within five years, very cheap to establish and requiring no input beyond a single annual trim in winter. For more, see our guide to planting a native hedge in Kent.

Holly (Ilex aquifolium) alone or mixed with other species is another excellent choice for its density, year-round interest (berries), tolerance of East Kent conditions, and thorny impenetrability. Slightly slower than the other options but needing cutting only once a year and essentially indestructible once established.

Timing: when to plant on a new build site

Bare-root whips are the most cost-effective way to plant a new hedge and are available from mid-November to late March. This is also the ideal planting window: the soil is usually moist and the plants are dormant, so establishment losses are low. Order in October so you are not waiting for stock in January.

Pot-grown plants are available year-round and can be planted at any time with appropriate watering, but they are considerably more expensive per plant and offer no real advantage for the species above. On a 20-metre boundary, the cost difference between bare-root and pot-grown can be significant.

The most common failure on new build sites is poor soil preparation. Developer topsoil is often thin, heavily compacted by machinery, and sometimes contaminated with builder's rubble. Always dig out a trench at least 45 cm deep before planting, incorporate compost, and check for rubble or membrane that will restrict roots. This single step makes more difference than species choice or aftercare.

For more on the mechanics of planting, see our hedge planting spacing guide and our newly planted hedge care guide.

Before planting: check with neighbours and your building regulations

Boundary hedges planted on new build sites can become a source of neighbour disputes once they mature, particularly over height and light. There are no legal restrictions on the height of a new garden hedge in the UK beyond the High Hedges legislation (Part 8 of the Anti-social Behaviour Act 2003), which covers hedges over 2 m that are forming a barrier of light or access. However, agreeing in advance with your neighbour about the intended height and maintenance schedule is a worthwhile conversation before planting anything that will eventually be 2 m tall. See our guide to hedge heights and neighbour law.

Summary: which species by situation

Coastal strip (Deal, Kingsdown, Sandwich Bay), salt wind: Griselinia as a first choice if winters are mild, otherwise native mix with hawthorn and blackthorn.

Inland chalk, village setting, formal look: Beech, copper beech, or photinia. Yew if you have five years' patience for the best formal hedge in England.

New build with developer soil, want low effort: Native mix or hornbeam. Both are tolerant of poor soil and require very little after the first two years.

Fast privacy on a tight budget: Bare-root privet or hawthorn. Neither is glamorous but both will fill a boundary quickly for under £1.50 per plant in quantity.
