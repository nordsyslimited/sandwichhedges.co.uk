How to cut an arched or curved hedge top

A flat top is easier to measure. An arch looks better, sheds rain, and ages more gracefully. Here is the template method we use in Kent gardens.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

A flat-topped hedge looks neat. An arched or curved top looks considered, and it does a practical job too: water runs off the crown rather than sitting in the flat top where it can cause fungal problems, and there is less structural weight on the top growth after heavy snowfall.

You do not need special equipment or a trained eye to cut one. You need a decent template and the discipline to follow it each year rather than drifting outwards with the new growth.

Before you start: safety

PPE: eye protection is essential: chippings travel further than you think. Ear defenders for any petrol or electric trimmer. Sturdy gloves.

Ladders: the majority of garden injuries involve ladders. A long-reach hedge trimmer reaches most arched tops from the ground; use one if you can. If a ladder is unavoidable, have someone footing it at all times and never overreach sideways.

Electric trimmers: keep the cable behind you and on the side away from the blades. Always use an RCD adaptor. Cordless tools remove this risk entirely.

Wasp nests: check the hedge from the side before you start, particularly around the crown of an arch, which is a favourite nesting spot. See our guide to wasp nests in hedges if you find one.

Nesting season: 1 March to 31 August

Under the UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting runs roughly from 1 March to 31 August. The safe shaping window for most hedges is September to February. If you need to do a shaping cut outside that window, check the hedge carefully for active nests before you touch anything.

What you need

For a simple curved top you need: a hedge trimmer (petrol, cordless or electric), two garden stakes or bamboo canes, a length of string, and some way to mark the crown point. For a repeatable profile on a long hedge run, a plywood or cardboard template cut to your target cross-section makes the job much faster.

A spirit level is useful for checking that the crown stays at the same height along the run. A 10-metre piece of string set between two levelled stakes at the crown height serves the same purpose at less cost.

Step 1: decide the profile

Work out three numbers before you start: the height of the flat sides (where the arch begins), the height of the crown (the highest point at the centre), and the width of the hedge at that crown.

For a gateway arch, the crown typically rises 20–40 cm above the side height. For a gentle rolling curve on a boundary hedge, 10–20 cm of rise looks more natural. If in doubt, err on the less dramatic side the first year: you can always take more off, but you cannot put it back.

Step 2: make a cutting guide

The simplest guide is two canes set at the side height on each end of the section you are cutting, with a third cane or a straight batten set horizontally at the crown height at the centre. Run string from each side cane up to the crown cane in the shape of your target arch. This gives you a visible guide to cut to.

For a longer hedge or a more precise profile, cut a template from plywood or stiff card: mark the centreline and the width of the hedge, then draw the curve between the side height and the crown. Cut it out, hold it against the hedge as you work along, and trim to match. A template takes half an hour to make and saves a lot of second-guessing on the day.

Step 3: cut the crown first

Start at the highest point, not at the ends. Trim the crown to the target height, then work outward in both directions, lowering the cut gradually to match the curve down to the side height. This way any mistakes in the early passes are corrected as you blend outward, rather than being locked in from the start.

For a tall hedge where the crown is above comfortable trimmer reach, a long-reach tool held at an angle cuts the curve in one pass. For a narrower hedge where you are working from both sides, cut one face of the arch before moving around to the other.

Step 4: blend to the sides

Unless you want a gateway arch that rises from a sharp shoulder, blend the transition from the curved top into the vertical sides with a gentle sweeping pass. The aim is to avoid a hard horizontal line at the point where the arch meets the sides, which makes the curve look glued on rather than grown.

Step 5: step back and check

Do this from at least 10 metres, and view the hedge from both ends as well as straight on. What looks fine from beside the hedge often looks lopsided from a distance. Correct any asymmetry with a light follow-up pass before clearing the clippings.

Maintaining the arch in future years

An arch will try to widen with each season's growth as you add new material to the outside of the curve. The discipline is to always cut back to the previous profile rather than following the new growth outward. A photograph from the end of the first properly shaped cut is the best reference for every subsequent year.

Set the crown height cane again each time and check it is level with the previous year before you start cutting. On a long hedge run, a string line at crown height along the full length keeps the profile consistent. Resist the temptation to add height: an arch that quietly gains 5 cm per year is one of the most common ways a formal hedge gradually gets away from you.

Species notes

Yew

The best species for arched and formal shaped tops. Dense and slow-growing, it holds a cut edge with more precision than any other UK hedge plant and tolerates hard shaping cuts well. East Kent gardens with established yew hedges are well suited to this technique. See our yew hedge care guide for timing and maintenance.

Hornbeam and beech

Both respond well to shaped cuts and grow back predictably. Beech in particular keeps its coppery winter foliage even on shaped tops, which makes the arch visible through the dormant season. Cut in late July or August. See our beech hedge care guide.

Privet

Workable but faster-growing than yew or beech. A privet arch needs a more disciplined annual cut to stop the crown migrating upward and outward. A light summer trim and a main cut in late August keeps it in check.

Laurel

Possible for a broad arch, but the large leaves leave a ragged cut edge on the top face. Use a hedge trimmer to rough out the shape, then finish the visible top surface with secateurs on any exposed cut leaves. See our laurel care guide.

Leylandii and other conifers

A curved or arched profile is possible only if you stay inside the live green growth at all times. Conifers will not regenerate from bare brown wood. The arch profile must be established while the hedge is still relatively young and the green zone still extends to the surface. On an already dense mature conifer hedge, a very shallow curve (10 cm or less of rise) that stays within the outer green zone is the only safe option. See our conifer trimming guide for the full detail.
