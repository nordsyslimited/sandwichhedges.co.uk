Flowering currant hedge care: pruning Ribes sanguineum

One of the easiest informal screens you can plant in a Kent garden. The timing of pruning is the one thing that trips people up.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Flowering currant (Ribes sanguineum) is one of those hedging plants that almost looks after itself. Once established in a sheltered or semi-exposed spot, it puts on a reliably good show every spring, feeds the first bumblebees of the year, and needs very little from you. Except for one thing: you have to cut it at the right time.

Cut it in autumn like most hedges and you remove next year's flowers before they've had a chance to open. Cut it in late April or May, straight after it's finished blooming, and you're set for another year. That's the main message here.

Before you start: safety and nesting season

Nesting season: 1 March to 31 August

Ribes sanguineum flowers in March and April, right at the start of the UK nesting season. By the time you're ready to prune in late April or May, birds may well have nested in the plant. Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, damaging or destroying an active bird's nest is a criminal offence. Always check the plant thoroughly before you cut: look from below into the interior, move carefully around the outside. If you see a nest with eggs or young, leave it until the birds have fledged. It will only be another few weeks.

Before you start trimming

  • Wear eye protection: even hand-pruning sends small twigs and debris towards your face.
  • Sturdy gloves: ribes isn't thorny but the stems are rough and sappy.
  • If using an electric trimmer: use an RCD adaptor, keep the cable away from the cutting line, and never work in rain.
  • Prefer a long-reach or pole-mounted trimmer over a ladder for any stems above shoulder height. If a ladder is unavoidable, have someone at the foot of it.
  • Check for wasp nests in the interior of the plant before committing to a cut, particularly in mid to late summer.

What is flowering currant?

Ribes sanguineum is a deciduous shrub native to western North America, now naturalised across the UK and a staple of British cottage and country gardens. In East Kent you'll find it in sheltered town gardens in Sandwich and Deal, along cottage borders in Worth, Ash and Woodnesborough, and occasionally used as an informal boundary screen in rural gardens around Eastry.

It grows quickly when young, 40-60cm per year in reasonable soil, and will reach 2-2.5 metres in three or four years if left unpruned. The arching stems carry clusters of deep pink, crimson or white flowers in March and April, usually before the leaves are fully out. The flowers are followed by small, waxy, blue-black berries in summer.

The scent of the leaves is divisive: some people like it, others find it musty. It is strong when you brush against the plant or cut it. Worth knowing before you plant a large hedge of it near a doorway.

Common varieties

'King Edward VII' is the most widely planted UK variety: compact, reaches about 1.5m, deep crimson-red flowers. Good for a neater screen where space is limited.

'Pulborough Scarlet' grows larger, to 2.5m or more, and has very deep red flowers that open slightly earlier. Vigorous and reliable in coastal conditions.

'White Icicle' is the main white-flowered form. Flowers are smaller than the red varieties and slightly later. Useful where you want a lighter effect or don't want the plant to dominate a border.

'Brocklebankii' has golden-yellow foliage and pink flowers. Slower and more compact, to about 1.2m. Useful as a bright accent rather than a dense screen.

When to prune: the golden rule

Ribes sanguineum flowers on wood produced in the previous growing season. This is the fact that determines everything about pruning timing:

  • If you cut in autumn or winter, you remove the flower buds already forming on this year's growth. No flowers next spring.
  • If you cut immediately after flowering (late April to May), the plant uses the whole summer to produce new growth. That new growth carries next spring's flowers.

The ideal window is the two to three weeks after the last flowers drop and before the plant is in full leaf. You can still see the shape clearly, the plant has energy to respond, and you have the summer ahead of it for recovery.

How much to cut

Annual maintenance pruning

For a plant you're managing as an informal screen, remove roughly one-third of the oldest stems every year, cutting them back to near the base. This keeps the plant from becoming a tangle of old, unproductive wood at the centre. Also shorten the flowered stems of the remaining shoots by about a third to encourage branching. The aim is an open, vase-shaped structure with a mix of young and middle-aged stems.

This is best done with loppers and secateurs rather than a hedge trimmer. A trimmer shears the branch tips and works against the natural arching habit, producing a congested ball of stubbed stems rather than the open, flowing shape the plant grows naturally.

Keeping a fixed height and width

If you need the plant to stay within a defined space (for example, a narrow side passage in Sandwich or a small courtyard garden in Deal), a harder overall cut immediately after flowering is fine. Take it back to your target height and width. The plant will bounce back. Do this every year and it becomes routine.

Renovation of a very overgrown plant

An old, leggy, neglected flowering currant can be hard-pruned to about 30-40cm from the ground in late winter (February) before it comes into growth. You lose that year's flowers but the plant regrows strongly. Alternatively, use the one-third-per-year renovation approach over three growing seasons: less drastic, some flowers preserved each year, same end result of a rejuvenated framework.

Flowering currant and the nesting season

Because the ideal pruning window (late April to May) falls within the UK nesting season (1 March to 31 August), you should inspect the plant before you cut. Ribes sanguineum is not the densest nesting cover, but wrens, dunnocks and robins do nest in established plants. If you find an active nest, postpone cutting until the young have fledged. The wait is usually two to three weeks at most, and the plant will not suffer.

For more guidance on identifying active nests and knowing when it is safe to proceed, see our guide to checking hedges for birds' nests before cutting.

Flowering currant and wildlife

The flowers appearing in March and April are a significant early season resource for bumblebee queens emerging from hibernation, and for early solitary bees. At a time when most garden plants are not yet in flower, a flowering currant in full bloom can be genuinely valuable, particularly in gardens near the coast around Sandwich Bay and Deal where winters are mild and early insects are active from late February.

The berries that follow in summer are eaten by blackbirds and other thrushes. The plant also provides nesting cover and dense foraging habitat. For a garden trying to support wildlife through the year, flowering currant earns its place at the spring end of the calendar when other options are few.

Soil, position and establishment

Flowering currant is not fussy. It grows in most reasonably drained soils, including the chalky soils common around Ash and Woodnesborough and the heavier clay found in lower-lying gardens near Eastry. It prefers full sun or partial shade. A sheltered position suits it better than a fully exposed coastal site, though 'Pulborough Scarlet' and 'King Edward VII' cope reasonably well with salt wind if there is some windbreak nearby.

For the first two years, water in dry spells and mulch around the base in autumn to retain moisture. Once established, it largely looks after itself.

Common problems

No flowers: Almost always a pruning timing issue. Cutting in autumn or winter removes the flower buds. Switch to post-flowering pruning and you'll have flowers again within a year.

Leggy and bare at the base: Old plants that haven't been pruned regularly produce all their growth at the top. A phased renovation cut (one-third of oldest stems per year) restores the base within two to three seasons.

Leaf spots or rust: Ribes can develop fungal leaf spots, particularly in wet summers. Rarely serious enough to need treatment. Improve air circulation by thinning out congested stems. Remove and dispose of affected leaves rather than composting them.

Aphids: Currant blister aphid causes puckered red or yellow blisters on the leaves in spring. Looks alarming but rarely affects plant health or flowering. The aphids move to other hosts in summer and the plant recovers. Treat only if the infestation is very heavy and you want to protect a newly planted specimen.
