Flowering currant (Ribes sanguineum) as a hedge: pruning timing and care

Flowering currant is a Group 1 flowering shrub: prune it after the flowers go over, not before, or you clip off every bud for the following year. Get the timing right and it is an easy, informal boundary plant with early blossom and genuine bird value. Here is the full annual care calendar for East Kent gardens.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Flowering currant (Ribes sanguineum) is one of the shrubs that gets cut at the wrong time more often than almost any other. People clip it in autumn when they are doing the rest of the hedges, or cut it hard in February to tidy it up, and then wonder why it flowered poorly in spring. The answer is always the same: the flowers come on wood grown the previous summer, so cutting in autumn or winter removes all the buds before they can open.

Once you understand the pruning principle, Ribes is a forgiving, fast-growing plant that requires very little attention. It is native to western North America rather than Britain, but it has naturalised widely and its early pink flowers in March and April are a useful nectar source for queen bumblebees and early butterflies. In East Kent gardens, particularly the town boundaries and garden divisions in Sandwich and Deal, it turns up frequently as an informal screening plant.

Before you start: safety

  • Eye protection: safety glasses or goggles before operating any trimmer.
  • Gloves: Ribes sanguineum has no thorns, but stiff stems at the base can scratch.
  • Ear defenders: for petrol hedge trimmers.
  • RCD adaptor: always, for any corded electric tool, without exception.
  • Working height: if you need a ladder to reach the top of a tall, mature plant, use a spotter and do not overreach sideways.

Nesting season warning: late April and May pruning

The main pruning window for flowering currant (after flowering, late April to May) falls within the UK nesting season, 1 March to 31 August. Ribes's dense leafy structure in late spring is actively used by nesting birds, particularly chaffinch, dunnock and robin. Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 it is an offence to damage or destroy an active nest.

Before cutting: part the branches and look carefully for a nest. If you find one with eggs or young, stop, mark the location, and come back in three to four weeks. After the nest is empty, cut as normal. Do not attempt to move or remove the nest.

Annual care calendar

January and February: do nothing

Resist the urge to cut. January and February are when most garden tidy-ups happen, but flowering currant's flower buds are already formed on the previous year's wood and cutting now removes the entire spring display. The plant may look straggly and bare, but leave it alone. If there are any dead or diseased stems, remove those cleanly with loppers.

March and April: enjoy the flowers, keep an eye on growth

Flowering currant opens from mid-March in most East Kent gardens, occasionally as early as the first week of March in the sheltered town gardens of Sandwich and Deal after a mild winter. The flowers are deep pink to red on most common cultivars, hanging in racemes 7 to 10 cm long. The smell is distinctive: a sharp, slightly resinous scent that divides opinion. The flowers are visited by queen bumblebees, which emerge early and need accessible nectar.

By late April the flowers are going over and dropping. This is your cue.

Late April and May: main pruning

As soon as the last flowers have dropped, this is the window for pruning. The plant now has most of the growing season ahead of it to produce new wood, and that new wood is exactly what will carry next year's flowers.

Check carefully for nests before cutting (see the warning box above). Then proceed:

  1. Remove one-third of the oldest, thickest stems at ground level entirely. These are the ones with the roughest, most ridged bark, usually grey-brown rather than the reddish colour of the newer wood. Removing old stems to the base keeps the plant producing vigorous new growth from below and prevents it becoming a congested tangle of old wood that flowers less well each year.
  2. Reduce the remaining stems by about a third of their length, cutting just above an outward-facing bud. This controls the overall height and spread.
  3. Remove any crossing, damaged or inward-facing stems.
  4. On a hedge or screen, a light all-round trim with shears is fine at this point to tighten the shape, provided you are not cutting back into bare old wood without buds.

For a very overgrown or neglected plant, cut the whole thing back to within 30 to 45 cm of the ground in late April or May. It will push back strongly from the base in the same season and be back to a reasonable height within a year.

June and July: main growth

This is when flowering currant puts on the majority of its annual growth. New shoots extend quickly in warm weather, particularly in the long June days. No action is needed unless the plant is growing out into a path or driveway. A light tidy pass in July is fine if necessary, but avoid cutting back into the new growth too hard, as this reduces the wood that will carry flowers next spring.

Dark blue-black berries appear from June onwards, taken readily by blackbirds, house sparrows and thrushes. The berries are edible but taste strongly resinous and are not worth picking unless you specifically enjoy that flavour.

August and September: light tidying only

August is the end of the nesting season. From 1 September, the restriction on cutting lifts. A light shape tidy in late August or September is fine: trim back any shoots that have grown out of the desired shape. Avoid a hard cut in September, which can stimulate soft new growth that then gets caught by early frosts in Kent's October and November.

October and November: leaf drop and winter appearance

Flowering currant is fully deciduous. Leaves fall from October, leaving a fairly uninteresting tangle of bare stems for winter. The autumn leaf colour is unremarkable, mostly yellow-green. This is the least attractive season for this plant, which is worth considering if your boundary needs winter screening. For year-round coverage, combine Ribes sanguineum with an evergreen species such as viburnum tinus or escallonia rather than relying on flowering currant alone.

November to February: dormant pruning of dead wood only

Remove any wood that died during the year, cutting cleanly back to healthy growth. Do not remove live, healthy stems in this window: those stems are carrying next year's flower buds.

Growing conditions in East Kent

Ribes sanguineum is unfussy about soil. It grows well on the chalk and flint soils common in gardens around Sandwich and Woodnesborough, on the heavier loams of Worth and Ash, and in the sandier soil on the east side of the coast near Sandwich Bay. It tolerates partial shade as well as full sun, which makes it useful for north-facing garden boundaries where many flowering shrubs perform poorly.

It has moderate wind tolerance. In the exposed parts of Sandwich Bay, where salt-laden wind can kill less hardy shrubs, flowering currant survives but may not look its best. In the more sheltered walled gardens and town plots of Sandwich and Deal, it is a reliable and low-maintenance choice for an informal flowering screen.

Cultivar note

The standard species Ribes sanguineum has deep pink to red flowers and is the most commonly planted form. 'White Icicle' (white flowers) and 'King Edward VII' (deep crimson) are also widely available from UK nurseries. For a hedge or screen, the standard species or 'King Edward VII' tend to be the most vigorous and require least attention.
