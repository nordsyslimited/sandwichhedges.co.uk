May hedge care in Kent: peak nesting month

May is the month when more birds are actively nesting in East Kent hedges than at any other point in the year. Here is what that means in practice, and how to use the month well without touching the clippers.

Updated September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Nesting season: peak activity in May

The UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. The nesting season runs roughly 1 March to 31 August. In May, most established hedges in East Kent will contain at least one active nest. Blackbirds and robins may be on a second brood, house sparrows are at peak nesting activity, and pigeons are frequently mid-brooding. Treat May as a no-cut month for the vast majority of hedges.

May is frustrating if you are used to keeping a tidy garden. The hedge is growing fast, the new shoots are stretching out in all directions, and the whole boundary looks a bit wild. Resist the urge. The growth that looks untidy in May is the growth that becomes well-anchored, tight new wood when you cut it in September. Cutting it now removes that potential, stresses the plant, and almost certainly disturbs nesting birds.

Here is how to use May well, step by step.

Step 1: check before you go near the hedge for any reason

Even if you are just going near the hedge base to weed, mulch or water, take two minutes to check for nesting birds first. Stand back, watch, and look for birds flying in and out of the same area. In May, blackbirds are so well established in East Kent privet, hawthorn and laurel hedges that you may hear alarm calls before you even approach. That is enough reason to give the hedge a wider berth for the time being.

Our full guide on checking a hedge for birds' nests covers exactly what to look for and what counts as evidence of active nesting.

Step 2: the leylandii and conifer exception

Leylandii, thuja, Lawson cypress and other conifers are the one category where a May trim is often appropriate, provided the nest check comes first.

Before you start on leylandii: safety and nest checks

  • Check for nests: spend two to three minutes watching before you start. Look for birds flying into the hedge, check from both sides and from underneath. Leylandii provides less nesting cover than broadleaf, but it does get used, particularly by sparrows and pigeons.
  • Stay inside the green: never cut into brown wood on a conifer. The hedge will not regrow from bare, brown stems. Take a little off the green tips only.
  • Eye protection: safety glasses. Conifer clippings flick at speed.
  • Ear defenders: for any powered trimmer session lasting more than a few minutes.
  • RCD adaptor: essential for corded electric trimmers, not optional.
  • Pole trimmer over a ladder: a long-reach pole trimmer is safer for the top of a tall hedge. If a ladder is unavoidable, have someone foot it and never overreach.

The first trim of the year for leylandii is typically in April or May. You are not taking the hedge down, just taking off the tips of new growth from the previous season to keep the shape tight. A September trim is still needed for the final formal cut. If you are unsure about the process for leylandii specifically, our leylandii guide covers the detail, including the one-third rule and why you cannot reduce the height hard without permanent brown patches.

A useful video on leylandii training over successive years, published in September 2025, is available at NEW Leylandii Hedge Trimming and Training, Year 3, Trust the Process. It covers the gradual, patient approach that works for new conifer hedges, which is the right mindset for a May trim: light, incremental, not trying to correct everything in one go.

Step 3: feed if you have not already

If March and April passed without feeding, May is still useful for it. A slow-release granular fertiliser applied before the hedge's main flush of growth in late May and June still makes a difference. In the clay and loam soils around Woodnesborough and Eastry, an annual feed in spring is the single biggest factor in whether a hedge looks dense and healthy by August or thin and yellow.

Step 4: water new hedges thoroughly

Any hedge planted in the last eighteen months needs attention in May. The soil around Sandwich Bay and the coastal strip is sandy and drains quickly. New hedges that look healthy in April can suffer genuine drought stress in May if there is a two-week dry spell, which East Kent gets most years. Water deeply and slowly at the base rather than lightly over the top: you want the water to reach the root ball, not just the surface. Willow, hornbeam and hawthorn bare-root plantings are particularly vulnerable in their first growing season.

Step 5: check box hedging for box tree moth

May is the main emergence month for the box tree moth (Cydalima perspectalis) second generation in the UK. If you have box hedging anywhere in your garden, check carefully for the pale, webbed larvae and green frass. Infestations in May can strip a box hedge bare within a week if left unchecked. See our box tree moth guide for identification and treatment options.

Step 6: cut back weeds at the hedge base

Grass, bindweed and nettles competing at the hedge base can be dealt with in May without disturbing the hedge itself. Keep a clear, weed-free strip along the base of the hedge where your mulch sits. This is particularly important for newly planted hedges, where grass competition in year one or two can reduce growth rates significantly. For bindweed and brambles that have established themselves in the hedge structure, our weeds at the hedge base guide has the step-by-step approach.

Step 7: book your autumn trimming now

September and October are the busiest months for professional hedge cutters across East Kent. If you want a hedge trimmed by a professional in the main window, May is the time to book it, not August. By the time late August comes around, diaries fill in days.
