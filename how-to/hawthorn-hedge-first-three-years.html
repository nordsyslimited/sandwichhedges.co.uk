Hawthorn hedge: first three years

The decisions that shape a hawthorn hedge for the next thirty years are made in the first three. Here is the decision guide: how hard to cut, when to cut and what to do when things go wrong.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Hawthorn (Crataegus monogyna) is the most common hedging plant in the British countryside, and the reason is straightforward: it grows fast, it establishes reliably, it handles almost any soil including the chalk downland and heavier clays of East Kent, and it ends up with serious wildlife value. The berries in autumn draw fieldfares across from the Continent. Nesting birds go in in March and are still at it in July. A good hawthorn hedge along the edge of a garden in Ash or Eastry is not just a boundary: it is a habitat.

Getting there requires a few specific decisions in the first three years. The decisions are not complicated, but they are consequential, and the choices that produce a dense, wildlife-rich hedge are counter-intuitive if you are used to other hedging species.

Nesting season: 1 March to 31 August

Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 it is an offence to damage or destroy a nest that is in active use. Hawthorn is one of Britain's most popular nesting shrubs. This means that for most of the first growing season, your new hedge cannot be touched without checking carefully first. The safe cutting window for young hawthorn is September to late February. See our wildlife-friendly trimming guide for more detail.

Decision 1: how hard do I cut it at planting?

This is the foundational decision and it depends on whether you planted bare-root or container-grown hawthorn.

Bare-root hawthorn (planted November to March): cut it back hard. The traditional instruction is to reduce the plant by half to two-thirds at planting, so a 60-cm whip comes down to 20 to 30 cm. This is called planting back and it looks extreme. Do it anyway. Hawthorn that is not planted back typically develops a few strong vertical shoots with minimal branching at the base, and that base will stay open and gappy for years. The hard planting cut forces the plant to send out multiple shoots from low down, and those low shoots become the dense, interlocking base that makes hawthorn the stockproof barrier it is famous for.

Container-grown hawthorn (planted any time, but ideally spring or autumn): lighter cut. Remove any crossing, damaged or very untidy shoots with secateurs, but do not hard cut the whole plant back. The plant has an established root system in a known soil, and cutting it back hard at planting sets it back without the same compensatory benefit as with bare-root. Through year one, focus on watering during dry spells and mulching.

Decision 2: do I cut it in year one, or leave it alone?

For bare-root hawthorn that received the planting cut: by late August of year one, the plant will have sent out multiple new shoots from the stumps of the planting cut. If the hedge is clearly empty of nests, you can take a light pass over the sides with hedge shears or a trimmer, taking off no more than a third of the new growth. Leave the top alone entirely. This is optional but beneficial if the new growth has been vigorous: the light trim stimulates more lateral branching and helps thicken the base earlier. If the summer has been dry or the hedge looks stressed, skip it and water instead.

For container-grown hawthorn: leave it completely alone in year one. Let it establish.

Decision 3: what do I do if the base is gappy?

Gaps at the base in year one are not unusual if the planting cut was done correctly: the plant was cut back hard and is in the process of sending out new growth. Gaps that persist through the whole of year two, or gaps that exist on a hedge that did NOT receive the planting cut, are a different matter.

If your hawthorn hedge is thin and open at the base by the end of year two, the most reliable fix is a renovation cut in late winter of year three: take the whole hedge down to 30 to 40 cm and let it start again. This is severe. The hedge will look like a row of sticks for the rest of that year. But hawthorn recovers exceptionally fast, and the regrowth from a hard cut on an established root system will be dense, vigorous and much more uniform than the original gappy hedge. By the end of year four, it will be a solid hedge. This is the same technique used to renovate overgrown hawthorn hedgerows that have gone wild, applied at a smaller scale.

Year 2: building the density

For bare-root hawthorn (year two, or container-grown in year two or three): clip the sides two or three times during the growing season, with the same nesting-check discipline each time. A good schedule is a clip in late June (nests checked and cleared), one in late August and a final tidy in late September. Hold the top 10 to 20 cm below the final target height: hawthorn grows 30 to 50 cm per year in good conditions, so you have headroom. Each clip of the sides stimulates the dense lateral branching that makes the face solid. By the end of year two, the hedge should feel like a barrier when you walk up to it.

Year 3: letting it become what it is

By year three, a correctly managed hawthorn hedge is approaching its maintenance phase. Let the top reach its target height this year. Continue clipping the sides twice a season. The characteristic thorny, interlocking face that gives hawthorn its stockproof quality will be developing, and the hedge will start to hold its profile between clips. If you have a gap in the boundary, plant in a new bare-root whip in the winter of year three and hard-cut it at planting: a correctly planted whip will catch up with a two-year-old hedge in a season.

By the end of year three, the first berries may appear. These will draw birds in autumn and winter, and the nesting that follows in spring will be more intense than in a younger hedge. The wildlife value of a hawthorn hedge compounds over time: a five-year hedge in Ash or Woodnesborough is a genuinely productive habitat, not just a garden boundary.

Common questions

Should I cut a hawthorn hedge back hard when I plant it?

Yes, for bare-root hawthorn whips. The planting cut, reducing the plant by half to two-thirds, stimulates dense base growth and is not optional. Without it, the hedge grows tall but stays open at the base. For container-grown hawthorn, a lighter cut is appropriate.

Can I trim a hawthorn hedge in spring or early summer?

Only if you check carefully for active nests first. Hawthorn is one of the most popular nesting shrubs in Britain. The Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to damage a nest in active use. Nesting runs roughly 1 March to 31 August. The safe window is September to late February.

What do I do if my new hawthorn hedge has gaps at the base?

If the base is gappy and the planting cut was not done, the fix in year two is a hard cut, taking the whole hedge down to 30 to 40 cm in late winter. This is severe but hawthorn recovers fast and the resulting regrowth will be dense. Done in year two, the hedge will be in reasonable shape by year four.

How quickly does a hawthorn hedge establish?

Hawthorn is one of the fastest-establishing native hedging plants. A well-planted and correctly cut hawthorn hedge will typically be a presentable screen by the end of year three. By year five it will be a proper hedge with significant wildlife value. On good soils in East Kent, hawthorn grows 30 to 50 cm per year once established.
