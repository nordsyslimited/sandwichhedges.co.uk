Managing hedge width: how to reduce an overgrown hedge in stages

A hedge that has gained half a metre or more of unwanted depth is not necessarily a lost cause. Most species tolerate a staged width reduction very well, provided you give each side time to recover before cutting the other. Here is how to plan it.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Before you start: a quick checklist

  • Measure the current width of the hedge at its widest point.
  • Decide on the target width: 50 to 75 cm is enough for most formal hedges.
  • Identify the hedge species (this affects how aggressively you can cut).
  • Check which side of the hedge is "yours" to cut, and whether the other side belongs to a neighbour.
  • Confirm you are outside the nesting season (1 March to 31 August) before making any hard cuts.
  • Plan for 2 to 3 years if the reduction is more than 30 to 40 cm per side.
  • Arrange access to clear the cuttings from beneath the hedge after each stage.

Nesting season: width reduction cuts must be done September to February

Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, disturbing or destroying an active bird's nest is an offence. Nesting season runs from 1 March to 31 August. Any hard cut into old wood, including width reduction cuts, should be carried out between September and February. If you are unsure whether a hedge is being used for nesting outside those dates, check carefully before cutting.

Safety: working at the base of a wide hedge

Reaching into or through a wide overgrown hedge often means working in confined conditions with woody stems at face height. Wear eye protection (flying debris from power tools and from stems springing back is a genuine risk), sturdy gloves, and long sleeves. If you are using a pole hedge trimmer or a reciprocating saw on thick stems, ear protection is also advisable. Keep bystanders clear of the working area on both sides of the hedge.

Know your species first

The staged approach to width reduction applies differently depending on whether you have a deciduous hedge, an evergreen broadleaved hedge or a conifer hedge. The distinction matters because conifers cannot regenerate from old brown wood, which limits how far back you can cut them.

Species group Tolerance of hard width cut Examples
Deciduous High: regenerates readily from old wood Privet, beech, hornbeam, hawthorn, field maple, blackthorn
Evergreen broadleaved Good: regrows from old wood, though slower than deciduous Laurel, holly, yew, box, Griselinia, Viburnum tinus
Conifer Limited: will not regrow from old brown wood Leylandii, Cupressus, Thuja, Leyland cypress

Yew (Taxus baccata) is classed with the evergreen broadleaved group here because, unlike other conifers, it can and does regenerate from old wood when cut. It is slower to respond than deciduous species but is not in the "do not cut into old wood" category that applies to Leylandii and Cupressus.

The staged width-reduction plan

The principle behind staged width reduction is straightforward: at any given time, the hedge should have at least one side with green foliage cover on it. That foliage-covered side is producing the food that sustains the plant while the cut side recovers. Cutting both sides back to bare wood simultaneously removes that safety net and significantly increases the risk of the hedge dying or becoming very sparse.

Year one: cut one side back hard

In the first autumn or winter (September to February), cut one side of the hedge back to your target depth, or as close to it as the green growth allows. For a deciduous or evergreen broadleaved species, cutting into old wood is fine, though you should aim to leave some live buds or branchlets if any are visible at the target depth, as these speed up regrowth. Leave the other side alone, or give it only a light maintenance trim to keep it tidy.

After the cut, clear the cuttings, mulch the base if it is bare and water in dry spells in the following growing season. By late summer of year one the cut side should be showing significant new growth.

Year two: cut the second side back hard

In the following autumn or winter, once the first side has had a full growing season to recover and is visibly producing new foliage, cut the second side back to the same target depth. By this point the first side is back in leaf and can support the plant while the second side recovers.

For most deciduous hedges and laurel hedges in East Kent's growing conditions, two years is usually enough for a full staged width reduction of up to 50 cm per side. Yew and box are slower and may benefit from a third year before both sides are fully back to target depth.

Year three and beyond: maintain at the new width

Once both sides have been reduced and the hedge has recovered, maintain it at the new width in the normal way. The annual or twice-yearly maintenance trim should now be removing only the new season's growth, keeping the hedge at the target depth without the need for any further hard cutting.

What about the top?

Tackling width and height simultaneously is possible but doubles the stress on the hedge. If the hedge is significantly oversized in both dimensions, it is usually better to deal with one at a time. Reduce the width first over years one and two; once the hedge has recovered, the following year is the right time to address a significant height reduction if one is needed.

If the hedge only needs a modest height reduction (a few centimetres rather than a major cut), that can go alongside the year one or year two width cuts without much additional risk on a healthy deciduous hedge. Use judgement based on how healthy and dense the hedge looks before you start.

Conifers: a different conversation

If your overgrown hedge is leylandii, Cupressus or another conifer, the staged approach applies only to the depth of live green growth on the stems. If the green growth on each side extends 20 cm from the outer surface, you can reduce by up to 20 cm per side; no more. Cutting beyond the live green zone into old brown wood will not result in regrowth on most conifers.

If the hedge is significantly wider than the green zone allows you to reduce, the realistic options are to maintain it at a larger width than you would ideally like, or to have it removed and replaced with a species (privet, beech, hornbeam or yew) that will tolerate a harder renovation in the future.
