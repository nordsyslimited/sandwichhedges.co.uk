Dog urine damage to hedge bases: how to diagnose it and what to do

Brown patches at the base of a leylandii or privet hedge are one of those problems that gets worse every year unless something changes. Before you reach for the replacement plants, work through the diagnosis: is it actually dog urine, and if so, what can realistically be done about it?

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

What dog urine does to a hedge

Dog urine contains high concentrations of nitrogen and ammonia. In small amounts, nitrogen can act as a fertiliser. Repeated applications in the same spot, at the concentrations dogs produce, overwhelm a plant's ability to process it and cause chemical burn to foliage and, over time, to surface roots. The damage is essentially the same mechanism as over-fertilising: the soil concentration becomes toxic.

Leylandii is the species we see most affected in East Kent. Its tight, dense base structure means urine pools against the stems rather than dispersing into the soil, and the inner foliage that gets repeatedly wetted eventually dies and does not regenerate. Privet, box and laurel also show up regularly. Beech and hornbeam, with their more open lower stems, tend to cope better.

Diagnosing the cause: four questions to work through

1. Where is the damage? Dog urine damage is confined to the base of the hedge, typically within the lowest 30 to 40 cm. If the browning or die-back is higher up, or scattered randomly through the hedge at different heights, the cause is almost certainly something else: drought stress, disease, root problems or pest infestation.

2. Is it in the same spot or two spots, rather than spread evenly? Dogs are creatures of habit and return to the same locations. Urine damage concentrates in one or two consistent spots, often near a gap in the hedge, a gate post, or a corner that a dog on a lead is likely to be walked past. Damage spread evenly along the full base is more consistent with drought, waterlogging or a soil problem.

3. Is there a visible tide-mark at a consistent height? The transition from healthy foliage to brown is often quite sharp, at the height where urine contacts the stems. This horizontal band at 15 to 30 cm, consistent from spot to spot, is distinctive. A disease like honey fungus tends to progress downward from branches rather than upward from the base in a horizontal line.

4. Does the damage worsen in dry spells? Without rainfall to dilute urine, the salt and nitrogen concentration in the soil rises sharply. If the browning is worse in summer and improves after a wet autumn, that is strong evidence for urine rather than fungal disease, which typically behaves the opposite way.

If you have answered yes to the above, the cause is almost certainly dog urine. If the damage is not in the classic base-only pattern or does not correlate with dryness, check our hedge health troubleshooting guide for other likely causes including honey fungus and vine weevil.

What to do immediately

If you can catch the event, flush the area with a generous amount of water as soon as possible. Diluting the urine before the plant absorbs it makes a real difference, particularly in dry weather. This is only practical if you see it happen or are told promptly, which is often the challenge with a neighbour's dog.

A temporary physical barrier, even a low decorative fence or a row of close-planted spiny shrubs at the base, will often cause a dog to move to a different spot. Dogs are not especially determined about where they go; a mild inconvenience is usually enough. Citrus-based deterrent sprays can reinforce this but need reapplying after rain. David Domoney, a UK gardening presenter and RHS ambassador with over 300,000 subscribers on YouTube, has covered practical deterrent options in accessible video format for those who want to see techniques demonstrated.

Repairing the damage

For species that regenerate from old wood (privet, beech, yew, hornbeam), there is a reasonable chance the plant will push new growth from the affected area once the cause is removed. Clear the dead foliage, address the urine problem, and give it a season. Feed with a balanced slow-release fertiliser in spring once things have stabilised.

Leylandii is less forgiving. It does not shoot from brown wood, so a section that has gone completely dry and brittle will stay brown. If the dead section is small (30 cm or less), adjacent green growth will usually close the gap over two or three seasons as the hedge fills out. For a larger section, the options are: cut out the dead material and plant a new leylandii in the gap, accepting that it will take a few years to match the height; or replace the whole affected section with a species that handles this kind of base damage better.

Longer-term prevention

The most effective interventions are physical. A simple low trellis or decorative panel at the base of the affected section, set 20 to 30 cm out from the hedge, is usually enough to move a dog's chosen spot elsewhere. Dense planting at the base using thorny or prickly species (berberis, pyracantha, Rosa rugosa) achieves the same result over time and looks better than a barrier. For a footpath-adjacent front garden hedge in Sandwich, Deal or Worth, where a steady succession of dogs are walked past on leads, a combination of low planting and deterrent spray gives the most consistent protection.

Where a specific neighbour's dog is responsible, a direct conversation explaining the damage is usually worth having. Most dog owners are unaware of the cumulative effect and will make the effort to walk their dog to a different spot if they understand what is happening.
