Bay laurel hedge care: bay vs cherry laurel vs privet

All three are popular choices for a formal green hedge in Kent gardens, but they behave very differently once they are in the ground. Here is how they compare on growth rate, pruning method, hardiness and long-term appearance.

Updated September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

The three plants at a glance

Bay laurel (Laurus nobilis), cherry laurel (Prunus laurocerasus) and privet (Ligustrum ovalifolium) are all widely grown as formal hedges in East Kent gardens. They look superficially similar from a distance: large-leaved, dark green, dense enough for privacy. But the experience of growing them is quite different, and so is the result.

Bay laurel: growing and care

What it is

Bay laurel (Laurus nobilis) is a Mediterranean evergreen shrub or small tree grown in British gardens for its aromatic foliage, its tolerance of clipping, and its usefulness as a culinary herb. Clipped into a formal hedge or trained as a standard, it is attractive and distinctive. It grows more slowly than cherry laurel or privet, which reduces maintenance but means it takes longer to establish a screen.

Where bay laurel grows best in East Kent

Bay is rated RHS H4, meaning it should survive down to about -10°C in a sheltered position, but prolonged hard frost will damage or kill it. In practice, East Kent's mild coastal climate suits bay well. Gardens in Deal, Sandwich Bay and along the coast rarely experience the hard frost events that damage bay in more inland parts of the UK. In a sheltered walled garden or against a south-facing fence, bay is reliably evergreen through winter.

Exposed, north-facing positions in Worth, Ash or Woodnesborough are higher risk, particularly in an unusually cold winter. If your garden is frost-prone or open to a north or east wind, cherry laurel is a safer choice for a permanent formal hedge. If you want bay for its culinary and aromatic qualities, plant it in a pot so it can be moved under cover in a severe winter.

Bay grows well on the well-drained chalk soils of coastal East Kent. It dislikes heavy, waterlogged clay; if your soil is heavy, improve drainage before planting or choose a different species.

Planting bay laurel

Plant in spring or early autumn. Space plants 45 to 60 cm apart for a hedge, closer if you want faster coverage. Bay tolerates partial shade but grows more densely in full sun. Add grit or sharp sand to heavy soils at planting, and work in some general fertiliser. Water regularly through the first season; once established, bay is fairly drought-tolerant on chalk.

Pruning bay laurel

The key rule with bay is to use secateurs rather than hedge shears. Bay's large leaves are cut in half by shears and the cut edges turn brown, which looks unsightly on a formal hedge. Patient work with bypass secateurs gives a much cleaner result: you cut between leaves rather than through them, leaving the hedge looking healthy and whole.

Prune once a year, in late spring (May to early June), once the risk of hard frost has passed and before the plant puts on significant summer growth. Remove wayward shoots and maintain the shape. Avoid pruning in autumn; late-season growth produced in response to cutting will be soft and vulnerable to frost damage.

Before you start

  • Use bypass secateurs, not shears: shears cut through the large bay leaves and leave brown edges. Secateurs take longer but give a much cleaner result.
  • Gloves: bay leaves and stems release oils that can irritate sensitive skin. Light gloves are advisable for extended pruning sessions.
  • Eye protection: when pruning dense growth, twigs and stems flick back. Safety glasses are worth wearing.
  • Wasp nests: check inside the plant before starting. Bay provides good nesting cover.
  • Working at height: tall bay hedges may require step platforms or a long-reach trimmer. Use a stable platform rather than a leaning ladder for two-handed secateur work. See our long-reach trimmer guide.

Nesting season: 1 March to 31 August

Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Bay laurel's dense evergreen cover is attractive to nesting birds. The nesting season runs roughly 1 March to 31 August. Timing bay's main prune to late spring (May or June) means checking carefully for active nests before you start. The safest approach is to check thoroughly first and postpone if you find activity.

Cherry laurel: the fast alternative

Cherry laurel (Prunus laurocerasus) is the most widely planted formal hedge in East Kent, and for good reason. It establishes fast, handles most soils including heavy clay, tolerates shade, and is fully hardy. Where bay requires secateurs and patience, cherry laurel is more forgiving: you can use powered hedge trimmers on it, though again secateurs give a cleaner finish on the large leaves.

Cherry laurel needs pruning twice a year, typically late spring and late summer, to keep a tidy formal shape. It is a more demanding hedge in maintenance terms than bay once established, simply because it grows so fast. It is not related to cooking bay and the leaves are toxic; it is called "laurel" for historical reasons.

Privet: the traditional British choice

Common privet (Ligustrum ovalifolium) was the default front-garden hedge in post-war Britain, and it is still a practical choice where fast establishment and low cost matter. It is the easiest of the three to shear: the small leaves do not brown at cut edges the way laurel does, and a powered hedge trimmer gives an acceptable finish.

The drawbacks are significant, though. Privet is semi-evergreen: in cold winters it may lose some or most of its leaves, which is fine for a mixed country hedge but frustrating if you planted it for a formal year-round screen. It also needs pruning two or three times a year during the growing season to keep a tidy shape, more work than bay laurel demands. The flowers, produced if you let the hedge grow unpruned, have a strong sweet scent that some find pleasant and others find unpleasant.

Which to choose for an East Kent garden

Choose bay laurel if you want a slower-growing, lower-maintenance formal hedge in a sheltered, well-drained position. Coastal gardens in Deal, Sandwich Bay and the medieval centre of Sandwich suit bay particularly well: the mild winters reduce frost risk, and the small to medium scale of many urban and coastal plots suits bay's more measured growth. If you want to harvest leaves for cooking, bay is the obvious choice.

Choose cherry laurel if you need a fast, tough, fully hardy hedge that handles heavy clay soils, shaded boundaries and exposed positions. It is the right choice for most situations in Worth, Ash, Woodnesborough and Eastry where soil is heavier or the site is not sheltered. The extra pruning requirement is the trade-off.

Choose privet if budget is the primary consideration and a few bare patches in cold winters are acceptable. It is the cheapest option to establish and easy to trim, but the semi-evergreen nature makes it a poor choice for a formal privacy screen in all-year-round use.

Common questions

Will bay laurel survive an East Kent winter?

In most years, yes, particularly in sheltered and coastal positions. Deal and Sandwich Bay rarely experience the sustained hard frost that damages bay. An exceptionally cold snap, prolonged temperatures below -8°C, can cause significant browning of the foliage or, in a severe case, kill a large bay plant. Plant in a sheltered position and avoid north or east-facing exposures. In more exposed gardens in Worth or Ash, cherry laurel is more reliable.

Can I use bay leaves from a hedge for cooking?

Yes, Laurus nobilis is the true cooking bay. Leaves from a garden hedge are identical to those sold dried in supermarkets. Pick fresh leaves and use them immediately for the strongest flavour, or dry them slowly in a warm place. Note that cherry laurel and Portuguese laurel are not edible and should not be confused with bay laurel.

Why do bay leaves go brown after pruning?

Bay leaves brown at cut edges when shears or powered trimmers slice through the leaf blade. The exposed tissue desiccates and oxidises. The solution is to use bypass secateurs and cut between leaves rather than through them. This takes longer but produces a hedge that looks clean and healthy immediately after pruning rather than having a brown cast that fades over a few weeks.

How do I deal with brown patches on a bay hedge?

Brown patches in winter are usually frost damage. Cut back to healthy green wood in late spring once new growth has started. Do not prune dead wood in early spring before growth starts: it is difficult to tell which wood is dead and which is just dormant. If the browning affects most of the plant after a hard winter, wait until May and assess what remains alive before deciding whether to replace the plant.
