Topiary for beginners: how to start shaping your garden hedge in Kent

Topiary looks intimidating until you understand what it actually requires. This guide starts with a checklist of what needs to be in place before you begin, then covers species, tools, first shapes and how to build confidence over a season or two.

Updated September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Lee Burkhill, award-winning garden designer, RHS-qualified horticulturist and BBC Garden Rescue presenter, explores the transition from hedge trimming to topiary shapes in his Garden Ninja YouTube video "From Hedge Trimming to Topiary Art: Tips to Transform Your Garden" (youtube.com/watch?v=GFNyrDBFDt8, published September 2025). The central idea in the video is exactly what we tell clients across Sandwich and Deal: topiary is not a separate skill from hedge trimming but an extension of it. The difference is patience, the right species, and knowing where to start.

Before you start: the checklist

Run through these points before picking up the shears. If any of them do not apply to your hedge, sort that out first.

  • Your species responds well to topiary. Yew (Taxus baccata) is the gold standard: it takes hard cuts on old wood and holds a shape beautifully. Box (Buxus sempervirens), holly (Ilex aquifolium) and hornbeam (Carpinus betulus) are also good. Privet, laurel, beech and leylandii do not hold topiary shapes precisely enough to be worth the effort for most garden situations.
  • The hedge is established. For most species, wait until the hedge has been in the ground for at least three years and has reached close to its intended finished height. Trying to add topiary to a hedge that is still maturing creates problems you will be correcting for years. If you want a shaped specimen (a lollipop, a cone, a ball), that is a separate training exercise from the start, not a modification of a mature hedge.
  • Your hedge has a correct basic profile. Before any topiary, the hedge should already have clean, consistent faces with a slight batter (wider at the base than the top). A hedge that is already ragged, hollow at the base or uneven in width will become more difficult to manage once you start imposing a shape. Get the basic profile right first. See our guide on hedge cutting profile and batter.
  • You have suitable hand tools. A power trimmer is fine for maintaining topiary that is already established, but for shaping work and corrections, you need sharp hand shears and a pair of bypass secateurs. Blunt tools crush stems and leave a brown, bruised finish on fine-leaved species. See our guide on hand-cutting technique for detail on what to use and how.
  • You have chosen a realistic first shape. See the section below on choosing a starting point. Spirals and complex animal forms are not first projects.
  • You are working in the right season. Late August through October is the main topiary window. The nesting season has ended (see legal callout below), the hedge has made its main growth, and mild temperatures let the plant recover before winter.
  • You have realistic expectations about the timeline. Significant topiary shapes take two to three seasons to develop from an existing hedge. You are not going to get a crisp finished cone from a plain hedge in one afternoon. Each session moves a little closer to the shape, and the hedge tightens its response over successive seasons.

Nesting season and the law

The UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs broadly from 1 March to 31 August. The safe cutting window for most hedges, including topiary work, is September to February. If you are considering a light tidy-up between March and August, check the hedge carefully for active nests before starting. The end of August into September is when we start topiary work for most Kent clients.

Before you start cutting

  • Wear sturdy gloves, particularly for holly: the cut leaves stay sharp for weeks. Eye protection is important for any overhead work or when cutting towards your face.
  • For powered work on topiary (initial shaping cuts on large hedges), use an RCD adaptor on any corded electric trimmer and check the cable before starting.
  • Check for wasp nests before reaching into the hedge. Wasps nest inside dense hedges, particularly in late summer.
  • Prefer hand tools or a pole trimmer to a ladder for work at height. If a ladder is necessary, have someone footing it and never overreach sideways. Our guide on long-reach trimmers covers the alternative.

Which species to use: the honest summary

Yew (Taxus baccata)

The best topiary plant in the UK. Yew tolerates hard cuts on established wood, holds a crisp edge between trimmings, and can be radically reshaped over several seasons if needed. The main trimming window is late August to early October. Yew is slow growing compared to privet or laurel, which means less maintenance once the shape is established. A yew hedge in Sandwich or Deal will need trimming once a year, usually in September, to maintain a topiary form. See our full guide to yew hedge care.

Box (Buxus sempervirens)

The classic topiary plant for balls, cones and low edging shapes. Box is slow growing and holds a precise cut well. The main concern in Kent at the moment is box blight (Cylindrocladium buxicola): before investing time in topiary shaping, inspect your box hedge for the brown patches, bare stems and fungal webbing that indicate blight. See our guide to box blight treatment. If blight is present, consider box alternatives that hold topiary shapes. Trim box once in late May or early June and tidy in August. Avoid trimming in wet weather.

Holly (Ilex aquifolium)

Holly produces crisp topiary shapes and takes well to hard cutting. The main practical issue is the leaves: even with thick gloves, working with cut holly is uncomfortable, and the fallen leaves remain sharp underfoot for months. Trim in late August to September.

Hornbeam (Carpinus betulus)

Hornbeam is used for pleached hedges and formal structures and can be trained into quite precise forms. It is less common for classic topiary shapes (balls, cones) than yew or box, but works well for geometric hedging forms. See our guide to hornbeam hedge care for timing and technique.

Choosing your first shape: where to start

The temptation is to go straight for a spiral or a peacock. Resist it. The right starting point depends on your hedge and your experience.

Start here: clean flat faces with squared corners

Before any topiary, produce a hedge with genuinely flat, vertical faces and sharp 90-degree top corners. This sounds basic, but most hedges in Kent gardens are cut freehand and have slightly curved faces and soft corners. Using a taut string line to guide your cuts, produce faces that are genuinely flat and corners that are genuinely crisp. This exercise teaches you to cut to a reference, which is the foundation of all topiary work. It also looks noticeably better than a hedge cut without a guide.

Next step: a rounded or domed top

A gently domed top on a hedge is more forgiving than a flat top and looks well in Kent cottage and country gardens. Cut the top to a shallow curve, highest in the centre and lower at the edges. A template cut from hardboard or thick card makes this repeatable. Once you are happy producing a consistent dome, you are ready for more complex forms.

Separate specimens: a cone or ball

Pick a single well-placed yew or box plant in your garden, not part of a hedge run, and train it as a cone or ball over two to three seasons. Work toward the final shape gradually, taking a little more material each year. A cone is easier to start with than a ball: cut from the top down, maintaining the vertical centreline and sloping the sides consistently to meet the base. A ball requires cutting on all planes at once, which is more of an eye-and-feel skill. Our dedicated guide to topiary hedge shaping covers specific shapes in detail.

More advanced: lollipops and spirals

Lollipop and spiral topiary are best trained from a young plant from the start, rather than imposed on an existing hedge specimen. They require a clear vertical stem and careful progressive shaping over several years. See our guides to lollipop topiary training and spiral topiary training for these more involved projects.

Tools for topiary

A good pair of hand shears is the most important tool for topiary work. You need sharp, well-balanced shears with blades that close cleanly without wobble. For detailed work on small shapes, bypass secateurs give more precise control. A taut garden line (string between canes) is essential for flat faces and tops. For larger yew or hornbeam hedges, a good quality hedge trimmer is fine for rough shaping, but the final finish always needs hand shears.

Clean your blades with an oily cloth after each session and sharpen before each topiary cut. Blunt blades on fine-leaved box produce a brown, crushed finish that takes weeks to grow out. See our guide on sharpening hedge shears.

What to expect in year one

In the first season, you are largely marking out the shape and removing the material that sits outside the line you have decided on. The hedge may look slightly ragged after this first cut, particularly on yew, where old wood cut back hard takes a few weeks to produce new growth. Do not panic. Most topiary plants produce new shoots from the cut surface within four to six weeks in good growing conditions.

By the end of year two, the shape will be noticeably cleaner as the plant has grown back into the line you established. By year three, you will have a hedge that looks as though it has always been that shape. This is the timeline we explain to clients in Woodnesborough and Eastry who are starting topiary from an existing privet or yew hedge: patience is the main skill.

The best topiary in East Kent gardens started with someone who just decided to cut to a straighter line this year, and a slightly more defined corner the year after. It builds up without you quite noticing.
