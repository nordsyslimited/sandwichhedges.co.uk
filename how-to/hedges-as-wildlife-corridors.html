Hedges as wildlife corridors: what lives in your hedge and why it matters

Your garden hedge is not just a boundary. For birds, bats, hedgehogs and hundreds of invertebrate species it is a home, a hunting ground and a safe route across a landscape that gets more fragmented every year. Here is what uses your hedge and the seven things that make the biggest difference.

Updated September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Seven-point wildlife-corridor checklist

Tick these off before moving on to the detail below.

  1. Mix at least three to five native species rather than one
  2. Keep hedge height at 1.5m or above
  3. Maintain hedge width at 1m or more where the boundary allows
  4. Leave a 13cm-diameter gap at the base of any adjacent solid fence for hedgehog passage
  5. Keep an uncut grass and wildflower strip along at least one base of the hedge
  6. Cut on a rotational basis: not every side every year
  7. Always cut in the September to February window; check for nests before any summer work

Hedgerows cover roughly 500,000 kilometres in Britain. That is more than five times the length of the road network. In a countryside reshaped by monoculture farming and urban expansion, these linear features are the capillaries that keep wildlife moving between the remaining patches of habitat. Gardens play a larger role in this than most people realise, because garden hedges form the urban end of many hedgerow networks that extend out into the Kent farmland.

In East Kent specifically, the old hedgerow network connecting the farmland behind Sandwich and Deal with the coastal marshes, the Stour Valley and the chalk downland is still largely intact compared with much of England. Garden hedges in Sandwich, Deal, Worth and the villages form the last link in those chains as they reach the town edges. Whether those chains stay connected depends, in part, on how garden hedges are managed.

Who uses your hedge

Birds

In a well-managed garden hedge in East Kent, you might expect to find active nests from dunnock, blackbird, song thrush, robin, wren, chaffinch, linnet and whitethroat. The dunnock is the most consistent garden hedge nester: it builds a tidy cup nest in the base of dense vegetation, often inside a hawthorn or blackthorn section, and is easily overlooked. Song thrushes prefer a thicker hedge, 1.5m or wider, and often use the same general territory for several seasons if undisturbed.

Berries matter as much as nesting cover. A hawthorn hedge produces hundreds of haws from September onwards. They are taken first by redwings and fieldfares arriving from Scandinavia in October, then by resident blackbirds and thrushes through winter. A blackthorn section ripens its sloes in October. Spindle berries are taken by robins in particular. Once the berries are gone, the dense structure itself provides roosting shelter through cold nights.

Yellowhammer and linnet, both on the red list of birds of conservation concern, use the hedgerow network between the farmland around Worth, Woodnesborough and Eastry to move between feeding and nesting sites. Garden hedges at the edge of those villages that connect to the field hedgerows play a part in supporting those populations.

Bats

Several bat species recorded in East Kent use hedges as linear navigation routes. Common pipistrelles and soprano pipistrelles (both of which roost in buildings across Sandwich and Deal) forage along hedge lines at dusk, hawking for moths and midges in the turbulent air column on the windward face of the hedge. Natterer's bats, which roost in older buildings and trees, feed lower in the hedge structure, taking prey from leaves and bark.

The key point about bats and hedges is connectivity. A bat commuting between a roof roost in a Sandwich terrace and feeding grounds on the Stour marshes will follow a hedge line rather than cross open ground. A 20m gap in that hedge forces the bat into open flight where it is exposed to predation and loses the navigational reference of the hedge edge. In a fragmented landscape, the continuity of hedge lines is more important than any single hedge's condition.

Hedgehogs

Hedgehogs need to travel one to two kilometres each night to forage. They are reluctant to cross open ground and will follow linear features, including hedge bases, fence lines and garden walls. A hedgehog moving along a hedge on one side of a road may pause at a fence panel but will continue through a gap. The hedgehog highways movement has popularised the 13cm-diameter hole at the base of solid fencing, and it does work: any gap large enough for a hedgehog but small enough to exclude rabbits allows free movement through connected gardens.

The base of a hedge provides food as well as a route: slugs, beetles, worms and earwigs are concentrated in the leaf litter and rough grass at the hedge bottom. A hedge with a mown margin right to the base loses most of this foraging value. An unmown grass strip, even 20 to 30cm wide, along the base restores it substantially.

Invertebrates

This is where the real ecological weight of hedges lies. A single mature hawthorn plant supports more than 300 species of invertebrate. Field maple supports over 50. Blackthorn supports over 150. The invertebrate communities of a hedge are largely determined by which plants are present, because most specialist invertebrates are tied to specific host plants or plant families.

The hedge also provides overwintering habitat. Ladybirds, lacewings, spider egg cases and the pupae of many moth species overwinter in the bark crevices, dead wood and leaf litter of an undisturbed hedge interior. An annual cut that removes all of the previous year's growth disrupts this more than a two-yearly or three-yearly cut that leaves some old growth in place.

What makes a hedge good for wildlife

Species diversity

The single biggest determinant of a hedge's wildlife value is how many plant species it contains. A privet hedge supports relatively few invertebrates (privet is not native to Britain and most native insects have no evolved relationship with it). A hawthorn and blackthorn hedge supports thousands of times more life. A hedge mixing hawthorn, blackthorn, field maple, hazel and holly supports more still, because different species host different invertebrate communities, flower at different times, and berry at different times.

Height and width

A hedge kept below 1m tall provides very little nesting cover and does not give bats the navigational reference they need. A hedge at 1.5m or above is genuinely useful to nesting birds. A hedge at 2m or above, with a width of 1m or more, provides deep internal structure: the dead wood, leaf litter accumulation and dense interior that support the broadest invertebrate community and the greatest density of nesting birds.

If a hedge needs to be kept below 1.5m for practical reasons (sight lines, an agreement with a neighbour, a planning condition), the remaining priority is width. A low but wide hedge (1m wide, 1m tall) still provides corridor function and invertebrate habitat even if its nesting value is limited.

Cutting frequency and timing

Annual cutting removes the current year's growth before it matures into berry-producing wood. Most berry-producing species only fruit on growth that is at least two years old. A hedge cut every year has few berries; a hedge cut every two years has significantly more; a hedge cut every three years on rotation (one face each year, never both in the same year) produces a sustained berry crop while remaining presentable.

Timing matters too. Cutting in September, October or November removes berries at exactly the moment migrants and winter visitors depend on them. The best compromise for wildlife is to cut in January or February, after the berry crop has been taken by birds, and before nesting begins in March. This window is also the best time for the plants themselves.

Legal requirement: nesting season (Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981)

It is a criminal offence to intentionally damage or destroy an active bird's nest, or to disturb birds while they are at or near a nest with eggs or young. Nesting season runs from 1 March to 31 August. The legally safe trimming window for most hedges is September to February.

If you must trim between March and August, inspect the hedge carefully from underneath before starting, looking for active nests. If you find one, leave that section untouched until the chicks have fledged. Do not assume that because a hedge was empty last year, it is empty this year.

The hedge base

The strip of ground directly below and at the foot of a hedge is often the most ecologically productive part of the whole boundary. Leaf litter accumulates here, providing overwintering habitat for invertebrates. Hedgehogs forage here. In undisturbed hedge bases, primroses, violets and cow parsley establish naturally, which in turn support early bees and hoverflies.

Mowing or strimming to the hedge base every year destroys this habitat almost entirely. Leaving a strip 20 to 30cm wide (or wider if the space allows) unmown makes a substantial difference. The strip looks tidy enough if cut once a year in August.

How to improve your hedge for wildlife

You do not need to let everything go wild. A hedge that functions as a wildlife corridor can still look maintained. The improvements that make the biggest difference are mostly about what you do not do, rather than what you add.

Cut less often. Moving from annual to biennial cutting is the single highest-impact change. The hedge looks slightly less precise in its off year, but it flowers and berries far more. Most neighbours and planning authorities accept a hedge that is trimmed every two years to the same profile as one cut annually.

Cut later in the year. Shifting from an October cut to a January or February cut allows the berry crop to feed birds through autumn and early winter before the hedge is touched. The plants benefit too: a late winter cut produces the season's cleanest new growth.

Leave the base. Stop strimming to the base. Leave an unmown strip and let the leaf litter accumulate. This is the highest-value invertebrate and hedgehog habitat you can provide.

Add one or two native species. If your existing hedge is single-species (privet, leylandii, laurel), you are unlikely to convert it entirely. But planting a few bare-root native whips at one end, or in a gap, adds plant diversity incrementally. Over five to ten years, those additions can become a meaningful proportion of the hedge.

Create a hedgehog gap. If there is a solid fence or wall at the end of your hedge boundary, make a 13cm gap at ground level. It costs nothing and can make a significant difference to hedgehog population connectivity across a neighbourhood.

Connect. A 10m hedge that connects to a neighbour's 10m hedge, which connects to a field hedge beyond, is incomparably more valuable than a 10m hedge in isolation. Talk to neighbours. A group of connected garden hedges forms a functional corridor; individual isolated hedges do not.
