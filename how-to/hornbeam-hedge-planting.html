How to plant a hornbeam hedge in Kent

Often mistaken for beech at a glance, hornbeam is a bit tougher on heavy soils and shadier sites. It's a native British species, it holds its skeleton leaves through winter just like beech, and November is the ideal month to get it in the ground as bare-root.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Hornbeam (Carpinus betulus) is a British native that makes a handsome formal hedge. Its muscled grey bark, serrated leaves and autumn skeleton-leaf retention give it a similar look to beech, but it's more tolerant of heavier soils and partial shade. On the clay-band soils west of Sandwich and in many Ash and Woodnesborough gardens, hornbeam is the more reliable choice where beech can sit yellow and sulky.

Bare-root hornbeam stock arrives from nurseries in November. This guide covers everything from ordering to planting to what the hedge needs in its first year.

Hornbeam versus beech: which suits your site?

If your soil is well-drained chalk or loam, either species works. If it's clay, compacted or in partial shade for more than a few hours a day, hornbeam is safer. Beech has slightly richer copper autumn colour and holds dead leaves a little more densely through winter. Hornbeam's winter skeleton is a little less full but still striking. See our beech vs hornbeam comparison for the full breakdown.

Step 1: Order bare-root stock and check it on arrival

Order from a reputable UK hedging nursery. For East Kent conditions, ask for 40–60cm plants, which establish vigorously and reach a useful hedge height within 4–5 years. Avoid very small transplants (under 30cm) if you want results sooner.

When the bundle arrives, open it immediately and check the roots are moist and the stems pliable. Bare-root stock dries out fast in wind or frost. If you can't plant within two days, heel the plants into a sheltered corner with the roots buried in damp soil or compost.

Step 2: Prepare the ground

Mark the planting line with a string line. Clear the strip (60cm wide) of turf and all perennial weeds. Bindweed and dock root left in the bed will compete with young hornbeams in year one and are much harder to remove once the hedge is in. On compacted clay, fork over to 25–30cm depth to improve drainage.

Work in a couple of spadefuls of organic matter per metre along the strip. On clay soils, adding horticultural grit helps drainage around the root zone in the first year.

Step 3: Dig the trench

For a run of three or more plants, a continuous trench is most efficient. Dig 30–40cm wide and 25–30cm deep. This lets you improve the soil evenly along the whole line and keeps spacing consistent. For just one or two specimen plants, individual holes to 1.5 times the root spread work fine.

Step 4: Set spacing and plant

For a formal boundary hedge, plant 2–3 per metre. At 50cm spacings in a single row you get a reasonable screen within 4–5 years. A double staggered row at 40–45cm centres fills in more quickly and gives a denser base from the start, which matters for a garden boundary at Ash or Eastry where privacy is the priority. For a wilder or more naturalistic hedge, 1–2 per metre is enough.

Place each plant in the trench with the root collar at soil level. Fan the roots out rather than bundling them. Backfill with the improved soil, pressing it in firmly with your foot as you go. Any air pockets left around the roots can dry them out during the first summer.

Step 5: Water in and mulch

Water each plant in well, even in November: the soil needs to settle closely around the roots. Then lay a 10cm bark mulch collar along the full planting strip, keeping a 5cm gap around each stem. The mulch suppresses competing weeds, holds moisture in summer and moderates soil temperature. Leave it in place through the whole first year.

Step 6: First-year care

Re-firm any plants lifted by frost or wind in the weeks after planting. When dry spells arrive the following spring and summer, water once a week. Young hornbeam dislikes staying wet (clay soils need watching), but it also dislikes drying out completely in its first summer. A deep watering once a week is better than little and often.

Resist trimming in year one. The hedge needs all its energy in the roots. Light shaping on the sides can begin in the second autumn, September or October. See annual hornbeam hedge care for the maintenance cycle once the hedge is established.

Planting season and nesting season: they don't clash, but trimming does

Bare-root planting runs November to March, well outside the bird nesting season. But once your hedge is established, all trimming and shaping must respect the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981. Nesting season runs 1 March to 31 August. The safe cutting window is September to February. This applies to hornbeam of any age.
