Brambles growing through your hedge: a step-by-step decision guide

One of the most common calls we get. Brambles thread through established hedges across Kent, and there is no single right answer: it depends on where the root is, what time of year it is, and whether you actually want them gone.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Brambles (Rubus fruticosus) are among the most determined plants in a Kent hedgerow. They root where they touch the ground, send canes arching out from the base, and can thread themselves so thoroughly through an established privet or hawthorn hedge that cutting them out damages the hedge itself. They are also, depending on your point of view, either a nuisance or a wildlife asset, often both at once.

The guide below is a decision tree: answer each question in order, and you will reach the right approach for your situation. Skip ahead and you risk either disturbing nesting birds or doing work that does not solve the problem.

Before you start

  • Thick leather gloves. Bramble thorns are persistent and nasty. Standard garden gloves are not enough; use heavy gauntlets that cover the wrist.
  • Eye protection. Canes whip back. Safety glasses are worth it.
  • Long sleeves and trousers. There is no other way.
  • Loppers or secateurs for initial work, not a powered trimmer. Bramble canes can catch in hedge trimmer blades and pull the tool, or flick debris unpredictably. Cut the canes to manageable lengths first, then tidy up with the trimmer if needed.
  • Check for nests before cutting anything. Bramble is a popular nesting site for blackbirds and robins. If it is March to August, see Step 1 before touching anything.

The decision guide

Step 1: Is it March to August?

If yes, your first action is to check for active nests, not to cut. Bramble thickets are actively used as nesting sites. Part the canes carefully and look before you do anything else.

Nests found: Stop. Come back after 31 August. The nest will be empty by then and you can proceed with full work.

No nests found: A light surface trim of young shoots is acceptable. Do not dig out root crowns or do heavy renovation during March to August; leave that for winter.

If it is September to February, proceed to Step 2.

Step 2: Is the bramble on your side of the hedge, or on the boundary?

This matters for heavy extraction work.

Your side only: You can proceed with full removal if you choose. The root is on your land and so is the work.

Boundary hedge: Agree with your neighbour before doing any heavy extraction. Even if the bramble started on their side, cutting into a shared boundary hedge without agreement can cause disputes. A conversation first costs nothing.

Step 3: How deep into the hedge has it grown?

Shoots only on the surface of the hedge: Cut the canes back to the hedge line in winter. Repeat each autumn. This exhausts the root over two to three years if you are consistent, and does not disturb the hedge structure.

Stems threaded through the hedge interior: Do not pull them out. Bramble canes thread through other plants and pulling them tears branches off the hedge. Instead: cut each cane at both the entry and exit points, then leave the dead stems in place. They will compost down over one to two seasons without damaging the hedge. Remove the cut stubs flush to the hedge surface.

Step 4: Do you want to keep it or remove it?

This is worth thinking about before you commit to full extraction.

Worth keeping: A managed bramble patch within or at the base of a hedge is a genuine wildlife asset. Keep it and manage it rather than eradicate it. See the wildlife section below.

Remove it completely: Cut all stems to 15cm stubs. Dig out the root crown to 20-30cm depth, taking as much of the root system as you can find. Treat any regrowth the following September when the plant is moving sugars to its roots. Repeat for two to three years. There is no quicker reliable method.

The wildlife case for a managed bramble patch

Before you spend a Sunday digging out bramble crowns, it is worth knowing what you are removing. Blackbirds and song thrushes nest in bramble; robins and wrens use it as cover. The berries are taken from August onwards by birds, foxes, and in areas where they occur, dormice. The flowers are good for pollinators in early summer. A hedge with a managed bramble patch at its base is a denser, richer habitat than a neatly clipped hedge without one.

The practical question is usually not whether to have bramble at all, but whether the spread is under control. Bramble that has taken over a third of the hedge and is pushing into the garden is a different problem from a bramble plant filling a gap at the base. The former needs cutting back; the latter might be better left.

Nesting season: check before you cut

Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs roughly from 1 March to 31 August. Bramble is one of the most commonly used nesting plants in Kent garden hedges. The safe cutting window is September to February. During summer, only a surface trim of young growth is safe, and only after a careful check.
