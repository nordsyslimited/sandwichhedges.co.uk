Protecting a newly planted hedge from rabbits and deer

A new hedge is most at risk in its first two winters, when plants are small and there is little else for wildlife to eat. Here is what to put in place before anything starts nibbling.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Quick checklist: five things to do when you plant

  • Fit individual spiral or mesh guards to every whip (rabbits).
  • Lay a mulch mat or bark mulch around each plant to suppress weeds and hold moisture.
  • If deer are known locally, use 1.2 m tree tubes or a temporary perimeter fence.
  • Bury the base of any mesh fencing 20–30 cm underground to stop burrowing.
  • Check guards each spring for chafing or constriction; remove when stems are fully woody (usually year two or three).

Why new hedge plants need protecting

Bare-root hedging whips go into the ground between November and March, which is exactly when rabbit food is scarcest. Rabbits do not wait for spring. A cold January night is all it takes for something to strip the bark clean around the stem of a new hawthorn or beech whip, killing the plant right at ground level. It is called ring-barking and it leaves no visible marks from a distance, so by the time you notice in April, the plant looks perfectly fine above ground and completely dead below it.

Deer damage is different but just as thorough. Fallow deer and muntjac are both present in East Kent, particularly where gardens back onto farmland near Ash, Worth and the margins of the Stour valley. They browse the growing tips rather than ring-bark, so young plants get pinched back again and again through winter, never building any real growth.

Neither problem is inevitable. Spend half an hour fitting guards when you plant and the hedge looks after itself for the next two years.

Rabbit guards: the right type and height

Spiral tree guards are the most common choice for individual whips. They wrap around the stem in a loose coil and expand as the plant grows. They do the job, but they have limitations: rabbits will still gnaw the bark through the gaps in a flimsy spiral if they are hungry enough, and the guards can trap moisture against the stem in wet conditions.

Mesh guards, the kind made from rigid plastic mesh, are more expensive but significantly sturdier. They let air through, dry out faster and can be staked firmly. For beech, hornbeam and other thin-barked species, mesh guards are worth the extra cost.

Height matters. A standard 45 cm spiral guard stops casual rabbit damage on level ground, but rabbits can reach higher when standing on snow or on raised soil. A 60 cm guard is safer for whips in open, exposed positions. For areas with very high rabbit pressure, like the downland edges around Woodnesborough and Eastry where warrens can hold large populations, a 90 cm tube or a continuous fence (see below) will give more reliable results.

Push a short bamboo cane or stake alongside each guard and tie it loosely. Without support, guards lean in wind and do not stay centred on the stem.

Mesh fencing for longer hedge runs

Where you are planting a continuous run of hedging, individual guards on every plant can be time-consuming. A strip of wire mesh or plastic rabbit netting along the whole run, pegged tightly and buried 20–30 cm into the ground to stop digging under, gives more complete coverage. Use 31 mm hex mesh (chicken wire) or small-gauge welded wire. The fence only needs to be 90–100 cm above ground since rabbits rarely jump over a properly staked fence under pressure.

Remove or roll back the fencing once the hedge is established enough to be unappealing to rabbits, usually when the stems at ground level are pencil-thick and the bark is properly hardened. This is typically two to three growing seasons after planting for fast-growing hawthorn and blackthorn, longer for beech and yew.

Deer protection

Rabbits and deer need very different solutions because deer are so much larger and stronger. A spiral guard will not slow a deer at all. Your options depend on how much pressure you are expecting.

For occasional deer browsing, 1.2 m tree tubes over individual plants give reasonable protection for the most vulnerable growing tips. Secure them firmly: a curious deer can dislodge a loosely fitted tube with a nudge.

For persistent deer, particularly muntjac, which are small enough to push through gaps and will revisit the same spot night after night, a perimeter deer fence is the only reliable solution for a new hedge run. The fence needs to be at least 1.8 m for fallow deer, 1.5 m is adequate for muntjac alone. It can be temporary post-and-wire fencing removed after two or three years, once the hedge is dense and tall enough to be less appealing to browse.

Check with neighbours before spending money on deer fencing. If you are the only garden on the road taking the problem seriously, the deer will simply work their way around the outside of your fence and through the next gap.

Mulch mats and bark mulch

Protection from wildlife is one side of the job. The other is keeping the soil around each new plant in good condition. Young hedging plants have small root systems and struggle to compete with weeds for water and nutrients, particularly in their first summer after a bare-root planting in winter.

Mulch mats, whether square plastic sheets pegged down around each plant or biodegradable jute squares, suppress weeds and slow moisture loss from the soil. A 50 cm square mat around each plant makes a noticeable difference to first-year establishment, especially on free-draining chalk or sandy soils common in parts of the Sandwich and Deal area.

Bark mulch does the same job and looks better once the plants are growing well. Apply a 7–10 cm layer, kept a few centimetres away from the stems to avoid collar rot. Top it up each autumn as it decomposes. On heavier clay soils in Ash and Woodnesborough, less mulch is needed since these soils hold moisture better, though they may still need weed suppression in the first year.

Aftercare in years two and three

Check guards every spring and after any severe storms. Spiral guards in particular have a habit of tightening as the stem grows and cutting into the bark if left too long. Remove any guard that has become constricted or that is sitting below the point where the plant has thickened.

Do not rush to remove guards entirely. A guard that seems unnecessary in summer, when the plant looks healthy and established, will still be doing useful work the following February when a late cold snap brings hungry rabbits out to graze. Two full winters of protection is a sensible minimum for most species; three winters for yew, box and other slow-growing plants.
