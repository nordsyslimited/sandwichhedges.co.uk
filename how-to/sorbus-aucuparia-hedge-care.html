Rowan (mountain ash) in Kent hedgerows

Not every hedge wants to be clipped square. Rowan earns its place in a mixed native hedgerow with berries that strip in days, autumn colour that stops people in their tracks, and a root system that holds a bank in any weather.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Sorbus aucuparia, the mountain ash or rowan, is one of those plants that people associate with upland Scotland and Lake District hillsides. Which is fair enough. But it grows perfectly well in the lanes and field margins of East Kent too, and in a mixed native hedgerow it is one of the more useful things you can put in.

It will not give you a formal, flat-topped boundary. That is not what it does. What it does is fruit heavily in September, feed the fieldfares and redwings that pour through Kent on their way south, and look genuinely good doing it. In a hedgerow between Worth and Ash, or running along the edge of a paddock outside Eastry, it fits naturally and needs very little from you once it is established.

Nesting season: check before you cut

The UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs roughly 1 March to 31 August. Rowan is a popular nesting site for thrushes and other hedgerow birds. Any pruning or shaping work on a rowan-containing hedgerow should take place between September and February. From late February onwards, always check the plant carefully before cutting anything.

What rowan actually does in a hedgerow

Rowan is classified as a hedgerow standard tree species, which means it is one of the plants that, in the countryside, is deliberately left to grow through the canopy of a cut hedgerow rather than being clipped down with it. You see this in the older hedgerows along the back lanes between Sandwich and Woodnesborough: the lower hedge of blackthorn, hawthorn and elder is kept at roughly head height, and a rowan or field maple every twenty metres or so is allowed to grow taller.

In a garden context, rowan works best in one of two ways: as a hedgerow tree in a native mix kept at informal height, or as a free-growing boundary plant in a wilder corner. It is not happy being clipped repeatedly to a hard line. That approach stresses it, encourages fireblight entry points, and removes the very growth that carries next year's berries.

Planting rowan in a new hedgerow

When to plant

Bare-root rowan is available from November through to March and is almost always the better buy: cheaper, quicker to establish, and more resilient in its first year than a pot-grown specimen. Plant between November and February for the best result. Pot-grown stock can go in at any time but needs more consistent watering in its first summer, which matters in the dry spells East Kent gets from June through August.

Spacing

If you are growing rowan as part of a mixed native hedge, space it at roughly one plant every three to five metres, alternating it with hawthorn, blackthorn, field maple and hazel. You are not trying to create a solid rowan screen. One or two plants in a twenty-metre run is plenty, and they will spread sideways as they grow.

Soil and drainage

Rowan is fairly unfussy. It tolerates chalk (common in the Eastry and Nonington areas), clay and sandy soils. It does not like waterlogged ground for any length of time. On heavy clay, dig in grit around the planting hole and consider planting slightly high, with the root flare level with or just above the surrounding soil. On the light sandy soils closer to the coast, mulch generously to retain moisture through the summer.

Before you start: basic precautions

  • Wear sturdy gloves (rowan branches have small spurs) and eye protection when working with hand tools near the crown.
  • If working at height to remove competing branches from a mature rowan, use a pole pruner or long-reach tool in preference to a ladder. If a ladder is necessary, have someone footing it at all times and never overreach sideways.
  • Disinfect cutting tools between plants, particularly if fireblight has been seen nearby. A dilute bleach solution or proprietary disinfectant wipe works well.
  • Check for wasp nests in the crown before working in late summer or autumn.

The first three years: formative work

Year one

Leave it alone. Your job in year one is to keep the grass back from the base (a 60cm weed-free circle), water in dry spells, and protect the stem from rabbits and deer with a spiral guard or mesh tube. Rowan has a central leading shoot that you want to preserve. Do not prune in the first year.

Year two

In February, while it is still fully dormant, remove any crossing or rubbing branches at the base. If the plant has produced multiple competing leaders, reduce to one clear leader unless you want a multi-stemmed form (which is also legitimate in a wilder hedgerow, just harder to manage later). Cuts should be clean and angled away from buds.

Year three and beyond

By year three, rowan in Kent's reasonably mild maritime climate should have put on substantial growth. The formative work is largely done. From here, management is light: remove any dead or diseased wood in winter, keep the base clear of competing grass and bramble, and resist the urge to clip the crown unless it is genuinely impeding something.

Ongoing management in a mixed hedgerow

When the surrounding hedge is cut

When you trim the lower hedgerow plants in late August through to February, leave the rowan uncut, or at most remove any whippy growth that has flopped sideways into the hedge line. The rowan should be growing above the cut level of the hedge, not competing with it for the same space.

Remedial work

If a rowan has grown too large and is shading out the rest of the hedge, crown reduction (shortening the main branches back to a suitable lateral) in the dormant season is the right approach. This is different from clipping: you are making a small number of deliberate cuts to structural branches, not shearing the whole crown. If the work involves anything more than a couple of metres of height, it is worth getting a tree professional in. For most garden rowans, though, they stay well within manageable size.

Autumn: berries, colour and timing

Rowan berries ripen from late August into September, turning from yellow-orange to bright red. In most years, the first fieldfares arrive in Kent from Scandinavia in October, and they will find the berries quickly. A rowan that has held its fruit all summer can be stripped in three or four days. This is not a problem; it is the plant doing exactly what it evolved to do.

If you want to extend the berry season, choose a species with orange or yellow fruits rather than the standard red: Sorbus 'Joseph Rock' (pale yellow) and Sorbus commixta 'Embley' (orange-red) are retained slightly longer by birds, simply because they are less familiar. But in a wildlife hedgerow, the speed of stripping is a measure of success, not failure.

The foliage turns orange-red in October and drops cleanly. Rowan does not hold its leaves like beech and hornbeam; by November the hedge line reverts to the twiggy structure of blackthorn and hawthorn, and the rowan disappears into the background until spring.

Common problems

Fireblight

Fireblight (Erwinia amylovora) is the main thing to watch for. It causes the classic scorched, wilting look in young shoots and flowers, particularly in warm wet springs. Rowan is in the same family as hawthorn, pear and apple, and all are susceptible. Remove affected growth immediately: cut back to 60cm beyond the visible symptoms, into clearly healthy wood, and disinfect your cutting tools between cuts. Do not compost affected material; bag and bin it. Severe infections sometimes need a professional assessment.

Canker

Various fungal cankers can produce sunken, discoloured patches on stems. In most cases the answer is the same: clean cut back to healthy wood, disinfect tools, and improve air circulation if the plant is in a very congested part of the hedge.

Coral spot

The small pink or orange pustules of coral spot (Nectria cinnabarina) appear on dead or dying wood. It is almost always secondary to another problem. Remove the dead wood, address any underlying cause (waterlogging, physical damage), and the coral spot generally goes away.

Slow establishment

Rowan on chalk or very thin soil in East Kent can establish slowly. Give it two full seasons before worrying. Mulching with 7-10cm of bark around the base, without letting it touch the stem, makes a real difference to establishment speed on the thin, dry soils around Sandwich Bay and the Lydden Valley.

Rowan in a hedgerow is one of those long-term decisions that pays off years later. Plant it now, manage it lightly, and in a decade it will be one of the best things in your boundary.
