Sea buckthorn hedge in East Kent

Hippophae rhamnoides is one of the few hedge plants that genuinely thrives in the salt wind and thin sandy soils of the Kent coast. Here is how to plant it, manage it and keep it from swallowing the garden.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Sea buckthorn: quick-start checklist

  • Plant in full sun on free-draining, ideally sandy or chalky soil
  • Include at least one male plant per four to six females for berry production
  • Space plants 1.5–2 metres apart in the hedge row
  • Trim once a year: late February (before nesting) or September (after nesting)
  • Remove root suckers annually to prevent the hedge spreading
  • Check for active bird nests before any summer work
  • Do not plant on waterlogged or heavy clay soil

Sea buckthorn grows naturally along the Kent coast. You will find it at Sandwich Bay, on the dune margins around Pegwell Bay and dotted through the scrub between Deal and Walmer. It seeds there without any help, colonises bare sand and gravel, and tolerates conditions that would kill a privet or a beech within two winters. If you have a coastal garden and an informal boundary to fill, it is hard to beat.

In a garden setting, though, sea buckthorn needs some management. It suckers prolifically. Left alone, a single plant becomes a thicket within a decade. The key is to decide from the start whether you want a loose, wildlife-rich hedge or a somewhat tidier single-row planting, and manage it accordingly.

Nesting season: check before you cut

Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to damage or destroy a nest that is in active use. Nesting season runs roughly from 1 March to 31 August. Sea buckthorn is dense and thorny and is a very popular nesting site for whitethroats, linnets and other hedgerow birds. Before any trim between March and August, part the branches and look carefully. If you find a nest with eggs or young, stop and come back in September.

Full sun and free-draining soil

Sea buckthorn's natural habitat is coastal dune and cliff-edge scrub: full sun, thin soil, constant wind, occasional salt spray. In East Kent this translates well to the sandy soils around Sandwich Bay, the chalky free-draining ground behind the cliffs near St Margaret's Bay, and the lighter soils along the Deal seafront gardens. It is not a plant for a shaded north-facing boundary or a heavy clay garden that holds winter water, and it will sulk on both.

If your soil is heavier than ideal, you can improve it: dig in generous amounts of grit and sharp sand, mound up slightly for drainage, and plant shallow rather than deep. But if the garden is genuinely waterlogged, choose hawthorn or blackthorn instead.

Male and female plants: getting the ratio right

Sea buckthorn is dioecious: separate male and female plants carry the flowers. Only female plants produce the orange berries, but they need a male nearby to pollinate them. The standard recommendation is one male per four to six females in the hedge row. You will need to buy named or sexed stock from a reputable hedging supplier to be sure what you are getting, because bare-root plants sold as an unspecified mix may not give you the right ratio.

The male plants are slightly larger and more vigorous and carry no berries, so it is worth labelling them when planting so you do not remove them by mistake when managing suckers later.

Planting spacing and establishment

For a hedge row, space plants 1.5 to 2 metres apart. Sea buckthorn is a large shrub that will reach 3 to 4 metres if left, so closer spacing is unnecessary and will lead to a congested hedge that is harder to manage. Bare-root plants (available November to March) establish well and are cheaper than pot-grown; plant them at the same depth as in the nursery, firm in well and water through the first summer if rainfall is low.

Growth in the first year is slow. Sea buckthorn puts its energy into root establishment before throwing up much top growth. By year three it is generally well established and starts to spread meaningfully.

Pruning and trimming

Sea buckthorn does not suit the close, formal clip of a privet or yew hedge. The thorns (which are genuine spines, unlike the softer prickles of a rose) make close work with hand shears uncomfortable, and the natural growth habit is arching and open rather than dense. Treat it as a semi-formal or informal hedge, maintaining the outline shape rather than a perfect flat face.

The two best times to trim are:

  • Late February: before new growth starts and before nesting season begins on 1 March. This is the preferred time. You can remove the previous year's growth, shorten long shoots and cut out any dead wood clearly without disturbing birds. A hedge trimmer with good guards handles the outer face; use loppers for thicker stems.
  • September: after the main nesting season ends (31 August), and before the berries have been taken by birds in October and November. A September cut tidies the summer's growth and keeps the hedge within its intended width. Do not cut hard in September, a light trim of new growth is enough.

Avoid cutting between March and August unless you have checked thoroughly and the hedge is demonstrably empty of nests.

Thorn safety

Sea buckthorn spines are short but genuinely sharp and are positioned at the tips of side shoots where your hands are most likely to land when reaching into the hedge. Wear thick leather gauntlet gloves for any pruning or trimming work. Eye protection is advisable when using a hedge trimmer, as cut material is thrown out at speed. If a spine drives deep into the skin, clean the wound thoroughly: like blackthorn, sea buckthorn carries surface bacteria that can cause persistent infections if left untreated.

Sucker management

Root suckers are the biggest management challenge with sea buckthorn in a garden setting. The plant sends up shoots from its lateral roots, often some distance from the base of the parent plant. Left unchecked, these colonise the surrounding lawn or border within a few years.

Deal with suckers as soon as they appear: cut them off at ground level with sharp secateurs or slice through the root with a spade. Do not pull them up, as disturbing the root tends to stimulate the plant to send up more. An annual patrol of the hedge base in spring, cutting off any new suckers immediately, keeps this manageable. On a larger hedge, a physical root barrier buried 30 to 40 centimetres deep along the garden side of the hedge will limit spread, though it is not foolproof on vigorous established plants.

Wildlife value

Sea buckthorn is one of the most wildlife-rich plants you can put in a Kent garden boundary. The dense, thorny structure provides excellent nesting cover for whitethroats, linnets, dunnocks and blackbirds. The autumn and winter berries are eaten by redwings, fieldfares, waxwings (in irruption years) and thrushes. The foliage hosts aphids that, in turn, support blue tits and other insectivorous birds. And the nitrogen-fixing root nodules improve soil quality for neighbouring plants over time.

David Domoney's guide to making a wildlife-friendly hedge, available on his YouTube channel (342,000 subscribers), covers the value of native thorny shrubs in the hedgerow and how to balance wildlife benefit with garden management.

Where it fits in East Kent gardens

Sea buckthorn works best in larger gardens with an informal style, coastal or semi-coastal locations, and gardens where wildlife value is a priority over a neat clipped boundary. It is an excellent choice for a windbreak hedge at Sandwich Bay, Worth or Walmer where the sea winds are a constant problem for less robust species. In a smaller, formal town garden in Sandwich or Deal town centre, a more controllable species like privet, hornbeam or beech will be easier to live with.

If you want the wildlife and coastal character without committing to a full sea buckthorn hedge, it works well as a structural element within a mixed native hedge alongside hawthorn, blackthorn and field maple, which gives you the berry and nesting value without the management demands of a single-species planting.
