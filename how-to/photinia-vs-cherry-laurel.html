Photinia Red Robin vs cherry laurel

Two of the most popular fast-growing evergreen hedging plants in East Kent gardens, compared across growth rate, trimming ease, disease, wildlife value and what each suits best.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Ask a garden centre in Sandwich, Deal or Eastry for a fast-growing evergreen hedging plant and you will almost certainly be shown either Photinia Red Robin or cherry laurel. Both are evergreen, both are vigorous, both produce glossy dark leaves, and both are genuinely easy to establish in Kent's maritime climate. The choice between them depends on what you need the hedge to do, how much time you want to spend trimming, and whether any of the specific disadvantages of either plant matter on your particular site.

At a glance: the key differences

Photinia Red Robin Cherry Laurel
Annual growth 20 to 40cm 30 to 60cm
Ultimate height 3 to 5m (manageable) 4 to 8m (vigorous)
New growth colour Vivid copper-red (spring & summer) Pale green, no seasonal colour
Leaf size Small to medium Large (10 to 20cm)
Trimmer suitability Good: clean cut Poor: shreds large leaves
Trimming frequency 2 to 3 times per year 2 to 4 times per year (faster growing)
Main disease Photinia leaf spot (Entomosporium) Shot hole, Phytophthora root rot (occasional)
Wildlife value Moderate (flowers and small berries) Good (flowers, black fruits for birds)
Shade tolerance Moderate Good
Hard renovation Tolerates it, but recovers slowly Tolerates it well, regrows vigorously

Where Photinia Red Robin wins

Photinia's main advantage over cherry laurel is ornamental: the brilliant copper-red new growth that flushes twice a year, in spring and early summer, genuinely catches the eye. For a garden boundary in Sandwich or Deal where the hedge is visible from the house, this makes a difference. Cherry laurel's new growth is pale green and unremarkable by comparison.

Photinia also cuts cleaner with a hedge trimmer. Its leaves are proportionate in size to what the trimmer blade handles comfortably, so a well-managed formal photinia hedge looks sharp immediately after trimming. Trimming cherry laurel with the same tool leaves the visible cut edges of large leaves brown and ragged for several weeks.

For a compact, manageable formal boundary hedge, photinia is the better choice. It can be kept to 1.5m without much difficulty and stays proportionate in most urban and suburban East Kent gardens.

Where cherry laurel wins

Cherry laurel is simply faster, and in some situations that matters. If you are planting a new screen and need height within two to three seasons, cherry laurel achieves this more quickly than photinia. It also tolerates shadier spots better: on a north-facing or part-shaded boundary, cherry laurel stays dense where photinia may become sparse.

Cherry laurel is also more forgiving of neglect. If an established hedge has grown very large, you can cut cherry laurel back hard, into near-bare old wood, and it will throw vigorous new growth. Photinia tolerates renovation, but its recovery is slower and less certain.

The wildlife value of cherry laurel also exceeds photinia. The white flower spikes in spring attract bees, and the small black cherry-fruits in late summer and autumn are taken by thrushes and blackbirds. If you want your boundary hedge to pull double duty as habitat, cherry laurel edges ahead.

The disease question

Photinia leaf spot (caused by Entomosporium) is the most significant issue with photinia in East Kent gardens, particularly in seasons with warm, wet weather. The telltale red and brown spots on leaves can spread rapidly in humid conditions and defoliate a hedge if left unmanaged. Avoiding trimming in wet weather, disinfecting cutting tools between uses, and removing affected material rather than leaving it at the hedge base all reduce the risk. For detailed guidance see our photinia leaf spot treatment guide.

Cherry laurel is generally hardy but can suffer from shot hole (a fungal leaf disease causing small circular holes) and, less commonly, Phytophthora root rot in waterlogged soils. Kent's variable soils mean that on heavy clay ground around Sandwich and Woodnesborough, poor drainage is worth addressing before planting either species.

Trimming timing: the nesting season applies to both

Nesting season: applies to both species

Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to damage an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs roughly 1 March to 31 August. Both photinia and cherry laurel are used as nesting cover by garden birds, particularly wren, blackbird and robin. The main cutting window for both species (late summer to late autumn) is September to October, well clear of nesting season. Always check before any cut if there is any doubt.

For both species, the recommended main cut is in late August or September once nesting is safely over. Photinia can also take a light second trim in late May or early June to keep new growth compact: always check for nests first. Cherry laurel can take a second cut in June once the spring flowering is complete.

Before you start: safety checklist

Checklist before trimming either hedge

  • Eye protection: flying debris from both species, particularly when using a trimmer on cherry laurel's large leaves.
  • Gloves: sturdy gloves for loppers on older stems; cherry laurel juice is a mild skin irritant in some people.
  • Cherry laurel toxicity: all parts of cherry laurel contain prunasin. In normal garden use the risk is low for adults, but keep cut material away from horses, cattle or grazing animals.
  • RCD adaptor: for electric trimmers, use an RCD and keep the cable behind you at all times.
  • Prefer a long-reach trimmer over a ladder for any cuts above shoulder height: a pole trimmer from the ground is safer than overreaching from a ladder.

The right choice for your garden

If you want an ornamental, reasonably compact formal hedge that cuts cleanly and offers visual interest through the year, choose Photinia Red Robin. If you want the fastest possible screen, need to cover a large run in shade, or want wildlife value from your boundary hedge, choose cherry laurel. Both will thrive in East Kent's mild maritime climate.
