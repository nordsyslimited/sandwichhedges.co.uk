Vine weevil damage to hedges: identification and treatment

Vine weevil is one of those pests that does most of its damage underground, so by the time a hedge looks seriously ill the root system may already be badly compromised. Knowing what to look for above and below ground makes a real difference to catching it in time.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

What vine weevil does to a hedge

The adult vine weevil is a dull black beetle, about 9mm long, that feeds at night. It chews smooth, U-shaped notches from the margins of leaves, working through euonymus, hebe, pittosporum, escallonia and berberis in particular. The notching is easy to spot once you know what you are looking for, and it is cosmetically unpleasant, but an adult feeding on foliage alone rarely kills a hedge outright.

The larvae are the real problem. Eggs are laid in the soil near host plants from midsummer, and the larvae hatch as white, C-shaped grubs that spend the autumn and winter eating roots. A hedge section that wilts suddenly in September or October, despite the soil being reasonably moist, may have a root zone already stripped by grubs. By the time the wilting is visible, a significant amount of root has usually been lost.

Which plants are most at risk

Euonymus japonicus is the hedging species most commonly affected in East Kent gardens, and it is widely planted in Sandwich, Deal and Eastry as a tough, semi-formal hedge for coastal conditions. Hebe hedges, pittosporum tobira and escallonia also show up regularly. Leylandii, privet and beech are rarely touched. Any of the susceptible species growing in a container are at considerably higher risk, because the restricted root zone means larvae have little space to spread and the damage is concentrated.

How to confirm the cause

Leaf notching by itself confirms adult feeding. For a check on larvae, gently fork the soil around the base of a wilting plant. Vine weevil larvae are unmistakeable: cream-white, legless, C-shaped grubs about 10mm when fully grown, with a brown head. Finding several in a small area is a clear indication of an established infestation. It is worth checking in September and October, when larvae are present but have not yet done their worst damage.

Treatment: biological control with nematodes

The RHS recommends Steinernema kraussei as the primary biological treatment. This is a microscopic nematode sold as a soil drench under trade names including Vine Weevil Killer. The nematodes parasitise vine weevil larvae in the soil and are the most effective approach for an established problem.

Timing matters. The treatment works when soil temperature is between 5°C and 20°C: in East Kent, September to October is the main window, with a second opportunity in March to April. Apply to moist soil and water in thoroughly after treatment. The nematodes die in dry soil, so if autumn is dry (a common enough situation in East Kent), water the area well before applying. Annual treatment is usually needed, as adults flying in from neighbouring gardens will re-establish populations each season.

Note that Steinernema kraussei tolerates cooler soil temperatures than the related species Steinernema carpocapsae, making it the better choice for autumn and early spring treatment in Kent. Check the product label confirms which species you are buying.

Physical control alongside nematodes

Reducing the adult population alongside nematode treatment improves results. Adults do not fly, so sticky physical barriers around stems can intercept them as they move up from the soil. Checking plants at night with a torch and removing adults by hand is surprisingly effective in a small garden. Yellow sticky cards are used in some polytunnel situations, but they contribute little outdoors.

Recovery after treatment

A hedge treated in time, with reasonable root mass remaining, usually produces new growth in spring. If a section looks completely dead, scratch the stems with a thumbnail: green or white tissue beneath the bark means the plant is alive; brown, dry and brittle all the way through means it is not. Euonymus in particular can look alarming in late winter and still push new growth from living stems once temperatures rise. Give treated plants a few weeks before writing off a section as lost.

A note on East Kent conditions

Vine weevil does particularly well in the milder coastal strip of East Kent. The ground around Sandwich Bay, Deal and Eastry rarely freezes hard enough for long enough to knock back overwintering larvae naturally, so populations accumulate over successive seasons in untreated gardens. If you have euonymus or hebe hedging that has been looking patchy and nibbled for a few years, it is worth checking the root zone before assuming the cause is drought or disease. The RHS has useful guidance on vine weevil identification and treatment as a cross-reference.
