Aucuba japonica (spotted laurel) hedge care

Spotted laurel is one of the few hedging plants that genuinely thrives in deep shade, tolerates hard cutting and does not complain when you forget about it for a season. Here is the checklist for keeping it tidy.

Updated September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Aucuba japonica gets a bad reputation because people plant it in the wrong place. In a sunny border where a photinia or viburnum tinus would thrive, aucuba looks a little tired and coarse. Put it in a shaded side passage, a north-facing boundary or under the drip of a big tree, and it is almost the only evergreen that will make a decent hedge. It is also very forgiving of hard cutting, which cannot be said for many of its competitors.

Here is the checklist we use when maintaining aucuba hedges around the Sandwich and Deal area.

The aucuba japonica maintenance checklist

  1. Prune in mid-spring (April to May). This is the main pruning window. The worst of winter is over, so fresh cuts are not exposed to hard frost, and the plant has a full growing season ahead to put on new growth and fill in any gaps. A second light tidy in late August is optional if the hedge looks messy.
  2. Use secateurs, not hedge shears. Aucuba has large, glossy leaves. Shears and powered trimmers cut through individual leaves, leaving brown, raggedy edges at every cut point. Secateurs used to cut back complete stems give a much cleaner result. For a long hedge run this takes more time, but the finish is far superior. If you do use a powered trimmer, follow up with secateurs to remove the cut-leaf stubs.
  3. Check for nesting birds before starting. See the WCA 1981 note below.
  4. Cut back to a visible bud or side-shoot. Identify where you want the branch to end, find the nearest outward-facing bud or side-shoot below that point, and cut just above it at a slight angle. This ensures the plant will regrow in the direction you want rather than producing a stump that dies back.
  5. Renovation cuts are fine. Unlike lavender, aucuba regenerates readily from old bare wood. A very overgrown hedge can be cut down by half or more in spring and will re-shoot from the main stems during the same season. Cut it in stages if you are nervous: hard prune one side one year, then the other side the following spring.
  6. Check the gender balance if you want berries. Aucuba is dioecious: berries only appear on female plants, and only when a male plant is nearby. If your hedge is producing no berries and you want them, check whether any male plant is present. If not, plant a 'Rozannie' (self-fertile) or add a male plant within reasonable pollinating distance.
  7. Feed lightly in spring. A balanced slow-release fertiliser in April keeps the foliage glossy and promotes new growth after the pruning cut. Do not feed in autumn.
  8. Collect trimmings promptly. Aucuba clippings are large and take a long time to break down. Compost them or take them to a green waste site. Do not leave them piled against the base of the hedge.

Nesting season: look before you cut

Aucuba is a dense, sheltered evergreen and is a common nesting site for blackbirds, robins and dunnocks. Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to damage or destroy an active nest. Always look carefully into the hedge before cutting, especially in the spring window that overlaps with peak nesting season. Nesting season runs roughly from 1 March to 31 August. The spring pruning window (April to May) falls right in the middle of it: check thoroughly, and if birds are nesting, either trim carefully around the nest or wait until the young have fledged. A late summer trim (August) is sometimes easier for this reason.

Common problems

Black sooty patches on leaves

Usually sooty mould growing on honeydew excreted by scale insects on the stems above. Check the stems for flat, scale-like brown insects clinging to the bark. Treat scale with a horticultural oil in late spring when the juveniles (crawlers) are active. Remove severely affected stems.

Leaf blackening after pruning

Cut leaf edges on aucuba turn black relatively quickly. This is cosmetic rather than a disease, and it is the main reason to use secateurs rather than shears. New leaves produced after pruning will be clean and unmarked.

Thin, yellowing foliage in sun

Aucuba does not enjoy full sun, especially in a south-facing position in East Kent summers. If the hedge is in a sunny spot and showing thin or yellow-green foliage, it is in the wrong location. Consider replacing with a sun-tolerant species and moving the aucuba to a shadier boundary.

Varieties for East Kent

Aucuba japonica 'Crotonifolia'

The most common variegated form, with gold-spotted leaves. Female, so produces red berries when pollinated. The spots are less vivid in deep shade but the plant remains healthy. Very widely available and reliable.

Aucuba japonica 'Rozannie'

Self-fertile female that produces berries without a male nearby. Compact and neat: one of the best choices for a tidy, berry-producing hedge in a shaded spot. Leaves are plain glossy green rather than spotted.

Aucuba japonica 'Salicifolia'

Narrow, lance-shaped plain green leaves giving a more refined look than the broad-leaved spotted forms. Female. A good choice when the spotted form looks too bold for the setting.

Aucuba japonica (plain green)

The plain-leaved species is the most vigorous and reliable in very deep shade. Less decorative than the spotted forms but very tough. If the primary requirement is screening and shade-tolerance rather than ornament, this is the plant.
