Correcting an uneven hedge top: how to get it level again

If your hedge slopes from one end to the other, or has a bump or dip in the middle, here is the straightforward way to set a new reference line and cut it back to level in one season or two.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

An uneven hedge top is almost always the result of small inconsistencies accumulating over several years. One season somebody cuts slightly high on the left, the next person cuts slightly low on the right, and by year four there is a visible slope of 15 to 20 centimetres across a ten-metre run. The good news is it is not a problem with the hedge: the plant is healthy. The problem is geometric, and geometry is fixable with a string line and a bit of patience.

Before you start: safety and timing

PPE. Wear eye protection and gloves before picking up any powered cutting tool. Flying debris is the most common cause of eye injuries in garden work. For powered hedge trimmers, add ear defenders. If the hedge is above shoulder height, use a long-reach or pole trimmer rather than a ladder where possible. If a ladder is unavoidable, have someone footing it and never overreach sideways.

Electric trimmer cable. If you are using a mains-powered trimmer, fit an RCD adaptor at the socket. Running the cable along the ground next to a ladder or sheet creates a trip hazard; lay it away from the working area and keep it visible.

Nesting season. Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is a criminal offence to damage an active bird's nest. The nesting season runs roughly 1 March to 31 August. If you are correcting an uneven top during this window, walk the hedge first and look for nests before cutting. The safe main cutting window is September to February. Most correction work is best done in September or October, when growth has stopped for the season and the reference line you cut to will hold clearly through the following year.

Step 1: Measure and record the unevenness

Before cutting anything, stand at one end of the hedge and look along the top at eye level. Note the highest and lowest points. Then measure the actual height at three or four points along the run: at each end and at least one point in the middle. A tape measure from the ground to the top of the hedge at each station takes two minutes and gives you numbers to work from.

Say the hedge is 1.6 metres at the left end, 1.8 metres at the right end, and 1.75 metres in the middle. The total unevenness is 20 centimetres. The question is: where do you want the finished height to be?

Step 2: Choose the target height

You have two main options. You can cut down to the level of the lowest section (1.6 metres in the example), which corrects everything in a single pass. Or you can split the difference and set a new height between the extremes, reducing the high end and letting the low end grow up to it over a season. The first option is quicker and cleaner. The second is better for species that do not respond well to sudden hard reductions, particularly any conifer.

For most deciduous hedges in Kent, beech, hornbeam, privet, hawthorn: cutting to the lowest existing point in one go is fine provided the hedge is healthy. For leylandii and other conifers, aim to remove no more than one-third of the current height of the high sections in any single cut. Cutting into dead brown wood on a leylandii is almost always permanent: nothing re-grows from it.

If you are dealing with a neighbour's hedge that is affecting light, the relevant legislation is the Anti-social Behaviour Act 2003 (Part 8). See our detailed guide on hedge heights and neighbour law before taking any action that might touch the boundary.

Step 3: Set up the string line

This is the step that makes the difference between an eye-balled correction that introduces new unevenness, and a true level cut. You need:

  • Two garden canes or wooden stakes, at least 30 centimetres taller than your target finished height
  • Builder's line, twine or garden string: strong enough not to stretch mid-cut
  • A spirit level or a line level (a small spirit level that clips to the string)
  • A tape measure

Drive a stake into the ground at each end of the hedge, set back from the hedge face by about 20 centimetres so you can walk past without knocking them. Measure the target height from the ground up each stake and mark it, then tie the string at that mark on each stake. Use a line level clipped to the string in the middle to check that both ends are at the same height. On a long hedge, add a third stake at the midpoint to stop the string sagging under its own weight.

Walk along the string from both ends before cutting. The string should appear as a straight line, not a V shape or a wave. Adjust until it is.

Step 4: Cut down to the string

With the string in place, start at one end and work along the top, keeping the blade or shears just below the string. The string is a ceiling, not a guide you press the tool against: keep the cutting plane parallel to it, not touching it. Pause every two metres and look back along the cut line from behind the hedge to check you are tracking true.

For sections that are well above the string, do a first rough pass that brings them within 5 to 10 centimetres, then do a second finishing pass to the line. Trying to take off 20 centimetres in one pass with a rotary trimmer often results in a wavy surface because the tool deflects slightly on heavy material.

Once the top is cut, leave the string in place and step back to the street or to a point five to ten metres away. Look at the hedge from a distance. Minor inconsistencies are much easier to spot from 10 metres than from 50 centimetres. Mark any high spots and go back to take them down before removing the string.

Step 5: Check from a distance and tidy

After removing the string, check the finished level from several angles: from the front, from each end, and if possible from an upstairs window if the hedge is along a property boundary. Photograph the result so you have a reference for next year's cut.

Clear all clippings from the base of the hedge. On older hedges in Kent gardens where the base is against a fence or wall, clippings left to rot against the wood encourage fungal disease in the hedge and rot in the fence.

Step 6: For severe unevenness, plan a two-season approach

If the hedge is a conifer, if the high sections would require removing more than one-third of the hedge height, or if the hedge was cut heavily last year and needs a rest season: do half the correction now and mark the target height clearly for next year's cut. A simple method is to knock a small nail or clip a cable tie to the stake at the half-way height, then when you return in a year the stakes tell you how far to go next.

Most hedges in Sandwich, Deal and across the coastal strip of East Kent grow particularly fast between April and July thanks to the relatively mild winters. A correction cut in October will hold well through winter, but check the new level again in July of the following year before the second main cut to make sure growth has not already reintroduced variability.
