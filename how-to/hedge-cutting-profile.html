How to cut a hedge to the right profile

The A-frame batter: why hedges must be wider at the base than the top, and the step-by-step for getting that taper right every time. Written by the team that cuts hedges in Sandwich, Deal and across East Kent.

Updated September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Most people who trim their own hedges cut the sides straight up and down. Flat, vertical sides. It looks fine the first year. By year five the bottom half has gone sparse, thin, and bare. The top is lush. The bottom is embarrassing. This is not a disease or a pest problem. It is a geometry problem, and the fix is a small angle.

Professional hedge cutters call that angle the batter, sometimes written as the profile. You trim each side so the base is slightly wider than the top, forming a gentle A-frame cross-section. The difference in width does not need to be dramatic: five to fifteen centimetres each side over a metre of height is typically enough. The effect on the long-term health of the hedge is significant.

Nesting season applies here too

The UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs roughly 1 March to 31 August. The safe cutting window for most hedges is September to February. A light trim during nesting months is only acceptable if you have checked the hedge is genuinely empty first. This guide is written with the September-to-February window in mind.

Before you start: safety

  • Eye protection is not optional. Flying debris from hedge trimmers is a leading cause of DIY eye injuries.
  • Ear defenders for petrol or electric trimmers: sustained exposure at trimmer noise levels causes hearing damage.
  • Sturdy gloves to protect against cut stems, and to keep a grip on the trimmer or shears.
  • Avoid ladders where possible. Ladders are involved in the majority of serious garden accidents. Prefer a long-reach pole trimmer for any hedge above about 1.5 metres. If a ladder is unavoidable, have someone footing it, and never overreach sideways.
  • Electric trimmers: plug into an RCD-protected socket. Keep the cable behind you at all times.
  • Check for wasp nests and brambles before starting, particularly in late summer and autumn when wasp nests are at their largest.

Why the batter matters: light and structure

A hedge is a living wall. Like any wall, it needs light and air to stay in good condition all the way to its base. When you cut sides vertically, the upper growth casts a shadow over the lower growth. Over several seasons the lower leaves get progressively less light. They thin, yellow and fall. You end up with a hedge that is all lush canopy and bare wooden legs.

The angled batter solves this. By making the top narrower than the base, sunlight reaches the face of the hedge at the bottom. Growth remains even from ground level upward. In East Kent gardens, where privet, beech, laurel and yew are common, the difference between a properly battered hedge and a flat-sided one becomes obvious within two or three seasons.

There is a secondary benefit: snow. A hedge with flat sides and a flat top will collect snow like a shelf. Heavy snowfall can splay a formal hedge apart permanently, especially box and privet. The angled profile sheds snow to the sides rather than letting it pile up.

Step 1: set your target width at top and base

Decide how wide the hedge should be at the base and at the top. For a hedge of one metre to 1.5 metres tall, a base width of, say, 70 cm tapering to 50 cm at the top gives a clear, visible taper. For a shorter hedge of 60 to 80 cm, even a 10 cm difference is enough to make a meaningful difference.

Write the numbers down if you tend to forget mid-job. Consistency between one section and the next is what makes the hedge look uniform.

Step 2: use a string line or batter board as a guide

This is the step most people skip, and why most home-trimmed hedges drift over time.

The simplest approach: push two bamboo canes into the ground at either end of the hedge run. Run a string from cane to cane at the height you want the base of the taper to sit, then run a second, shorter string at the top, set inward by the desired taper amount. You now have two angled lines to cut between. Do this on each side.

A batter board is a piece of plywood or timber cut to the exact target cross-section of the hedge. You hold it against the hedge face as a moveable template. Cut everything flush with the edge of the board, move the board along, repeat. It takes a few minutes to make but is extremely accurate and useful if you trim the same hedge every year.

Step 3: cut the top first

Always start at the top. Set your string line or use a spirit level across two canes to give you a flat horizontal reference. Hold the trimmer flat and sweep it in smooth passes from one end to the other. Do not dig in at any point: keep the blade skimming the surface. Cutting across the full length before moving to the sides means any large clippings fall clear of your work area.

Step 4: cut each side with the correct taper

Hold the trimmer at the angle that matches your target batter. On most domestic hedges this is a shallow angle, perhaps 5 to 15 degrees from vertical. Sweep upward from base to top on each pass rather than pushing down, so that if the blade snags, it moves away from you rather than toward your legs.

Start at the base of the hedge and work upward in overlapping passes. Check the taper frequently against your string line or batter board. It is much easier to take a little more off than to try to correct a section that has been cut too deep.

Step 5: step back and check from a distance

Ten metres back is the minimum. Up close, small irregularities are invisible. From a distance, any section cut steeper or shallower than the rest stands out clearly. Touch up those sections with a light second pass rather than re-cutting the whole thing.

A useful habit: check the taper at multiple points along the hedge at eye level. Crouch down and look along the base to confirm it is wider than the top face, uniformly, along the full length.

Step 6: clear up

Sheet on the ground before you start makes clearing easy: pull the sheet away with all the clippings on it. Otherwise, rake clippings off the lawn and borders promptly. Wet clippings from laurel and privet in particular will scorch grass if left sitting. For what to do with the resulting clippings, see our guide to composting hedge trimmings.

Species notes for Kent hedges

Privet (very common in Sandwich, Deal and Worth): responds well to the batter and recovers quickly from being cut back to achieve it. Fast-growing, so the taper may need re-establishing each season.

Beech and hornbeam: slow to show the benefit, but the batter prevents the characteristic bare lower stems that beech hedges develop when cut flat-sided for years. Cut late July to August for the annual trim.

Yew: tolerates quite hard shaping, so establishing a proper batter is straightforward. Slow growth means the profile holds well from year to year.

Laurel (also common around Woodnesborough and Ash): use secateurs for large-leaved varieties to avoid the shredded-leaf look, but the batter principle is exactly the same. The wide base is particularly useful for laurel, which tends toward bare lower stems if flat-sided.

Leylandii and conifers: the batter is essential, not just helpful. Conifers will not regrow from brown wood, so the lower sections must get enough light to stay green. Never cut into the brown and always maintain the taper. See our leylandii guide for the additional rules on this species.

Common mistakes

  • Flat sides. Looks tidy for a year or two. Then the bottom goes bald.
  • Top too wide. If the top is wider than the base, the hedge is upside down and will shadow the bottom even more aggressively.
  • Inconsistent angle. Sections cut at different tapers look lumpy. Use a guide.
  • Not checking from distance. You cannot see the profile accurately from one metre away.
  • Cutting too deep in one go. Little and often is better than one severe cut, which can stress the plant and leave ugly gaps.
