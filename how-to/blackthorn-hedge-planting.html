How to plant a blackthorn hedge in Kent

Blackthorn is one of the best-value native hedging plants for East Kent: cheap as bare-root whips, wildlife-rich, and so thorny that nothing gets through it once established. Getting it off to a good start is straightforward if you respect the thorns.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Blackthorn (Prunus spinosa) is a native British shrub that has been used for field boundaries in Kent for centuries. The hedges along the old drove roads north of Sandwich and in the lanes around Worth and Eastry contain blackthorn that is probably hundreds of years old. As a garden hedge it offers a dense, impenetrable barrier, abundant spring blossom before the leaves appear, sloe berries in autumn, and outstanding wildlife value throughout the year.

November to March is the bare-root planting window. Bare-root blackthorn is inexpensive and available from most UK hedging nurseries in this period. Planting in November or December gives the longest possible time to establish before summer.

Thorn safety: take this seriously

Blackthorn thorns are hard, sharply pointed and can cause serious infections if a tip breaks off under the skin. The injury often looks minor at the time. Days later, the puncture site can become swollen, hot and painful, requiring antibiotic treatment. Wear thick leather gloves throughout all handling, planting and pruning work. Keep a close eye on any thorn puncture for signs of infection in the days afterwards.

Step 1: Choose and order your plants

Bare-root blackthorn whips are sold in 40–60 cm or 60–90 cm sizes. Smaller whips are cheaper, easier to handle and often establish more vigorously than taller stock. For a garden boundary, 40–60 cm whips are the practical choice. Order from a reputable UK hedging nursery. When the plants arrive, check that the roots are moist and the stems are not dried out. Plant within a day or two. If you cannot plant immediately, heel the plants into a shaded corner with roots covered in damp soil.

Step 2: Prepare the ground

Mark the planting line with a string line or canes. Strip the turf along a 60 cm-wide strip and remove any perennial weeds, especially bindweed and couch grass. These will compete hard with young plants in year one. Fork over the soil to 25–30 cm depth. Blackthorn is adaptable and grows on most Kent soils: chalk, clay, and the loamy soils around Sandwich Bay. It is one of the least fussy native hedging plants going.

On thin chalk soil, work some garden compost into the trench bottom. It improves moisture retention in the first summer, which is when young plants are most vulnerable. Blackthorn does not need rich soil; it just needs not to dry out completely in its first season.

Step 3: Dig the planting trench

For a row of plants, a continuous trench is quicker and ensures even treatment along the whole line. Dig 25–30 cm wide and 20–25 cm deep. For small groups or individual plants, individual holes are fine: dig each to about 1.5 times the root spread. Keep the soil to one side.

Step 4: Set the spacing

For a dense garden boundary hedge, plant 3 to 5 plants per metre: at roughly 25–30 cm spacing in a single row. A double staggered row fills in faster but complicates management later when the suckering growth starts to spread. For a rural field boundary where security matters most, the closer spacing is worth the extra plants.

In mixed native hedges, blackthorn is typically interspersed with hawthorn, field maple and hazel at similar spacings. If you are planting a mix, aim for roughly one blackthorn in every three plants, or more if security is the priority.

Step 5: Plant at the right depth

The root collar (where the stem meets the roots) should sit at soil level, not buried below it. Fan the roots out in the trench bottom. Backfill with the excavated soil, firming it in with your boot as you go to remove air pockets. Wear your thick leather gloves throughout this stage: the thorns on even small blackthorn whips are sharp and plentiful.

Step 6: Formative cut immediately after planting

This is the step most people skip, and it matters. Immediately after planting, cut each whip back by roughly a third to a half. This seems counterintuitive but it is the right thing to do. It forces growth from the base, which is what produces a dense, bushy hedge rather than a leggy, open one. A hedge planted without this formative cut will often develop a sparse base that is difficult to correct later.

Use clean, sharp secateurs. The cut can be diagonal, just above an outward-facing bud. Dispose of the cut material carefully and do not leave it where children or animals might tread on the thorns.

Step 7: Water in and mulch

Even in November, water in well after planting. Then apply a 10 cm layer of bark mulch along the full planting strip, keeping it 5 cm clear of the stems. The mulch holds moisture, suppresses grass and weeds and moderates soil temperature. It matters most in the first summer if the weather turns dry.

In gardens bordering fields around Sandwich, Worth and Ash, fit rabbit guards if rabbits are present. Rabbits will strip the bark from bare-root whips in winter. Even a simple spiral guard on each plant gives adequate protection.

Blackthorn and the nesting season

Planting is fine year-round. All trimming and shaping, however, must respect the bird nesting season. Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to damage or destroy an active nest. Blackthorn is an outstanding nesting plant for whitethroats, linnets and other species. Nesting season runs approximately 1 March to 31 August. The safe trimming window is September to February.

What to expect in years one to three

Year one: not much visible. The plants put energy into roots, not top growth. Some may look almost dead by July. This is usually not a cause for alarm. Water during dry spells and keep the mulch topped up. Year two: noticeably more growth, the first real impression of a hedge forming. Year three: the plants begin to knit together and the hedge starts to take on its own character, particularly once the suckering growth begins to fill lateral gaps.

By year four or five, a properly planted blackthorn hedge in East Kent is effectively self-sufficient. It needs an annual trim between September and February to keep it at the right size, and occasional spade work to manage suckers that emerge outside the intended line.
