Mulching hedges: feed the root zone, suppress weeds and keep moisture in

One barrow of mulch per season makes more difference to a hedge's health than most feeding programmes. Here is what to use, when to apply it and what to avoid.

Updated September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Mulching hedges: the six-point checklist

  1. Timing: apply in late autumn (October to November) or early spring (February to March).
  2. Clear first: pull or hoe weeds from the base of the hedge before mulching.
  3. Feed first if needed: sprinkle a slow-release fertiliser before the mulch layer if the hedge is struggling.
  4. Depth: 5 to 8 centimetres of mulch across a 30 to 60 centimetre-wide strip along the base.
  5. Keep clear of stems: leave a 5-centimetre gap around each stem to prevent collar rot.
  6. Repeat annually or every two years: bark chips last two to three seasons; compost breaks down in one.

Why the root zone matters

Most hedge care focuses on what happens above ground: when to cut, how hard to go, which tool to use. The root zone rarely gets attention unless something is visibly wrong. That is a missed opportunity. A hedge's roots live in the top 30 to 50 centimetres of soil, and in many Kent gardens that zone is compacted, weed-infested and drying out faster than it should.

Mulch addresses three problems at once. It suppresses weed growth that competes directly with hedge roots for water and nutrients. It holds moisture in the soil during dry spells, which matters in East Kent where summer droughts have become longer and more frequent. And as it breaks down it feeds the soil biology: the worms, fungi and bacteria that convert organic matter into nutrients the hedge can actually use.

In Sandwich Bay and along the Deal seafront, free-draining sandy soils lose moisture particularly quickly. In the heavier clay soils around Ash, Eastry and Woodnesborough, the problem is the opposite: waterlogging in winter and a hard surface crust in summer that sheds rainfall rather than absorbing it. Mulch helps in both cases by improving soil structure over time.

Checklist detail

1. Timing: autumn is best, spring works too

The ideal window is late October to November, after the main autumn cut and before hard ground frosts. The soil is still warm enough for roots to keep growing slowly, and the mulch provides insulation as temperatures drop. A late-winter or early-spring application (February to March) is nearly as good: you are protecting roots from the last frosts and setting the hedge up for the growing season.

Avoid applying mulch in midsummer to dry soil. It acts as a barrier that keeps moisture out rather than in until the next proper rain.

2. Clear weeds first

There is no point mulching over established perennial weeds. Ground elder, couch grass, bindweed and dock will push straight through 8 centimetres of bark chip within a season. Spend ten minutes pulling or hoeing annual weeds and, where perennial weeds are present, either remove the roots by hand or apply a targeted weedkiller and wait before mulching.

3. Feed before you mulch if the hedge is struggling

Mulch and fertiliser are not the same thing. An established, healthy hedge in good soil may need no fertiliser once you are mulching regularly with compost. But a newly planted hedge, a thin or pale hedge, or one recovering from hard renovation will benefit from a handful of balanced slow-release granules worked into the soil surface before the mulch goes on top. See our guide to feeding hedges for more detail.

4. Depth and width

Aim for 5 to 8 centimetres deep across a strip roughly 30 to 60 centimetres out from the base of the hedge on each side. A thick layer is more effective than a thin one, but anything over 10 to 12 centimetres can impede drainage and create humid conditions that encourage stem rot and slug activity. The strip width matters: hedge roots extend well beyond the visible canopy drip-line, so a wider strip helps more than a deeper layer.

5. Leave a gap around each stem

Keep the mulch 5 centimetres or so away from the base of individual stems and the main trunk line. Mulch piled against stems holds moisture against the bark, which encourages collar rot and can introduce fungal disease. This is particularly important for privet, laurel and yew, all common in Kent gardens.

6. Which mulch to use

Well-rotted garden compost is the most beneficial for soil health. It breaks down quickly and feeds the hedge as it does. The downside is that it needs replacing every season and is sometimes in short supply if you are composting for a large garden.

Bark chips are practical, widely available from garden centres around Sandwich, Deal and Canterbury, and last two to three seasons before needing topping up. They give a tidy finish along a hedge border. Choose a medium-grade chip: very fine bark breaks down quickly and mats together; very coarse bark looks out of scale in a domestic garden.

Composted wood chip (often sold as "soil improver" or available free from tree surgeons) is excellent where you can get it. It adds organic matter and improves soil structure over time.

Composted hedge clippings work well once fully rotted, usually after six to twelve months in a heap. Fresh clippings should not be used as mulch directly: the green material compacts and creates a dense mat. See our composting guide for how to turn clippings into usable material.

Avoid mushroom compost around acid-loving plants (rhododendron, pieris, camellia) as it is slightly alkaline. And avoid fresh grass clippings in thick layers: they compact quickly, overheat and create a barrier.

A note on newly planted hedges

Mulching matters most in the first two or three years after planting, when the hedge is establishing its root system and has no tolerance for competition or water stress. A 60-centimetre-wide mulch strip applied immediately after planting and topped up each autumn makes a measurable difference to how quickly a new hedge closes up into a solid screen. See our newly planted hedge care guide for the full establishment routine.

The RHS on choosing mulch materials

The Royal Horticultural Society's YouTube channel covers mulch types and application in practical detail. Their video "Choosing and using garden mulches" is a useful companion to this guide, covering the range of materials available and how different mulch types affect soil structure. The RHS is the UK's leading gardening charity and the video was published in December 2024. You can find it by searching "Choosing and using garden mulches RHS" on YouTube (video ID: 0LzKGDPPFpk on the RHS Royal Horticultural Society channel).
