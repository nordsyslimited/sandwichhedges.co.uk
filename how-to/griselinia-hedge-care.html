Griselinia hedge care in Kent

One cut a year, tolerance for salt wind and a near-indifference to coastal exposure. Griselinia is the hedge we recommend most often for seafront and exposed village gardens in East Kent.

Updated August 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Griselinia littoralis is native to New Zealand, but you would not know it to look at East Kent's coastal gardens. The pale apple-green, slightly waxy oval leaves shrug off salt spray, the plant establishes quickly in sandy and chalky soils, and the mild winters along the coast of Sandwich Bay and Deal mean it rarely suffers winter damage. It grows at a measured pace, typically 30 to 45 centimetres a year once established, and needs just one cut to stay tidy.

Where other hedges struggle in exposed positions, griselinia gets on with it. That is not an accident of geography: its toughness in maritime conditions is exactly why it colonised coastal settlements across Britain after its introduction in the Victorian era.

Nesting season: check before you cut

The UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs roughly 1 March to 31 August. The main cutting window recommended here, late June to July, falls within nesting season, so a careful check before cutting is essential: walk the hedge, look from underneath, and watch for birds flying in and out. If the hedge is clearly occupied, wait until August at the earliest. The fully safe cutting window for most hedges is September to February.

Before you start: safety

Before you start the cut

Wear eye protection against flying fragments and sturdy gloves. Griselinia is easy to cut with most tools, but loose debris can reach your eyes quickly when trimming. For hedges above chest height, use a long-reach pole trimmer from the ground rather than a ladder wherever possible. If a ladder is needed, have someone footing it and do not overreach sideways. Plug any corded electric trimmer into an RCD adaptor and keep the cable visible behind you. Check for wasp nests, particularly at the base of the hedge, before starting.

Step 1: time your annual trim for late June or July

One cut a year is enough for most griselinia hedges. Late June to July is the practical sweet spot: the main spring growth flush is complete, nesting season is winding down for most species (though always check first), and the hedge has time to tidy up before winter.

If the hedge has put on a lot of growth and looks untidy again by September, a light tidy then is perfectly fine. Griselinia is not sensitive about the timing of a second light pass in the way that some species are.

Avoid cutting in early spring when the new growth is still very soft. It is more easily torn than cut cleanly and the ragged result takes longer to recover.

Step 2: choose your tool

A hedge trimmer (cordless battery or petrol) is the standard choice for griselinia, and it works well. The leaves are smallish and the growth is not so coarse that the trimmer blades struggle. The result is clean and fast.

Hand shears give a slightly neater finish on a formal tight shape, but for most garden hedges the trimmer is fine. Unlike laurel, where large leaves shredded by a trimmer turn brown at the edges for weeks, griselinia's smaller leaves do not cause the same issue.

For renovation cuts on thick old stems, use loppers or a pruning saw rather than forcing the trimmer through oversized wood.

Step 3: how hard can you cut it?

Griselinia tolerates moderately hard cuts, including into old wood. It is not as reliable at regenerating from very old, thick stems as leylandii or laurel, but it will produce new growth from stems of a couple of years' maturity without difficulty.

For routine annual maintenance, trim back the current season's growth by about two-thirds, keeping a consistent outline. This produces a dense, compact hedge over the years.

For renovation of a hedge that has grown significantly out of shape, cut back in stages over two years rather than taking everything back hard in one go: take the top and sides back substantially in year one, and tidy the remainder in year two once new growth has confirmed the plant's recovery. See our guide to renovating a neglected hedge for the general approach.

Step 4: after the cut

Newly planted griselinia (within the first two years) needs watering during dry spells, particularly on the sandy and chalky soils common around Sandwich and Eastry, which drain quickly and can leave young roots dry. Once established, griselinia is reasonably drought-tolerant and will manage through a normal English summer without supplemental watering.

A balanced slow-release fertiliser in spring helps hedges that have been left a few years without feeding, or those on thin, free-draining chalk soils. An annual mulch of garden compost around the base, not pressed against the stems, improves moisture retention on sandy ground.

Common problems

Brown leaves after frost. Griselinia is hardy to around minus 10 degrees Celsius, which covers virtually all East Kent winters, particularly in coastal positions. In hard inland winters or very exposed sites, the outer leaves can scorch and turn brown. This looks alarming but is rarely fatal: the plant will push out new growth in spring and the browned outer layer will be covered. Trim off the worst of the damaged material in March once the risk of hard frost has passed.

Brown patches after very windy weather. On exposed seafront positions at Deal or Sandwich Bay, the windward face can look scorched after severe gales. This is salt wind scorch, not disease. New growth will cover it, and griselinia's tolerance of maritime conditions means this is usually a cosmetic issue rather than a plant health problem. If the site is extremely exposed, consider an initial windbreak of Leyland cypress or woven willow fencing on the worst-facing side until the griselinia establishes fully.

Yellowing leaves. Pale or yellowing leaves on an established griselinia usually point to waterlogged roots rather than disease. Griselinia does not tolerate heavy, compacted or waterlogged soil well. If the planting site holds water after rain, improving drainage before planting would have been the solution; for an established hedge in this situation, improving drainage around the root zone (forking in grit or organic matter) and reducing competing vegetation can help.

Neighbour and height considerations

Griselinia grows at a moderate pace and does not usually cause the hedge-height disputes that leylandii provokes, but an untrimmed griselinia will eventually reach 4 to 8 metres and beyond. If a neighbour's griselinia hedge is already blocking light, our guide to hedge height law in Kent covers the formal steps available.

Griselinia in East Kent: why it works here

East Kent's coastal strip, from Sandwich Bay through Deal down towards Dover, has conditions that suit griselinia particularly well: mild winters moderated by the sea, sandy and chalky free-draining soils, and exposure to salt-laden Channel winds that would trouble many other species. Gardeners in these exposed seafront positions often find that griselinia outperforms privet, beech and hornbeam for straightforward year-round cover. Inland around Ash, Worth and Woodnesborough, where frost risk is slightly higher, griselinia still performs well but a sheltered spot is worth seeking if available.

If a garden is within a kilometre of the sea, our first recommendation for a new boundary hedge is nearly always griselinia or escallonia. Both take the salt. Both are low-maintenance. Both earn their keep.
