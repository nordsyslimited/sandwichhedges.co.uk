Phytophthora root rot in hedges: diagnosing and managing this common killer

A section of your laurel or leylandii hedge has gone brown from the bottom up. The roots look rotten. Here is how to work out whether phytophthora is responsible, and what your options actually are.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Phytophthora is not a true fungus. It is a water mould, in the same kingdom as algae. The distinction matters because fungicides used against true fungi are largely ineffective against it, and because it spreads through water in the soil rather than primarily through air. Those two facts shape everything about how you deal with it.

Several phytophthora species cause root rot in garden hedges. The two most common in British gardens are Phytophthora cinnamomi, which attacks a wide range of species on poorly drained soils, and Phytophthora ramorum, which causes the dieback known as sudden oak death and also damages rhododendron, viburnum and some other hedging plants. Both can be present without being immediately obvious, and by the time the foliage tells you something is wrong, the root system is typically already badly damaged.

Decision guide: working out what you have

Use this sequence to narrow down the cause before doing anything else.

Is the dieback starting from the base of the plant or from one side?

Dieback from the base upward, or in a section of a hedge affecting multiple consecutive plants, points toward a root or soil problem. Dieback from one side only, or from exposed tips, is more likely to be weather, drought or spray damage. If the base is affected, continue through the guide below. If only the tips or one face is affected, see the hedge health troubleshooting guide for wind scorch, frost and drought symptoms first.

Check the roots and stem base

Dig carefully around one of the affected plants and expose the main roots and the stem base, down to 15 to 20 centimetres. What you find at this point tells you a great deal.

Comparing the visible signs of phytophthora root rot and honey fungus
What you see Points toward
Roots dark brown, water-soaked, rotted at the tips; bark at stem base discoloured, often reddish-brown internally Phytophthora root rot
Black, bootlace-like strands (rhizomorphs) under the bark at the base; white mycelium fan when bark is peeled back; toadstools in autumn near the plant Honey fungus
Roots pale and sparse but not rotten; soil compacted and waterlogged Waterlogging alone (phytophthora may follow if susceptible species)
Roots pale, fine and underdeveloped; no dieback pattern Vine weevil, rootfeeder damage or nutrient deficiency

If you see dark, water-soaked root rot with no bootlaces and no white mycelium, phytophthora is the most likely cause. For more on distinguishing the two diseases, see the honey fungus in hedges guide.

Check the conditions

Phytophthora thrives in wet, poorly drained soils. If the affected area sits in a natural low point where water pools after rain, or the clay subsoil prevents drainage, the conditions are right. In inland East Kent, heavy clay soils around Ash, Woodnesborough and Eastry hold water through the autumn and winter in a way that the lighter soils near the coast do not. This makes clay-soil gardens significantly more vulnerable to phytophthora on susceptible species.

Check the species

Not all hedging plants are equally susceptible. If the dying plants are cherry laurel or Portugal laurel, leylandii, or yew, phytophthora is a plausible cause. If they are privet, beech, hornbeam or hawthorn, the cause is more likely to be something else. Cherry laurel is the species we most commonly see with confirmed phytophthora dieback in Kent gardens.

What you can do

The honest answer is: not a great deal, once infection is established. There are no soil treatments licensed for domestic garden use that reliably eliminate phytophthora from the soil. The practical options are set out below.

Improve drainage

Phytophthora cannot spread through dry soil. If the site drains well, the pathogen is contained. If it does not, improving drainage is the single most useful intervention you can make. For a hedge on a low-lying clay site, a French drain or perforated pipe alongside the hedge root run, discharging to a soakaway away from the hedge, can significantly slow the progression of disease even in an already-infected site. This is not a cure but it may save neighbouring plants.

Remove infected material carefully

Remove affected plants, including as much of the root ball as possible, and dispose of them in household waste. Do not compost them. Do not use a chipper on infected root material: the resulting mulch will spread the pathogen. Bag root material separately and bin it.

After removal, disinfect all tools that contacted the infected root ball (spades, shovels, gloves) with a 1:10 bleach solution or a proprietary horticultural disinfectant. Remove soil from footwear before walking elsewhere in the garden. The pathogen moves in water and in soil attached to tools, boots and vehicle tyres.

Wait before replanting

Replanting a susceptible species immediately into the same location is likely to result in the same outcome. Wait at least two to three growing seasons. Improve drainage during that period. Consider whether the site will ever drain well enough for laurel or leylandii.

Choose a resistant replacement species

For East Kent conditions on heavy soils, field maple (Acer campestre), hornbeam (Carpinus betulus), hawthorn (Crataegus monogyna) and native blackthorn (Prunus spinosa) are all significantly more tolerant of wet soils and phytophthora pressure than laurel or leylandii. They are also excellent for wildlife. For a formal evergreen screen where laurel or leylandii was originally chosen, western red cedar (Thuja plicata) or the cultivar 'Green Giant' is more resistant to phytophthora than leylandii on damp sites, though no species is fully immune. See the renovating a neglected hedge guide for replanting advice more broadly.

When to call a professional

If you have a valuable mature yew hedge showing base dieback, or a long laurel hedge where multiple plants are dying progressively, it is worth having the diagnosis confirmed before removing everything. An arboricultural consultant can take soil and root samples for laboratory phytophthora testing, which costs more than a site visit but rules out other causes and may change the management decision significantly.
