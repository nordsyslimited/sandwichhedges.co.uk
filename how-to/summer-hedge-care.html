Summer hedge care: June, July and August

Most of summer is a holding pattern for hedge work. Here is the checklist of what you actually can do, what you should leave alone, and how to set yourself up for the main autumn cut.

Updated August 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Summer hedge care: the quick checklist

  • Check for active bird nests before touching any hedge
  • Light trim of fast-growing species in June, if demonstrably empty
  • Feed with a balanced fertiliser if you have not done so this year
  • Water newly planted hedges (under three years old) weekly through dry spells
  • Inspect for aphids, scale insect and box tree moth caterpillars
  • Clear weeds at the base and refresh the mulch layer to about 10 cm depth
  • Book your main September cut before the diary fills up

Summer is the strangest part of the hedge-care calendar. The plants are growing hard, but you mostly cannot cut them. Most of the work in June, July and early August is observation, feeding, watering and keeping the base clear rather than anything with a blade. There is a narrow window for a light trim, and a firm rule that governs it. Here is how we handle summer across the gardens we maintain in Sandwich, Deal, Worth, Ash, Woodnesborough and Eastry.

Nesting season: the rule everything else works around

The UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs roughly from 1 March to 31 August. That covers the entire summer. Before you touch a hedge with anything sharp, check it properly. Stand back, watch for five minutes, look for birds flying in and out of the same spot. If there is any sign of occupation, leave it alone. The safe main cutting window for most hedges is September to February. See our full guide to when to trim hedges in Kent for the month-by-month picture.

Before you start any trimming work

  • PPE: safety glasses (flying debris is the most common cause of eye injury), sturdy gloves, ear defenders for any powered trimmer
  • Electric trimmers: always use an RCD (residual current device) adaptor when running a corded electric trimmer from a mains socket. Check the cable is behind you and trail it away from the cutting direction
  • Ladders: a pole or long-reach trimmer is almost always safer than a ladder for taller hedges. If a ladder is necessary, have someone footing it and never overreach sideways
  • Check for wasp nests and brambles: both are common inside established hedges and both catch people out in summer when growth is dense

June: the light trim window

June is the month where a light tidy is genuinely useful for fast-growing species. Privet in particular can put on 30 cm or more in a few weeks in a good growing season. Left until September, it will have lost most of its shape and feel like a much bigger job. A quick trim in early June, with a second pass in late August, makes the main autumn cut straightforward.

The same logic applies to photinia (Red Robin), box and hornbeam. These are all species where a June tidy keeps things neat without harming the plant or removing new growth that matters.

The discipline is: check the hedge is empty first. Really check. Stand back, watch, look for nest material or birds moving in and out. If you see anything, stop. Come back in three or four weeks and check again. A light trim is only worth the risk of a fine if the hedge is clearly free.

Laurel is one to leave until late summer. It grows in broad leaves rather than small ones, and shears tend to cut through the middle of leaves rather than between them, leaving brown-edged stumps. It looks much better if you use secateurs to remove individual stems rather than running a trimmer over it in full leaf. Late August, when growth has slowed, is the time for laurel.

July: stop cutting, start observing

For most hedges, July is a month of watching rather than doing. Late nesters (pigeons, house sparrows, and a handful of other species) can still be active, so this is not a month for anything other than the lightest tidy on hedges you are certain are empty.

What July is good for is spotting problems while the hedge is in full leaf. Walk the length of each hedge and look for:

  • Aphids: clusters of small insects, often green or black, on young shoot tips. A strong jet of water from a hose removes most of them without the need for chemicals. See our guide to treating hedge aphids for the full detail.
  • Box tree moth caterpillars: if you have box hedges, check inside the hedge for fine webbing and bright green droppings. An infestation can strip a hedge in weeks. See our guide to box tree moth caterpillar damage.
  • Early signs of honey fungus: yellowing patches that do not recover with water, sometimes with white fungal material at the base. See our honey fungus guide.
  • Drought stress: wilting or yellowing that is not caused by pests. Newly planted hedges in particular need regular water through a dry July. See our guide to hedge care during summer drought.

Feeding in summer

If you did not feed your hedges in spring, a balanced granular fertiliser applied in June or early July is better than leaving it until next year. Slow-release pellets (a general-purpose 7:7:7 NPK or similar) worked into the soil at the base of the hedge and watered in give the plants nutrition for the rest of the growing season.

Avoid feeding after mid-July. Late feeding pushes soft new growth that will not harden before autumn frosts, making it vulnerable to cold damage. In East Kent, mild coastal winters mean this is less of a problem near the coast, but gardens inland around Worth, Ash and Eastry can see frosts from October onwards. If you miss the window, wait until February or March next year.

Watering

Established hedges (over three years old with settled root systems) rarely need supplementary watering in a typical East Kent summer, even through a dry spell. They will look stressed in a long drought but usually recover once rain returns.

Hedges planted in the last one to three years are different. Their root systems have not extended far enough to find deep moisture, and a hot dry July can cause real damage or death in young plants. Aim for a thorough soak once a week rather than light daily sprinkles. Water at the base, not the foliage, and do it in the evening when evaporation is slower.

For detail on managing hedges through a dry summer, see our guide to hedge care in summer drought.

August: the green light, gradually

Late August is when summer hedge care gives way to the start of the main cutting season. By mid-to-late August, most birds have fledged. Fast growers can take a proper trim now, and this is also the best time for a first proper cut on species like yew, which prefers a single annual trim rather than multiple light passes.

The East Kent coastal climate helps here. Mild temperatures through August mean hedges trimmed in late August have time to harden the cut edges before any cold weather arrives. A late August cut on privet, leylandii, laurel, photinia and beech gives the hedge one last surge of growth that fills in the face before winter, then stops.

Check the hedge one more time before you start, even in late August. Pigeons in particular can have a third or fourth brood late in the season. If there is any sign of occupation, wait another two weeks. September is just around the corner.

What to do at the base of the hedge in summer

The one area where you can work freely throughout summer is the soil at the base of the hedge. Weeds compete with the hedge for moisture and nutrients, particularly damaging in dry conditions. Pull them before they set seed. Spreading a 10 cm layer of mulch (bark chip, well-rotted compost or woodchip) along the full length of the hedge suppresses weeds, retains moisture and gradually improves the soil. Keep the mulch a few centimetres away from the stems to prevent rot at the base.

Planning the main autumn cut

Summer is also a good time to decide what needs doing in September. Is the hedge genuinely just a tidy, or has it grown out of shape and needs a renovation cut? If it has been left for more than two years, you might want to start the reduction process in September rather than waiting until next winter. Our guide to reducing an oversized hedge covers the staged approach for hedges that have got significantly out of hand.

If you would rather have professionals do it, booking in June or July for a September slot is sensible. By August, the diary for September tends to be full for most contractors across Sandwich and Deal.
