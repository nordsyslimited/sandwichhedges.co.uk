Building a traditional hedge bank in Kent

The hedge bank is one of the oldest boundary techniques in England: a raised earth mound with a ditch alongside and a hedge on top. It improves drainage, keeps stock in, and creates a dense wildlife habitat in one job. Here is how to build one from scratch.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Hedge banks are common on farmland across Kent and East Sussex, particularly on the clay soils around Ash, Woodnesborough and the Stour valley. You see them along old parish boundaries and field margins where the ditch-and-bank combination solved two problems at once: how to get soil out of a waterlogged field edge, and how to create a barrier that stock could not push through.

The principle is straightforward. You excavate a ditch along the outer edge of the boundary line; the soil from the ditch is heaped up on the inside to form the bank. The hedge is planted along the top. The ditch carries surface water away, the raised root zone keeps hedging plants above the saturation point in winter, and the bank itself becomes a hibernation and nesting site for reptiles, small mammals and invertebrates.

For a garden boundary, you are unlikely to be building a full agricultural-scale bank (1.2m wide at the base, 1m high). A modest version, 60-70cm high and 70-80cm wide, will still raise the hedge roots above standing water and give you the characteristic ridge-and-ditch profile.

Before you start

  • Know your boundary. Agree the precise line with any adjoining landowner before you mark out. A hedge bank that encroaches on a neighbour's land, even by 20cm, can cause disputes that are very difficult to resolve later.
  • Check for buried services. Gas, electric and water pipes often run along boundary lines. Call before you dig (the free Dial Before You Dig service) if you are not certain of the routing. Even in gardens, old drains run close to fences and walls.
  • Back and spade safety. Bank-building is heavy digging. Work in short sessions, keep the spade handle upright rather than scooping at an angle, and do not try to do the whole run in a day. A wheelbarrow makes the soil-movement stage much easier and reduces the number of lifts.
  • Highway boundary check. If your boundary is adjacent to a road or public footpath, check whether any excavation near the highway requires the highway authority's consent. A modest garden bank well set back from the road is unlikely to need anything, but it is worth confirming.

Step 1: Mark out the line

Use canes and string to mark the centreline of the bank. Decide which side the ditch will be on (usually the outer or downhill side) and mark a second line 60-70cm outward from the centreline to show the outer edge of the ditch. The bank itself will sit on the inner side.

For a garden boundary, a bank of 5-10 metres is a reasonable first project. Longer runs are proportionally more work and best approached in stages.

Step 2: Strip and stack the turf

Strip the turf from both the bank area and the ditch area. Stack it face-down in a separate pile: stacked turf breaks down into useful loam over six to twelve months and can be used to finish the bank surface or improve planting holes elsewhere. Do not bury turf in the bank itself; it will decompose and cause settlement.

Step 3: Excavate the ditch

Dig the ditch along the outer edge: typically 45-60cm deep and 50-60cm wide, with clean near-vertical sides. The shape does not need to be a precise V; a slightly rounded U profile is fine. What matters is that the ditch is deep enough to intercept surface water running along the boundary line.

On Kent chalk soils, particularly around Eastry and Worth, you will often hit chalk within 30-40cm. This is not a problem for the ditch, but it does mean the bank will be built partly from chalk subsoil, which is less fertile. Mix in the stripped topsoil when you dress the bank surface.

Step 4: Build the bank

Heap the ditch excavation on the inner side, shaping it into a rounded mound with sides at roughly 45 degrees. For a 60-70cm bank, the base width will be approximately 70-80cm. Compact as you go by treading the layers firm. Work in roughly 15cm lifts, compacting each before adding the next.

Top-dress with the reserved topsoil (not the turf, which you have stacked separately). The finished surface should be firm enough to walk on without sinking more than 2-3cm.

Step 5: Allow to settle before planting

Ideally, build the bank in October and allow it to settle for three to four weeks before planting. By November the bank will have consolidated enough for the hedge roots to find purchase. If you build in late November and want to plant in the same bare-root season, you can do so, but expect slightly more settlement movement in the first year. Drive your stakes in firmly to compensate.

Step 6: Plant the hedge

Plant bare-root hedging along the top of the bank at the standard spacing for the species you have chosen. For a stock-proof or wildlife-rich hedge, a staggered double row at 30-40cm spacing works well. For a more formal boundary hedge, a single row at 45cm is adequate.

Species for Kent chalk and clay banks:

  • Hawthorn (Crataegus monogyna) is the backbone of most Kent hedgerow banks. Fast-establishing, stock-proof when cut hard, and excellent for birds.
  • Blackthorn (Prunus spinosa) spreads by root suckers and fills gaps quickly on a bank. Very effective stock barrier. Keep it in check or it colonises the bank sides.
  • Field maple (Acer campestre) provides good autumn colour and structure, tolerates chalk well, and responds to hard cutting without dieback.
  • Hazel (Corylus avellana) is less stock-proof but very wildlife-rich, and grows quickly. Good for the inner section of a mixed bank hedge.
  • Dog rose (Rosa canina) and wild privet (Ligustrum vulgare) are good gap-fillers in a mixed hedge on chalk soils. Dog rose provides hips from September.

Step 7: Protect and water in

Fit rabbit guards to each plant if rabbits are present (they are common on the downland edge around Eastry and Woodnesborough). Water in well and mulch the top of the bank with a 7cm layer of bark chips. This is particularly important on a bank, where the exposed, well-drained surface dries out faster than flat ground in a dry spring.

Keep the ditch clear of leaf debris in the first winter so it drains freely. A blocked ditch behind a fresh bank is counterproductive.

Nesting season: plan the groundworks for autumn

Bank construction involves disrupting ground-level vegetation and soil, which can disturb ground-nesting birds and reptile hibernation sites. Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, disturbing active nests is an offence. Nesting season runs from 1 March to 31 August. November and December are the ideal months for bank construction: birds have finished nesting, reptiles are retreating to hibernation spots, and the soil is still workable before hard frosts.

Maintaining the bank hedge

Hedge banks are managed the same way as any mixed native hedge: cut between September and February, avoid cutting every year on the same face, and allow some sections to grow into shrubs or small trees periodically to maintain the habitat value. The bank itself needs almost no maintenance. Keep the ditch clear of debris each autumn, and tread firm any sections that show significant settlement in years two and three.

Traditional management includes hedge-laying on bank hedges, which produces the most stock-proof result of all and is covered in a separate guide on this site.
