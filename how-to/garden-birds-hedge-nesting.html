Which garden birds nest in hedges, and what do they need?

Blackbirds, robins, dunnocks, wrens, song thrushes, whitethroats: most of the birds people want in their garden nest in hedges, not trees. Which species uses which hedge plant, at what height, and what management helps them succeed.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Nesting season: 1 March to 31 August

Under the UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to intentionally damage or destroy an active bird's nest or disturb birds while nesting. The main hedge-nesting season runs roughly 1 March to 31 August, though early blackbirds may begin in mid-February and late broods can persist into September. Before any hedge cut, check for active nests. See our practical guide to checking hedges for birds' nests.

Why hedges matter more than trees for garden birds

Garden birds are often associated with trees, but for most species the hedge is the more important structure. Trees provide song posts, roost cover and some foraging. Hedges provide what most birds actually need: concealment at nesting height, proximity to insect-rich foraging ground, and thorny or dense growth that excludes cats and crows from the nest.

In East Kent, where gardens tend to be bounded by laurel, privet and leylandii, the hedge is also often the only substantial woody structure in the garden. A 20-metre hawthorn or mixed hedge along a field boundary near Woodnesborough, Eastry or Worth will typically hold more nesting pairs in any given spring than all the garden trees within 200 metres of it.

Robin

Preferred hedge plants: privet, box, small-leaved hedges with dense interior structure; also nests in hedge bases and at ground level in vegetation scrambling through the hedge.

Typical nest height: very variable, from ground level to 1.5 metres. Often in a cup of moss and dead leaves tucked into a dense section of hedge or a gap in the hedge base.

Nesting season: late March to late July; two or three broods.

Robins are early starters and will investigate a hedge as potential nesting territory in February. Their nests are well concealed, often in a hollow or behind a clump of dead leaves pressed against the stems. They will use a privet hedge that other species would find too exposed, provided the interior has been allowed to fill out with a few years' layering of older wood.

What helps: let the base of the hedge get slightly rough and leafy rather than cutting it flush. A privet hedge that has been allowed to grow slightly wider at the base than at the top (the "A" profile) retains ground-level shelter that robins use.

Blackbird

Preferred hedge plants: hawthorn, laurel, privet, mixed hedges with some density at 1-2 metres. Blackbirds also use ivy scrambling through a hedge or fence.

Typical nest height: 1-2 metres, often on a lateral branch pressed against the main stems.

Nesting season: mid-February to late July; up to four broods in a good year.

Blackbirds are one of the earliest hedge-nesters and one of the most likely to be disturbed by an early spring cut. In a Kent garden a blackbird may begin nest building in the last week of February in a mild year, well before most householders would think of picking up the trimmer.

Their nests are substantial: a cup of grass and plant material reinforced with mud, roughly 15 cm across. Dense laurel is popular because its large leaves provide overhead rain cover, and its thick stems offer the lateral support a blackbird nest needs.

What helps: delay any first cut until you have walked the hedge in early March. A blackbird nest is relatively easy to spot once you know what to look for: a flat mud-and-grass cup, usually with a clear flight line to the entrance from below.

Dunnock

Preferred hedge plants: hawthorn, bramble-threaded hedges, blackthorn; any low thorny hedge with rough ground at the base.

Typical nest height: 20-80 cm. Dunnocks nest lower than almost any other hedge bird, often in the base of a hawthorn where the stems are thickest and most tangled.

Nesting season: late March to July; usually two or three broods.

The dunnock is one of the birds most immediately affected by hedge base management. It needs the lowest 60 cm of a hedge to be dense, tangled and undisturbed. A hawthorn hedge that has been cut hard to a clean face at ground level, with no overhang of stems and no rough grass at the base, will not hold dunnocks.

What helps: allow a slight overhang at the base of hawthorn or blackthorn hedges. Leave rough grass or dead stems at the hedge foot rather than stripping it back to bare soil.

Song thrush

Preferred hedge plants: hawthorn, privet, any hedge at 1.5-2.5 metres that provides a substantial branching structure for the nest to sit on.

Typical nest height: 1-2.5 metres, often in the main stem fork of a hawthorn or on a horizontal branch with good overhead cover.

Nesting season: early March to late July; usually two or three broods.

The song thrush nests in hedges with a distinctive mud-lined interior: the smooth plastered mud cup is the most reliable identification feature of its nest. They tend to choose positions with a clear foraging area nearby (short grass, bare soil) and a hedge that provides shelter from above without blocking a low approach flight.

Song thrush numbers have fallen sharply in the UK since the 1980s. In East Kent, they are most consistently found in the mixed hedge-and-orchard landscapes between Ash and Eastry, and in gardens bordering open land toward Worth.

What helps: leave sections of hawthorn hedge uncut for two years at a time. A song thrush will often return to the same site in subsequent years if the hedge structure is not significantly changed between seasons.

Wren

Preferred hedge plants: bramble-threaded hedges, hawthorn with scrambly undergrowth, any complex interior structure close to the ground.

Typical nest height: 30-80 cm. The male wren constructs several dome-shaped nests from which the female selects one; they are deeply concealed in tangles of growth.

Nesting season: mid-April to late July.

Wrens are cavity-and-tangle nesters: they need the kind of dense, scrambly interior that a hedge develops when it has not been cut hard for several years. A beech or hornbeam hedge kept at a tight formal profile will not hold wrens. A hawthorn hedge that has been given one or two seasons of lighter management, with some bramble or dead stems at the base, is much more likely to.

What helps: the same rotational or sympathetic cutting approach that benefits dunnocks. A rough, layered hedge base is more important than overall hedge height for wrens.

Whitethroat

Preferred hedge plants: blackthorn, hawthorn, any prickly hedge at the field margin with rough grass on both sides.

Typical nest height: 20-60 cm. The nest is a neat, deep cup in low thorny growth, usually very close to the ground.

Nesting season: late April to July; a summer migrant, arriving from West Africa in late April.

Whitethroats are declining nationally and their presence in East Kent is a useful indicator of hedge quality on agricultural boundaries. They favour the tall, prickly boundary hedges on the margins of the arable fields between Sandwich and Eastry: blackthorn in particular, allowed to grow to 2-3 metres with rough grass at the foot.

They rarely nest in garden hedges unless the garden is bordered by open farmland. But maintaining a tall hawthorn or blackthorn field boundary hedge, rather than coppicing it to a low fence-height run, directly supports whitethroat populations in the parishes around Woodnesborough and Worth.

Linnet

Preferred hedge plants: gorse, hawthorn, blackthorn at field margins.

Typical nest height: 0.5-1.5 metres, often in the heart of a dense gorse or hawthorn clump.

Nesting season: late April to late July; often colonial, several pairs in the same hedgerow section.

Linnets have declined significantly across Kent with the loss of mixed arable margins and tall boundary hedges. They are still present in the rougher coastal areas around Sandwich Bay and on the Lydden valley farmland. Gorse-bearing hedge sections or unimproved field margins are the key habitat.

Greenfinch

Preferred hedge plants: conifers (leylandii, Lawson cypress), dense laurel, thick privet; a species that tolerates more formal hedges than most.

Typical nest height: 1.5-4 metres, in the denser interior of a conifer hedge or in lateral branches of a tall privet.

Nesting season: late April to late July.

Greenfinches have declined in the UK due to trichomonosis (a disease spread via bird feeders) but they remain present in most Kent gardens. The leylandii and laurel hedges that are common in residential Sandwich and Deal streets are actively used by greenfinches for nesting; a dense formal conifer hedge is not the wildlife desert it is sometimes described as.

Yellowhammer

Preferred hedge plants: hawthorn, blackthorn: tall, thick, prickly hedges at field margins with unimproved rough ground at the base.

Typical nest height: ground level to 30 cm, often at the base of the hedge or in a tussock immediately beside it.

Nesting season: late April to late August; up to three broods. A late-nesting species: active broods are one reason the hedge-cutting window closes late in summer.

Yellowhammers were once a common sight on every Kent field boundary. They have declined by over 50% nationally since the 1980s. They are still present on the arable farmland east of Ash and south of Worth, where tall boundary hawthorn hedges remain. The requirements are specific: a hedge at least 1.8 metres tall, a thick base with no hard ground-level cut, and open land nearby with bare soil or short-cropped grass for foraging.

A single decision, to maintain a field boundary hedge at 2-2.5 metres rather than cutting it annually to 1 metre, has a direct and significant impact on yellowhammer habitat.

The hedge plant that supports the most species: hawthorn

If one hedge species stands out across this guide it is hawthorn. It is the preferred nesting plant for dunnock, blackbird, song thrush, whitethroat and yellowhammer; it is used by robin, wren, linnet and chaffinch; it provides caterpillars for chicks in spring (supporting over 200 species of invertebrate), and haws in autumn for redwing, fieldfare and song thrush. It tolerates hard cutting and regenerates from old wood. It is found in the vast majority of Kent boundary hedges.

The single most effective action for hedge-nesting birds in East Kent is to maintain hawthorn hedges at a good height (1.8 metres or above), with some sections given a rest from annual cutting to build density and berry-bearing wood.
