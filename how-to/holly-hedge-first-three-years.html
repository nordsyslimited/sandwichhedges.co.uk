Holly hedge: first three years

Pot-grown versus bare-root holly compared for East Kent gardens: which costs less, which establishes faster, and what both types look like by year three.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Holly (Ilex aquifolium) is a native evergreen that has grown in the hedgerows and woodland edges of East Kent for centuries. It is slower to establish than cherry laurel or privet, but it offers something they cannot: dense wildlife habitat, winter berries for thrushes and redwings, and a hedge that looks as good in December as in June. The prickly leaves are also an effective deterrent for anyone or anything considering pushing through.

When we are asked about planting holly, one of the first questions is usually "pot-grown or bare-root?" The answer affects the planting window, the cost and the establishment timeline. Here is how they compare, year by year.

Nesting season: 1 March to 31 August

Holly is one of the best nesting plants in the garden: dense, prickly, year-round cover that blackbirds, thrushes, robins and sparrows all use. Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 it is an offence to damage or destroy a nest in active use. Nesting runs approximately 1 March to 31 August. The safe cutting window for significant trimming is late August to February. Check carefully before any cut near the nesting season boundary.

Before any cutting work: PPE and care

Holly leaves are sharp. Thick leather gloves (not fabric) are essential when working around or inside a holly hedge. Wear safety glasses to protect against falling leaves and debris. Ear defenders with a powered trimmer. For hedges above chest height, a pole trimmer is safer than a stepladder. Run electric-trimmer cables behind you and use an RCD adaptor. Check for wasps' nests at the base before starting.

Pot-grown vs bare-root: the key differences

Factor Pot-grown Bare-root
Planting window Year-round (avoid frozen or waterlogged ground) November to late February only
Relative cost Higher Lower (often 40 to 60% cheaper per plant)
Root system at planting Intact, established in container Bare: must re-establish from scratch
Year-one establishment Faster: often shows visible new growth within weeks Slower: may show little top growth in year one
Year-three appearance Dense and presentable Dense and often bushier at base
Watering in year one Critical, especially in summer Important, particularly in spring and early summer
Availability Widely available, all year Specialist hedge nurseries, autumn to early spring

The difference in establishment speed narrows significantly by year three. By year five, a bare-root holly hedge planted as small whips often looks denser and more uniform than a pot-grown hedge of the same age, because the bare-root plants built their root systems from scratch in the soil they will live in permanently, rather than adapting from a container environment. If the budget allows, pot-grown holly is the faster visual result. If cost is the main constraint, bare-root holly planted in November gives almost the same outcome by year five at a considerably lower price.

At planting: both types

Regardless of whether you are planting pot-grown or bare-root holly, the approach at planting is the same in one important respect: do not cut it back severely. This is different from privet, which benefits from a hard planting cut. Holly establishes from its roots, not from regrowth after a heavy cut, and removing too much top growth at planting slows establishment rather than speeding it up.

At planting, use secateurs to remove any physically damaged stems, any stems that cross and rub against each other, and very long, unbranched leaders that look out of proportion with the row. Reduce these leaders by no more than a quarter. Leave everything else alone.

Space pot-grown holly at 50 to 60 cm centres; bare-root at 40 to 50 cm. Apply a 7 to 10 cm mulch of composted bark along the base, keeping it clear of the stems. Firm in well and water thoroughly on planting day.

One additional point specific to holly: if you want berries, you need a pollinator. Most holly hedges use a female variety for the dense growth, but berries only form when a male holly plant is present within reasonable pollination distance (about 50 m in a garden setting). Plant at least one male plant for every five to eight females in the hedge row.

Year 1

Pot-grown holly planted in autumn will typically show visible new growth by the following spring. Its intact root ball means it can begin drawing water and nutrients almost immediately after planting. Water once a week in dry spells from April to September. The first summer is critical: even though the root system is ahead of a bare-root plant, a pot-grown holly planted into East Kent's chalk-based soils needs regular watering to establish into the surrounding ground. Keep the base strip weed-free for the whole first year.

Bare-root holly planted in November or December may show very little top growth in year one. This is normal, not a sign of failure. The plant is concentrating all its energy on establishing a new root system in the soil. As long as the leaves do not turn entirely brown or start dropping in quantity, the plant is alive and working. Water once a week in dry spells from April onwards, and do not cut or disturb it at all. Some bare-root holly hedges look almost static through year one and then push on strongly in year two: patience here is well rewarded.

Neither type should be cut during year one. No trimming from 1 March to 31 August. In late September or October, if the pot-grown hedge has put on significant new growth, a very light tidy with secateurs, taking the tips of any very long leaders back by 10 to 15 cm, helps encourage branching. For bare-root plants showing little year-one growth, leave them completely alone until year two.

Year 2

By spring of year two, both types should be showing clear new growth. For pot-grown plants, the hedge is beginning to look like a hedge. For bare-root plants, year two is typically when the visible growth picks up significantly as the root system matures.

Continue watering in dry spells through year two, though the established root systems at this stage make both types considerably more resilient. Keep the weed-free strip in place.

The year-two formative trim is in late August. Use secateurs. Take off the current season's growth on the sides by about half, working in the slight batter angle (wider at the base, narrowing toward the top). Do not touch the top yet unless the leaders have already reached the target height. The goal is to encourage dense lateral branching and begin establishing the finished profile. One pass with secateurs, working methodically from one end of the hedge to the other, is enough for year two.

Year 3

Pot-grown holly planted three autumns ago should now be clearly hedge-shaped: a consistent height, reasonable density across the face, and a recognisable profile. The gap between the two types narrows in year three, though the pot-grown hedge still typically has a slight lead in density.

Bare-root holly is often beginning to close the gap by year three. Where bare-root plants went in as small whips and were left to establish without disturbance, the year-three hedge often shows a denser, more uniform base than pot-grown plants of the same age, because the branching pattern developed naturally from ground level up.

Year-three trimming continues with secateurs in late August, working the sides and, if the leaders have reached the target height, beginning to bring the top in. One cut per year is sufficient for most holly hedges at this stage. Holly is not a fast grower: 20 to 30 cm per year on a well-established plant in a good growing season in Kent's mild conditions is typical. This means the annual cut is not a large job in year three; the work of establishing the shape is mostly done.

By year five, both pot-grown and bare-root holly should look fully established and structurally complete. At that point, the transition to straightforward annual maintenance is finished.
