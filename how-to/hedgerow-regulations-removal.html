Do I need permission to remove a hedge?

The Hedgerow Regulations 1997 explained in plain English. Work through the decision tree below to find out whether your hedge removal requires notice to the council, or whether you're free to go ahead.

Published September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

We're asked about hedgerow removal regularly, and the honest answer is that most East Kent homeowners can take out a garden hedge without any formal permission at all. But there are situations where you genuinely do need to notify your council first, and getting it wrong can lead to prosecution. So it's worth spending ten minutes with this decision tree before you book anyone in.

This guide covers the Hedgerow Regulations 1997. It's a separate question from planning permission, Tree Preservation Orders, and neighbour disputes (which we cover in separate guides). Work through the questions in order, starting with Question 1.

Important: this guide is for information only

Hedgerow law is complex, and penalties for getting it wrong can be significant. If you're not certain which category your hedge falls into, contact your Local Planning Authority (LPA) before you touch it. In East Kent: Dover District Council covers Sandwich, Deal, Worth, Ash and Woodnesborough; Folkestone and Hythe District Council covers the Hythe and Folkestone areas.

The decision tree

Question 1: Is the hedge inside your domestic garden?

The Hedgerow Regulations 1997 do not apply to hedgerows within the curtilage of a dwelling. This means the garden and any land immediately associated with a house. If your hedge is a boundary hedge around your garden, a hedge within your garden, or a hedge that forms part of a residential property boundary, the Regulations almost certainly do not apply to you.

YES, it is in my garden — the Hedgerow Regulations 1997 are very unlikely to apply. Stop here, but read the note below about planning conditions and TPOs before proceeding.

NO, it is on agricultural, common, forestry or other non-residential land — continue to Question 2.

Still check planning conditions and TPOs

Even if the Hedgerow Regulations don't apply, a hedge can still be protected by: a planning condition attached to your property (check with your LPA or your original planning permission documents); a Tree Preservation Order (TPO), which occasionally extends to hedges or individual trees within a hedge; or a Conservation Area designation, which restricts works to some trees and hedges. When in doubt, a free call to your LPA's duty planning officer will clarify.

Question 2: Does the hedgerow meet the basic size and location criteria?

To fall under the Hedgerow Regulations 1997, a hedgerow must meet both of these criteria:

  • It must be at least 20 metres in length, or it must join another hedgerow at each end (even if shorter than 20 metres)
  • It must stand on land used for agriculture, forestry, common land, or a Site of Special Scientific Interest (SSSI)

NO — the Regulations do not apply. You can proceed without giving notice under these Regulations (still check Question 1's note above about planning conditions).

YES — continue to Question 3.

Question 3: Has the hedgerow stood for at least 30 years?

Age matters. A hedgerow must have existed continuously for at least 30 years to fall within the scope of the Regulations. Younger hedgerows, including recently planted shelter belts and newly established farm boundaries, are not covered.

NO, it is less than 30 years old — the Regulations do not apply.

YES, it is 30+ years old, or I don't know — if uncertain, assume it qualifies and continue to Question 4. You can consult historical Ordnance Survey maps (available through the National Library of Scotland's map viewer) to check whether a hedge appears on maps from 30+ years ago.

Question 4: Is the hedgerow "important" under Schedule 1?

This is the most consequential question. Regardless of whether a hedgerow is "important" or not, you must still give 42-day written notice to the Local Planning Authority before removing it. The difference is what happens next:

  • If the hedgerow is not important: the LPA has 42 days to respond. If they don't respond, or if they give consent, you can proceed.
  • If the hedgerow is important: the LPA can issue a Hedgerow Retention Notice, preventing removal for up to 5 years.

A hedgerow qualifies as "important" if it meets at least one criterion from Schedule 1 of the Regulations. The most commonly relevant criteria are:

  • It contains 7 or more native woody species per 30-metre stretch (the Hooper Method, used as a rough guide to age)
  • It forms the boundary of a historic parish, manor or estate as recorded before 1850
  • It contains or is adjacent to archaeological features: bank, ditch, ridge-and-furrow, or scheduled ancient monument
  • It provides a habitat link between two or more areas of ancient woodland, SSSI, or other designated habitat
  • It includes certain protected or rare species: great crested newt, bats using the hedge as a commuting route, or listed rare plant species

In East Kent, many of the older hedgerows bordering the Romney Marsh, the Stour valley, and the open agricultural land around Ash and Wingham are genuinely old boundary hedges and may meet one or more of these criteria. If you're in any doubt, a habitat survey by an ecologist is a worthwhile investment before removing anything.

How to give notice: the practicalities

If Questions 2 and 3 confirm the Regulations apply to your hedgerow, you must submit a Hedgerow Removal Notice in writing to your Local Planning Authority before any removal work begins. Here is what the notice must include:

  1. Your name, address, and contact details
  2. The address or location of the land where the hedgerow stands
  3. A plan showing the position of the hedgerow to be removed (a marked-up Ordnance Survey extract works well)
  4. The reasons for the removal
  5. The date on which you intend to start work (must be at least 42 days from the date the LPA receives the notice)

Send it by recorded delivery or email to the LPA's planning department. Keep your proof of postage or email confirmation. The 42-day clock starts when the LPA receives the notice, not when you send it.

If the LPA neither gives consent nor issues a Hedgerow Retention Notice within 42 days, you may proceed with the removal. If they grant consent before the 42 days is up, you can start earlier.

What about trimming, not removing?

The Hedgerow Regulations 1997 only control the removal of hedgerows. They do not restrict trimming, cutting back, laying, coppicing, or any other management of a standing hedgerow. You do not need to give notice before trimming a hedgerow, however old or species-rich it is.

The separate rule that does apply to trimming is the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981: you must not cut hedgerows during the bird nesting season (broadly 1 March to 31 August) when nests are in active use. This applies to all hedges everywhere, not just agricultural hedgerows.

East Kent context

Most domestic hedges in Sandwich, Deal, Worth, Ash, Woodnesborough and the surrounding villages are garden hedges and are outside the scope of the Hedgerow Regulations entirely. The Regulations are most relevant to farmers and landowners with hedgerows running between fields or along historic boundaries.

That said, East Kent has some remarkable ancient hedgerows. The boundaries around Sandwich's medieval town fields, the hedgerow systems between Ash and Wingham, and the old boundary hedges along the Stour valley are features worth knowing about before any removal work is planned.

If you're buying or selling land in East Kent and a hedgerow forms part of the boundary, a solicitor should check for any relevant entries in the local land charges register, which can include hedgerow retention notices.
