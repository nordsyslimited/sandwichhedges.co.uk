Disposing of diseased, toxic or invasive hedge clippings safely

Composting hedge trimmings is usually the right thing to do. But a handful of plant types need very different handling. Here is what to do with the difficult ones.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Most hedge clippings go on the compost heap or into the green recycling bin and that is exactly right. But a handful of plants, whether diseased, toxic to animals, or classified as invasive under UK law, need to be handled quite differently. Putting the wrong material in the wrong place can spread disease, cause harm or, in the case of Japanese knotweed, land you in legal trouble.

Here is a checklist of the material that needs special treatment, followed by what to do with each type.

When in doubt, bag it and take it to the tip

If you are not certain what you are dealing with, sealing clippings in a plastic bag and taking them to your local household waste recycling centre (HWRC) is always the safe option. Do not leave unidentified cuttings in a pile on site.

The checklist at a glance

  • Diseased material (box blight, honey fungus, fire blight): bag, seal, HWRC. Do not compost.
  • Yew clippings: bag, seal, HWRC. Do not compost, do not leave where animals can graze.
  • Laurel clippings (large quantities): HWRC or commercial collection. Do not home-compost.
  • Invasive species (Japanese knotweed, giant hogweed): licensed contractor only. Not domestic waste.
  • Box tree moth material: bag, seal, HWRC. Do not leave in the garden.
  • Holly berries: bag if you have dogs; otherwise normal compost is fine.

The detail

Box blight (Cylindrocladium buxicola)

Box blight spreads by contact with infected material, water splash and tools. Once you start cutting, infected clippings carry live spores. Bag them in sealed heavy-duty plastic bags as you cut, rather than letting them fall and sit on the soil around the hedge base. Take the bags to the HWRC and deposit in the non-recyclable (residual) waste stream, not the garden waste bin.

Before moving your tools to the next garden or the next plant, wipe blades clean and spray with a proprietary disinfectant or a dilute bleach solution (1 part bleach to 9 parts water). Let it dry before continuing.

For the full picture on treating infected plants, see our box blight guide.

Honey fungus (Armillaria species)

Honey fungus spreads through soil via black rhizomorphs (bootlace-like structures) as well as through infected wood. If you are removing a hedge that has been killed by honey fungus, the roots and woody material should be dug out and disposed of through the HWRC rather than chipped or composted on site. Leaving stumps encourages the fungus to establish further. See our honey fungus guide for identification and treatment options.

Fire blight (Erwinia amylovora)

Fire blight is a bacterial disease that affects members of the rose family, including hawthorn, cotoneaster and pyracantha. Affected stems and leaves should be bagged immediately after cutting. Do not compost. Disinfect tools between cuts and between plants.

Yew (Taxus baccata)

Yew is toxic to horses, cattle, dogs and humans. All parts remain toxic after the plant dies. Do not compost yew clippings, do not leave them in a pile where livestock or pets can reach them, and do not burn them on an open garden bonfire (the smoke retains toxicity). Bag and take to the HWRC. If you have a large amount, contact your local council waste team or a licensed green-waste carrier.

The HWRC accepts yew in the residual waste stream at most Kent sites. Mixing small amounts with other non-toxic waste is generally fine.

Laurel (Prunus laurocerasus and P. lusitanica)

Laurel leaves contain cyanogenic glycosides, which release small amounts of hydrogen cyanide as they break down. For small quantities, the council green recycling bin is usually fine: commercial composting facilities operate at temperatures that break these compounds down safely. However, home composting large volumes of laurel is not recommended. If you have a significant quantity from a renovation cut, take it to the HWRC garden-waste skips.

Japanese knotweed and other Schedule 9 invasives

Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to cause Japanese knotweed (or other Schedule 9 species, including giant hogweed) to spread. Any stem section that can re-root counts as a controlled waste. Do not put knotweed material in any domestic bin, skip, compost heap or on bare soil. You need a licensed waste carrier. Your local council or the Environment Agency can advise on licensed contractors in East Kent.

If you are not certain whether your hedge contains Japanese knotweed or giant hogweed, call a specialist before cutting.

Box tree moth (Cydalima perspectalis)

Box tree moth caterpillars skeletonise box plants and pupate in webbing on the foliage. After treatment and cutting, bag clippings containing webbing and caterpillars in sealed bags for the HWRC. This limits the spread of pupae to neighbouring gardens. See our box tree moth guide for identification and control.

What you CAN compost

Healthy clippings from privet, beech, hornbeam, hawthorn, hazel, field maple, blackthorn, rosemary, lavender, berberis, escallonia, griselinia and most other hedge species compost well. Our composting guide covers how to handle the volumes a single cut can produce.
