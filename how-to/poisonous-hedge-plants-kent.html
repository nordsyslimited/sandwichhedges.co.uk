Poisonous hedge plants in Kent gardens

Several of the most popular hedging species in East Kent are genuinely toxic. Not exotic-sounding foreign plants, but everyday garden hedges you probably already have: yew, holly, cherry laurel and privet. This is what you need to know.

Updated September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

In twenty-odd years of cutting hedges across Sandwich, Deal and the villages of East Kent, we have seen plenty of garden accidents. Falls from ladders, cuts from shears, a wasp nest disturbed at short notice. What we have also seen, less visibly but just as importantly, is garden waste left in places where children or animals can reach it.

Most people know in a vague way that yew is poisonous. Fewer know just how toxic it is, or that several other very common hedging plants carry their own risks. This guide runs through the main species you are likely to have in an East Kent garden, what the risk actually is and how to handle clippings safely.

Before you start: safety with powered hedge trimmers

  • Eye protection: safety glasses when using any powered trimmer. Clippings fly.
  • Gloves: cut-resistant gloves for handling clippings from thorny species.
  • RCD adaptor: compulsory for corded electric trimmers. No exceptions.
  • Ladder safety: a long-reach pole trimmer is far safer than a ladder for tall hedges. If a ladder is unavoidable, have someone footing it and never overreach sideways.
  • Check for wasp nests: always look into the hedge before starting, especially in late summer. Running a trimmer into a wasp nest is unpleasant at the best of times.
  • After cutting: move clippings away from areas accessible to children, dogs, horses or livestock before they can browse the waste material.

Species guide: which hedges are toxic and to whom

Yew (Taxus baccata): the most serious risk

Yew is one of the most beautiful formal hedge plants available, and one of the most toxic plants in the British Isles. The entire plant is poisonous, with the exception of the red fleshy aril (the outer part of the berry). Leaves, seeds, bark and twigs all contain taxine alkaloids, which affect the heart in both humans and animals.

For horses, even a small quantity of yew clippings can be fatal. Cattle, sheep, goats and pigs are also at risk. Dogs and cats are vulnerable too. Historically, the most common cause of livestock deaths from yew has been garden waste thrown over a field boundary, or clippings left where grazing animals could reach them.

For humans, the risk from accidental ingestion of a leaf or two is moderate, but eating seeds or larger quantities of any part of the plant is dangerous. Children, because of their smaller body weight, are at greater risk than adults from the same quantity. Always supervise children around freshly trimmed yew.

Disposal: yew clippings must go via green bin council collection or a household waste recycling centre. Do not compost them in a heap accessible to animals, and do not burn them at a field boundary where livestock might breathe the smoke. Our full guide to yew hedge care and trimming covers timing and technique.

Holly (Ilex aquifolium): bright berries, real risk

Holly berries are toxic to dogs and, particularly, to young children. The berries contain saponins and other compounds that cause vomiting, diarrhoea and abdominal pain. As few as two or three berries can cause symptoms in a small child. Adults and older children who might eat one berry by accident are at low risk, but medical advice should be sought promptly if a young child has eaten holly berries.

The risk is higher at Christmas, when holly with berries is often brought inside and placed at child height. Holly hedge clippings should be moved away from play areas promptly after cutting. Keeping holly hedges regularly trimmed reduces berry production, since berries form on mature wood.

Holly leaves are not considered toxic in the same way as the berries, though the sharp spines are a practical hazard. Cut-resistant gloves are sensible when handling holly clippings. See our full holly hedge care guide for trimming advice.

Cherry laurel (Prunus laurocerasus): prussic acid in the leaves

Cherry laurel is arguably the most widely planted hedge in Kent and across the south-east. It is not toxic in the way yew is, but it is not entirely harmless either. The leaves, particularly when crushed or chewed, release hydrogen cyanide (prussic acid). The amount released by accidentally chewing a leaf or two is very unlikely to cause serious harm to an adult. However, horses and other livestock that browse fresh laurel clippings or freshly cut hedge waste can take in enough to cause poisoning.

The practical rules: keep laurel clippings away from horses and livestock, do not leave cut material at a field boundary, and do not burn large quantities of laurel in a confined space as the smoke can irritate the respiratory tract. For garden composting, shredded laurel is fine on a domestic compost heap that is not accessible to horses.

Privet (Ligustrum ovalifolium and Ligustrum vulgare): the everyday risk

Privet is probably the most common hedge species in suburban East Kent, from Sandwich to Deal and all the villages between. Most people think of it as completely harmless. The berries, which form in late summer and autumn on untrimmed sections, are mildly to moderately toxic. Eating a quantity of them can cause vomiting, diarrhoea and in larger quantities more serious symptoms in dogs and children.

The leaves are also mildly toxic if consumed in significant quantity. In practice, the risk is low because privet does not taste good and accidental ingestion of a dangerous amount is uncommon. However, if dogs are known to chew garden waste, keeping them away from freshly cut privet is sensible. Regular trimming reduces berry production, since berries form on growth that has been allowed to flower and set seed.

Box (Buxus sempervirens): bitter but toxic

Box is toxic to horses, cattle and humans if consumed in significant quantity. The bitter taste generally deters accidental ingestion by animals, and poisoning from box in domestic gardens is rare. However, horses that are hungry or bored may eat box leaves, and field-boundary hedges of box should be checked. Keep box clippings away from grazing animals.

For garden use, box is a low-risk species compared with yew. Wear gloves when handling clippings, as box sap can cause skin irritation in some people.

Elder (Sambucus nigra): raw berries only

Elder is sometimes used in native mixed hedges in Kent, and is very common in hedgerows around the villages of Woodnesborough, Ash and Worth. The ripe black berries are edible when cooked and are widely used for elderberry wine and cordial. However, raw elderberries contain compounds that can cause nausea and vomiting if eaten in quantity. Green, unripe berries carry a higher risk. The leaves, bark and roots are more toxic still and should not be eaten.

For practical hedge care, elder presents little risk to adults who are not eating the plant. Keep children from eating raw berries, and cook any harvest before use.

Safe handling of clippings: the general rules

For all hedge species, the practical rules are straightforward:

  • Move clippings away from areas where children play and where dogs or horses can access them, before the end of the working session.
  • Yew clippings specifically: use green bin collection or the household waste recycling centre. Never leave yew clippings at a field boundary or accessible to livestock.
  • Laurel clippings near horse fields: collect and bag before they wilt, as the prussic acid content increases as leaves dry.
  • Wash your hands after handling clippings from any of the species listed above.
  • Wear gloves when handling spiny or sap-heavy clippings (holly, box, euphorbias in adjacent borders).

Yew is the one to take seriously. A beautiful hedge, completely valid choice, but always dispose of the clippings via council collection and never leave them where horses or dogs can reach them.
