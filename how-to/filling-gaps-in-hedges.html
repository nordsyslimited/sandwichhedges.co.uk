How to fill gaps in an established hedge

A bare patch or dead section in an otherwise healthy hedge can look unsightly and leave your boundary porous. Here is how to diagnose the cause, choose the right method and get the hedge solid again.

Updated September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Gaps happen. A stretch of laurel dies back after a hard frost. An old hawthorn section gives out in a dry summer. Someone parks too close to the roots. Whatever the cause, the fix follows the same logic: find out why it died, then choose the method that suits the plant and the gap size.

Hedgelink, a national partnership of the RSPB, National Trust, Woodland Trust and Wildlife Trusts, produced a practical video guide to exactly this job, titled "Part 3 - Curing a gap" (Hedgelink, YouTube). Their hedge-laying expert Tom Hynes demonstrates the bending-and-pegging approach for filling gaps in native hedges, alongside conventional replanting. The steps below draw on the same approach, adapted for the garden hedges we work on across East Kent.

Before you start: nesting season

Working within or immediately around a hedge during the bird nesting season can disturb active nests, which is an offence under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981. Nesting season runs from 1 March to 31 August. Before cutting, bending, or removing any dead wood from within the hedge, part the growth and look for occupied nests. The safe planting and light-manipulation window is September to February for most of this work. September is ideal: the ground is still warm, bare-root stock is beginning to arrive, and nesting has ended for the year.

Safety: working around hedges

  • Gloves and eye protection are essential, especially when working with hawthorn or blackthorn. Thorns cause deep puncture wounds that can become infected.
  • Check for wasp nests before disturbing any dense or dead section. A section that died over winter is a common overwintering site.
  • If you need to use any power tools near the gap, use an RCD adaptor and keep cables clear of cutting blades.
  • If the hedge borders a road, wear a high-visibility vest and park any vehicle so it shields you from passing traffic.

Step 1: diagnose before you plant

Replanting into the same conditions that killed the original plants is a waste of time and money. Spend five minutes on diagnosis first.

Disease: Pull back any dead material and look at the base of the dead stems. White or cream fungal threads at soil level, or a smell of mushrooms, suggests honey fungus. Powdery grey-brown patches on box suggest box blight. In both cases, remove all infected material (bag it, do not compost) and treat the soil before replanting. See our honey fungus guide and box blight guide for species-specific advice.

Drought or soil: In East Kent, the chalk-over-clay soils around Sandwich and Ash drain freely and can get very dry in summer. A young plant put in without enough organic matter in the backfill, and not watered through its first dry summer, will silently die. Squeeze a handful of soil from around the dead roots: if it crumbles to dust, the ground needs improving before you replant.

Mechanical damage: Check for compressed soil or broken roots caused by vehicles, or damage to bark at the base of stems. Bark stripped all the way around a stem (ring-barked) kills everything above that point.

Over-cutting: If the gap coincides with where a hard cut was taken into old brown wood, the plant may simply have failed to regenerate. This is common with leylandii and other conifers, which will not regrow from bare wood at all.

Step 2: choose your method

There are two main approaches, and sometimes a combination of both works best.

Layering or pegging down existing stems: If there are long, flexible stems on either side of the gap, you can bend them horizontally into the gap and peg or weave them into place. This is the method Tom Hynes demonstrates in the Hedgelink video: stems from healthy flanking sections are bent, nicked at the bend point to encourage rooting, pegged down low in the gap, and tied to a supporting cane. New upright shoots appear within a season. This works best for native hedges with pliable stems, hawthorn, blackthorn, hazel, field maple, and similar. It is not suitable for stiff-stemmed evergreens like laurel or yew.

Replanting with new stock: If the gap is large, the flanking stems too short or too rigid to layer, or the cause was disease (which may persist in the soil), replanting is the better option. Choose bare-root whips in autumn and winter, or container-grown plants the rest of the year. Match the species to what is already there.

Step 3: prepare the gap

Remove all dead wood, roots, and debris from the gap area. If disease was involved, bag everything and dispose of it; do not put it on a compost heap. For honey fungus, remove as much root material as possible, as the fungus lives on decaying wood underground.

Loosen the soil to at least 40 cm deep. In East Kent's heavier clay patches, break up any compaction and dig in grit or sharp sand to improve drainage. In sandy or chalky spots around Sandwich Bay and the coast, add plenty of well-rotted organic matter to help moisture retention.

If vehicle compaction was the cause, the soil will be hard and airless. Fork through it repeatedly and add organic matter, then leave it to settle for a week or two before planting if you can.

Step 4: source matching plants

Match species and, if possible, height at purchase to what you already have. A large-leaved laurel replanted into a common laurel hedge will always look patchy. If you are not sure of the exact variety, take a cutting or a few leaves to a local nursery.

Bare-root whips (60-90 cm or 90-120 cm) are cheapest and establish quickest in autumn and winter. Order from a native hedging supplier if you need hawthorn, blackthorn, field maple or other native species. For formal hedges (yew, laurel, box), pot-grown plants are easier to source year-round but cost more. Planting three or four whips where there was one mature plant gives the gap a head start.

Step 5: plant properly

Plant at the same depth as in the original container or nursery bed: you can see the soil mark on bare-root stock. Dig a hole just wide enough for the roots to spread without cramping. Backfill with the original soil mixed with compost (roughly two shovels of compost to one of soil), firm gently with your foot, and water in well even if the ground seems damp.

Spacing within the gap depends on the species. For hawthorn or blackthorn whips in a native hedge, plant in a double staggered row at 30-40 cm apart. For laurel or privet, a single row at 45-60 cm is enough, the plants will bush out sideways. For yew, spacing at 60 cm gives them room but allows them to close up within three or four years.

Trim the adjacent hedge stems back by around a third. This encourages the flanking material to bush outward into the gap and helps the new plants integrate rather than being swamped by the existing hedge.

Step 6: aftercare

New plants in a gap are competing with established roots from the hedge on either side, which will have colonised the soil and will draw moisture and nutrients away from the new arrivals. Water the gap generously and regularly for the first full growing season: at least twice a week in dry spells.

Apply a 7-10 cm mulch of bark or wood chip around the new plants, keeping it clear of the stems. This retains moisture, suppresses weeds, and gradually improves the soil. Repeat in spring of the following year.

Fit spiral rabbit guards if rabbits are active in your area. Around Woodnesborough, Eastry, and the farmland edges on the outskirts of Sandwich, rabbit pressure is high enough to kill unguarded whips in a single winter.

Feed in March with a balanced slow-release granular fertiliser. Do not overdo it: too much nitrogen makes the new plants grow fast but weakly, and they are more vulnerable to the dry East Kent summers.

Species notes for common Kent hedges

Hawthorn and blackthorn

Very easy to fill. Bare-root whips establish reliably in autumn and winter. Plant at 30-40 cm centres in a double staggered row. Both species also layer well if there are long stems available on either side. Expect the gap to be invisible within two to three seasons.

Laurel (common and Portuguese)

Replant with container-grown plants of the same variety. Portuguese laurel (Prunus lusitanica) and common laurel (Prunus laurocerasus) look quite different when mature. Laurel does not layer. Trim the flanking stems back hard to encourage bushy sideways growth into the gap.

Yew

Slow to establish but very rewarding. Buy pot-grown plants for reliable establishment, or bare-root if ordering from a specialist nursery in autumn. Yew tolerates heavy pruning on the sides, so cut the flanking material back firmly and let the new plants grow upward without competition. Patience is essential: yew gaps can look poor for three or four years before they blend in.

Privet

Very easy. Pot-grown or bare-root whips both work. Trim the flanking sections back to encourage bushing. Privet grows fast enough that a gap can be filled in a single season with container plants.

Beech and hornbeam

Bare-root whips are cheap and establish reliably October to March. Plant slightly closer than you might for a new hedge (about 40 cm) to accelerate closure. Match the variety: copper beech (Fagus sylvatica f. purpurea) looks very different from common beech and will always stand out if you mix them.

If in doubt, plant more than you think you need. A hedge gap with five or six whips closing up looks far better after two years than one with a single plant struggling in the middle.
