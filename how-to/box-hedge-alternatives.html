Box hedge alternatives for Kent gardens

Box blight and box tree moth have made Buxus a difficult plant to grow in East Kent. Six alternatives that hold a formal shape, stay evergreen and do not carry the same disease risk.

Updated August 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

We have replaced more dead box hedges in the last five years than in the previous two decades combined. Box blight (Cylindrocladium buxicola) and box tree moth (Cydalima perspectalis) have between them stripped out box hedging across gardens in Sandwich, Deal, Worth, Ash and the surrounding villages. The moth's caterpillars can defoliate an entire hedge in a few weeks; the blight spreads in warm wet summers and is almost impossible to eradicate once it takes hold on chalk soils.

The good news is that several plants can do the job box used to do, some of them better in East Kent's conditions than box ever was. This article runs through the six we see planted and cared for most often, and weighs them honestly against each other.

1. Ilex crenata (Japanese holly)

Ilex crenata is the closest visual match for box. The small, dark, slightly glossy leaves are remarkably similar in texture and colour; clipped into a hedge, most people cannot tell the difference from ten feet away. It is fully hardy, tolerates chalk and sandy soils well, has no susceptibility to box blight or box tree moth, and once established needs only one or two trims a year.

The main drawback is cost: Ilex crenata plants are more expensive than box, and they grow more slowly, so a newly planted hedge takes longer to knit together. Spacing at 30–40 cm apart and patience of around three years for a modest hedge is realistic. Varieties to look for include 'Dark Green', 'Convexa' (excellent in East Kent's occasionally harsh winters) and 'Stokes' (the most compact). Avoid buying unlabelled plants described only as "Japanese holly" as growth habits vary widely between unnamed cultivars.

2. Osmanthus x burkwoodii

Osmanthus x burkwoodii is one of the better plants that most people have never heard of. It has small, toothed dark-green leaves, a naturally dense habit, and in April it produces masses of tiny white flowers that smell strongly of jasmine. This does not affect its usefulness as a formal hedge because the flowers are brief and the plant clips back neatly at any time outside of flowering. It grows to about 3 m if left alone, making it useful as a taller screen as well as a low edging hedge.

It is tolerant of chalk, tolerant of a degree of shade, and completely unaffected by either box problem. Growth rate sits between slow-growing Ilex crenata and fast-growing Lonicera nitida. Clipping once after flowering in May and once in late summer is usually enough. It also clips cleanly with hand shears if you prefer to avoid a hedge trimmer on a smaller hedge.

3. Lonicera nitida (Wilson's honeysuckle)

Lonicera nitida is the cheapest and fastest of the box alternatives, which makes it tempting. It roots readily from cuttings and is sold in large quantities by most garden centres. The tiny leaves give a fine-textured hedge that clips well and looks smart in its first few years.

The problems emerge later. Lonicera nitida grows fast in all directions, which means it needs clipping three to four times a year to hold a neat shape. It becomes open and leggy at the base within a few seasons if trimming is missed. It is also brittle in hard winters, occasionally dying back in cold snaps on exposed sites. For a low edging hedge where you do not mind frequent cutting it is fine; as a replacement for a formal boundary hedge it requires considerably more attention than box ever did. We tend to recommend it only in sheltered spots where cost is the primary concern.

For detailed trimming guidance see our Lonicera nitida hedge care guide.

4. Pittosporum tenuifolium

Pittosporum tenuifolium is an excellent choice for coastal areas and is the plant we most often recommend in Sandwich Bay and along the Deal seafront where salt spray is a factor. It has small pale-green leaves with wavy margins on near-black stems, giving a distinctive silvery-green effect quite unlike box, but very attractive in its own right. It tolerates coastal winds, light sandy soils and the relatively mild winters that East Kent gets by virtue of being close to the sea.

It grows to 4 m or more unpruned, making it a genuine screen hedge rather than just a low edger. Clipping once or twice a year keeps it in shape. The main risk is a hard continental frost: a sustained period below minus 8 or 9 degrees Celsius can kill or badly damage pittosporum. In East Kent this is rare but not impossible; in an exposed inland garden it is more of a concern. Keep it sheltered from the north and east if you can.

5. Euonymus japonicus

Euonymus japonicus is another plant with excellent coastal tolerance. It is dense, evergreen, clips well and is genuinely wind-tolerant. The variegated forms ('Aureus', 'Ovatus Aureus') add a bright quality that straight box never managed, but there are plain green forms too if you want the formal look without the variegation.

It is harder than pittosporum in cold weather, making it a safer option for inland gardens around Worth, Ash and Eastry where the temperature can drop several degrees lower than on the coast. Growth rate is moderate: you will get a reasonable hedge within two to three years from decent-sized starter plants. It clips cleanly with a hedge trimmer and does not tend to produce the brown patches that box blight creates. The main maintenance issue is that some variegated forms produce occasional reverted all-green shoots that can dominate if left uncut: remove these when trimming.

6. Teucrium x lucidrys (wall germander)

Wall germander is the smallest plant on this list: left to grow naturally it reaches only 40–50 cm, making it suited to low edging and knot-garden-style formal patterns rather than a boundary screen. Where it excels is on very dry, thin chalk or sandy soils that would challenge even box. The small grey-green aromatic leaves clip neatly; in summer unpruned plants produce mauve-pink flowers that attract bees strongly.

In a formal edging role around a kitchen garden or as a path border in Sandwich's drier town gardens, it is a good choice. It needs cutting in late spring before flowering and again in late summer. It is not the right plant for anyone wanting a hedge of more than knee height, but for low clipped edging it works well and is genuinely drought-tolerant in a way that box, with its shallow roots, never quite was on chalk soils.

When to trim your new hedge

Nesting season applies to all hedges, whatever species

Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 it is an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. The nesting season runs 1 March to 31 August. Even a newly planted alternative hedge can become a nesting site in its first or second year. The safe cutting window for any hedge trimming is September to February. If you need to trim during the nesting season, check carefully for active nests before you start.

All six plants listed above are best trimmed in late summer (August to September, once nesting season is well advanced) and again if needed in early spring (late February to early March, before nesting gets underway). The fastest-growing, Lonicera nitida, may also benefit from a light tidy in June if it is being kept to a strict shape. Avoid trimming Osmanthus during its April flowering period if you want the scent.

The RHS video "Alternatives to box", presented by RHS curator Matthew Pottage at RHS Wisley, walks through several of these plants growing in context and is well worth watching if you are still deciding between them.
