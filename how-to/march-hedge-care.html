March hedge care in Kent: nesting season begins

The first of March is the pivot point of the hedge-care year. The safe cutting window closes, growth restarts, and a different set of jobs takes over. Here is the complete March checklist for East Kent gardens.

Updated September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

March hedge care: your checklist at a glance

  • Very early March only: final light tidy on a hedge confirmed empty of nesting birds, before 10 March at the latest.
  • Feed: apply a balanced slow-release fertiliser along the root run before growth starts in earnest.
  • Mulch: 7 to 10 cm of bark chip or well-rotted compost along the base, kept 5 cm clear of stems, to lock in moisture.
  • Check for frost damage: walk the hedge and note any sections that browned or died back over winter.
  • Water newly planted hedges: as growth begins, new hedges need consistent moisture in dry spells.
  • Plan autumn work: book professional trimming for September to November before the season fills up.
  • Do not cut: any hedge you have not confirmed is empty of nesting birds. Most formal and established hedge cutting stops from 1 March.

March feels like a transition rather than a month, and for hedges it genuinely is. The dormant season ends, the safe cutting window closes, and the garden shifts into growth mode. Everything that was a straightforward job in February becomes a legal and practical judgement call from the first of March onwards.

In East Kent, the coastal mild winters around Sandwich Bay and Deal can accelerate the nesting calendar. Robins, one of the earliest nesters in the UK, can begin building in late February in a mild year. By the time the first week of March ends, the window for any significant cutting on most established hedges is effectively over.

1 March: the legal cut-off for most hedge trimming

Under the UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. The nesting season runs roughly 1 March to 31 August. From the first of March, any hedge cutting that damages an active nest is a criminal offence, even if the damage was accidental. The practical rule: treat 1 March as the cut-off for all significant hedge trimming unless you can confirm the hedge is empty, and even then limit any work to a very light tidy.

Before you start: if you are cutting in early March

  • Check for nests first: watch the hedge for two to three minutes before touching it. Look for birds flying in and out of the same spot. Check from underneath and from both sides.
  • Eye protection: safety glasses or goggles for any powered cutting.
  • Ear defenders: petrol and electric trimmers both exceed safe noise levels quickly.
  • Gloves: close-fitting, cut-resistant for spiny species such as hawthorn and blackthorn.
  • RCD adaptor: essential for corded electric trimmers, no exceptions.
  • Pole trimmer over a ladder: the ground is often soft and uneven in March. A long-reach pole trimmer is far safer than a ladder for the top of a taller hedge.

The March tasks, one by one

1. Any remaining cutting: early March only, and with care

A very light tidy on an established, open hedge that you can see right through and confirm is empty is not an offence in the first few days of March. However, most established hedges, the ones with dense growth and plenty of cover, should be left alone from the first. Robins, wrens and blue tits are already actively nest-building in East Kent by mid-February in a mild year, and by the second week of March nesting is well under way across the board.

If you absolutely must do some cutting in March, make it light, make it quick, and make it early. Do not take the opportunity to do the heavy work you postponed over winter. That is what September is for.

2. Feeding: the most valuable March task

March is the single best time of year to feed an established hedge. Growth is about to begin in earnest, and the fertiliser you apply now is taken up by the roots as the plant breaks dormancy. A balanced slow-release granular fertiliser, such as a general-purpose 7-7-7 or similar NPK formulation, broadcast along the root run on both sides of the hedge base and lightly raked in is the standard approach.

For hedges that are growing slowly or looking thin, a slightly higher-nitrogen spring feed gives an extra push. For hedges in excellent condition, a balanced feed is enough. Do not over-apply: excess nitrogen promotes fast, soft growth that is more susceptible to disease and less wind-resistant.

Hedges on the chalk soils around Eastry, Ash and Woodnesborough tend to be alkaline and can be short of trace elements. An occasional application of seaweed meal or a magnesium-rich feed (Epsom salts at 30 g per square metre) addresses trace-element deficiency on chalk sites, particularly for beech and hornbeam.

3. Mulching: lock in the spring moisture

Immediately after feeding, apply a mulch layer along the hedge base. A depth of 7 to 10 cm of bark chip, composted wood or well-rotted manure, kept 5 cm clear of the main stems to prevent collar rot, retains soil moisture through March and April when rainfall can be unreliable in East Kent.

The free-draining sandy and chalk soils around Sandwich, Worth and the coastal strip around Deal dry out quickly in a warm March. A mulch applied now pays dividends through the whole growing season and reduces the watering burden on any sections of newly planted hedge.

4. Check for winter frost damage

Walk the length of your hedge in March and note any sections that have browned, died back or suffered tip scorch over winter. Tender evergreens, pittosporum, choisya, griselinia and escallonia, are the most likely to show damage. In most cases, tip scorch on these species clears naturally as new growth comes through. Only consider trimming frost-damaged sections once you can see new growth and the risk of further frost is past, which in East Kent is usually April.

Brown patches on leylandii or conifer hedges that developed over winter require a different approach. See our conifer hedge going brown guide for diagnosis and options.

5. Watering newly planted hedges

Bare-root hedging planted through November to February will be starting to push its first new roots in March. These young plants are vulnerable to dry spells as the soil warms up. If March is dry, water newly planted hedges thoroughly once a week, about 10 litres per metre of hedge run, until they are visibly establishing with good leaf growth.

Established hedges generally do not need watering in March unless they are on very free-draining soils in an exposed site and the spring is exceptionally dry.

6. Plan and book autumn trimming

September, October and November are the peak months for professional hedge cutting in East Kent, and good contractors fill their books early. If you want your hedge cut in the main season, March is not too soon to make contact. Most jobs around Sandwich, Deal, Worth, Ash, Woodnesborough, Eastry and Sandwich Bay can be quoted from a photo or a brief description, without a site visit, for straightforward domestic hedges.

If your hedge needs renovation work, a significant height reduction or more than one full season's worth of overgrowth removing, booking early also means you get a sensible programme rather than a single rushed cut.

Horticulturists Simon and Lorna of Walking Talking Gardeners (two RHS-medal-holding gardeners with degrees from Greenwich University) produced a thorough March-specific guide, Never Prune, Cut or Trim these Plants in March, covering the wider range of garden plants that should be left alone this month. Useful context for the full picture beyond hedges.

March is the month when cutting stops and growing starts. The two or three weeks between the end of February and mid-March are the last chance for the year. Use them only if you genuinely need to, and then put the trimmer away until September.
