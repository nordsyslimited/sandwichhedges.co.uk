January hedge care in Kent: the renovation month

January is not a pause in the hedge-care calendar. It is the best month of the year to do heavy renovation work on deciduous hedges. The plants are dormant, the leaves are down and you can see exactly what you have. Here is what we actually do this month across Sandwich, Deal and the villages of East Kent.

Updated September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

There is a common assumption that January means leaving the garden to its own devices. For lawns and borders that is largely true. For hedges, it misses the point. The dormant season, broadly November through to the end of February, is the window when deciduous hedges can be renovated hard without stressing the plant. January sits right in the middle of it.

Around Sandwich Bay and Deal, the mild coastal climate means the ground stays workable for much of January. Inland villages, Woodnesborough, Eastry and Ash, see harder frosts earlier, but even there the bulk of January is practical working weather for hedge renovation. The sandy and chalky soils that run through this part of East Kent drain freely and do not become the mud traps that heavier soils in the Weald can turn into over winter.

January is in the safe legal cutting window

The UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. The nesting season runs roughly 1 March to 31 August. January is well within the safe window. There is no legal restriction on cutting in January, and no need to check for nests before starting on an established hedge. If you notice a bird regularly entering and leaving a particular spot, stop and investigate before cutting that section, but this is very unusual in January.

Before you start: safety

  • Eye protection: safety glasses or goggles for any powered cutting, even short sessions.
  • Ear defenders: petrol and electric trimmers both exceed safe noise levels quickly.
  • Gloves: sturdy, close-fitting gloves. Cut-resistant for spiny species such as hawthorn, blackthorn and holly.
  • RCD adaptor: essential for any corded electric trimmer. No exceptions.
  • Ladder awareness: ground is often soft and wet in January. A long-reach pole trimmer is far safer than a ladder for anything above head height. If a ladder is unavoidable, have someone footing it and never overreach sideways.
  • Check the forecast: avoid cutting evergreens in hard frost. Cut surfaces on holly and yew can suffer frost scorch before they harden if temperatures drop below -2°C within 48 hours of cutting.

What to do in January: task by task

1. Hard renovation of deciduous hedges

This is the primary January task, and the one that makes January exceptional in the hedge-care calendar. Beech, hornbeam, hawthorn, hazel, blackthorn and field maple are all fully dormant in January. With the leaves off beech and hornbeam, you can see the full structure of the hedge without any guesswork. Sections that have bulged, branches that have crossed, and the true width of a hedge that has spread over many years are all visible at a glance.

A standard renovation approach for an established beech or hornbeam hedge that has grown too wide is to cut one face hard in year one and the other face the following year. This gives the hedge time to produce new shoots from the cut face before the other side is taken. Both faces in a single season is possible for vigorous species like hawthorn and blackthorn, which regenerate fast enough to cover both cuts before spring. For beech and hornbeam, the two-year approach is kinder and produces a better result.

How hard is "hard"? On a healthy beech hedge that is three metres wide but should be one metre, you can cut back to the main structural branches with confidence. The plant will produce new growth from dormant buds along the old wood in spring. The cut looks alarming in January, but by August the hedge looks lush again. The same holds for most native deciduous species. A clean, sharp cut with a chainsaw or loppers on the structural branches produces the best result.

The leaves that beech and hornbeam hold all winter, the rusty brown papery remnants that cling to the branch until new growth pushes them off in spring, fall away naturally once the branch is cut. A hard January cut on a beech hedge therefore also removes the old brown leaf cover, which some gardeners find a bonus.

2. Holly: a light trim, and a useful one

Holly can be trimmed lightly in January without lasting harm. If you missed the December window for a formal holly hedge, early January is still fine. Keep the cut light and avoid exposing large areas of old brown wood, which will not regenerate. A holly hedge that has grown out of shape needs more careful management than a deciduous hedge: reduce it gradually over two or three seasons rather than in a single drastic cut.

Avoid cutting holly hard in late January if a frost is forecast within the week. The exposed cut surfaces need time to harden, and holly cut during a prolonged freeze can suffer tip dieback on the cut faces.

3. Yew: a gentle tidy if needed

Yew is one of the most cold-tolerant hedging species and tolerates light trimming in January. The same caution applies as with holly: avoid cutting in hard frost, and keep any January work to light shaping rather than significant reduction. Hard renovation of a yew hedge, reducing the height and width dramatically, is better done in late summer or early autumn when the plant has had the growing season to build reserves. A yew cut hard in January will regenerate, but it takes longer than a late-summer cut would.

4. What not to cut in January

A few species are best left alone until the growing season:

  • Leylandii and other conifers: avoid significant cuts. Leylandii, thuja, lawson cypress and western red cedar do not produce new growth from old brown wood. Cutting into the brown zone in January leaves a permanent brown patch. Light tidying of stray green shoots is acceptable; a proper annual trim is not. The right window for leylandii is May through October.
  • Tender evergreens (pittosporum, choisya, escallonia, griselinia): leave until March at the earliest. These species can suffer tip dieback on cut surfaces in cold weather. Around Sandwich Bay and the Deal coast, salt-laden winter winds from the Channel can be as damaging as frost for tender hedges after cutting. Wait for a mild, settled spell in late March.
  • Laurel: best left until March. Hard cuts in cold, wet weather can allow bacterial canker into the cut surfaces. If your laurel hedge is in genuinely poor shape and cannot wait, a dry mild spell in late January or early February is the best you can do, but March is safer.
  • Newly planted hedges: do not trim bare-root hedging you planted in autumn. Leave it to establish through its first spring before any formative trimming.

5. Mulching

January is a good time to mulch established hedges, particularly those on the free-draining chalk and sand soils around Sandwich, Worth and the coastal strip. A layer of 7 to 10 cm of bark chip or composted wood along the base of the hedge, kept 5 cm clear of the main stems, insulates the root zone against hard frosts and retains soil moisture through the dry spells that East Kent can see in late winter and early spring.

Do not apply fresh manure directly against hedge stems in January: the nitrogen concentration is too high for dormant plants and can cause root scorch. Well-rotted manure or composted garden material is fine.

6. Bare-root planting: the window is still open

The bare-root planting season runs from November through to the end of February. January is still a good planting month in Kent provided the ground is not frozen. Native species, privet, hawthorn, beech, hornbeam, blackthorn, hazel and field maple, establish well from January planting in East Kent's generally mild winters. The free-draining soils around Sandwich Bay and Worth allow roots to make contact with soil without the waterlogging issues that affect heavier clay soils elsewhere.

If you need to plant in the New Year, order bare-root stock now rather than waiting: reputable suppliers of the popular species often sell out by mid-January. See our guide to planting bare-root hedges for technique.

7. Plan and book the year's work

January is the quietest month for professional hedge contractors, which makes it a good time to arrange quotes and book work for the spring and autumn seasons. If your hedge needs a major renovation in January or February, contractors are available. If you want regular trimming in September or October, booking in January avoids the rush when the main season fills up fast.

Think through what each hedge in your garden actually needs this year. A beech that has grown a metre too wide? January renovation. A leylandii that needs an annual trim? Book it for July or September. A bare length of boundary that needs a new hedge? Order bare-root stock now and plant before the end of February.

January is when you can see what you have. The leaves are gone, the structure is clear, and the hedge cannot argue back. If you have been putting off a hard renovation, this is the month.
