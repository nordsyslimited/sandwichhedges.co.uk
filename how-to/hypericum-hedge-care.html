Hypericum ‘Hidcote’ as a low hedge in East Kent

An annual hard cut in late February, three months of yellow flowers from July, and almost no other care. The case for Hypericum as a low informal hedge.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Annual care checklist for Hypericum ‘Hidcote’

  1. Cut hard to 10–15 cm in late February or early March
  2. Apply a 5–8 cm mulch of composted bark after cutting, keeping it off the stems
  3. Water once a week in dry spells from April to September in year one
  4. Keep the base strip clear of weeds through spring and summer
  5. Watch for rust disease on the foliage from June onwards
  6. Divide or layer crowded sections every five to seven years to maintain vigour

Hypericum 'Hidcote' (St John's Wort) is not a hedge you see on every road in East Kent. It lacks the tall screening power of a privet or laurel boundary, and it has never been the go-to choice for the formal clipped look that many Kent front gardens use. But for a low informal hedge along a path, a driveway edge, or the front boundary of a smaller garden in Sandwich, Deal or Worth, it is a genuinely useful plant. It is semi-evergreen, extraordinarily easy to maintain with one annual cut, produces one of the longest flowering seasons of any hedging shrub and tolerates the chalky, free-draining soils of East Kent well.

What it is not: a tall screen, a formal clipped hedge or a low-maintenance substitute for privet. It needs the annual hard cut to perform. Skip it for two or three years, and the plant becomes leggy and woody with reduced flowering and an open, unattractive base. Keep to the routine below, and it looks good from April through to November.

Nesting season: 1 March to 31 August

The recommended annual hard cut for Hypericum falls in late February, which is just outside the main nesting season. If you are cutting later than early March, check the plant carefully before starting. Hypericum's open habit means it is not a primary nesting shrub, but late nesters and species using ground-level cover may still be present. Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to damage or destroy an active nest.

1. The annual hard cut: late February to early March

Hypericum 'Hidcote' flowers on growth made in the current year. The harder and earlier the cut, the more new growth the plant pushes through spring and the better the flowering display from July onwards. Cut the entire plant back to about 10 to 15 cm from the ground.

This looks alarming if you have not done it before. A Hypericum hedge in late February, after the cut, looks like a row of stubby bare sticks. Within four to six weeks, in the mild conditions that typically arrive across East Kent from late March, the new growth is already well under way. By the end of April the hedge is leafy again; by late June the buds are forming.

Use secateurs or loppers for a hedge of a few metres; a hedge trimmer cuts the soft growth but can leave a less tidy finish on thicker stems. Either way, clean up all the old growth and do not leave cut stems lying against the base, as they can carry fungal spores into the new growth.

2. Mulch after cutting

Apply a 5 to 8 cm layer of composted bark or similar mulch along the base of the hedge immediately after the annual cut. This retains moisture through spring as new growth begins, suppresses weeds that would otherwise compete for water and nutrients during establishment, and gradually improves the soil structure. Keep the mulch clear of the cut stems by a few centimetres: material against the stems encourages fungal problems.

East Kent's chalk-based soils drain freely, which is an advantage for Hypericum (it dislikes waterlogged conditions) but means moisture availability in spring can be low during dry spells. Mulching makes a noticeable difference in year one and is worth continuing as an annual habit.

3. Watering in year one

An established Hypericum hedge is drought-tolerant and needs no additional watering in a typical Kent season. In the first year after planting, however, watering once a week through any dry spell from April to September helps the plant establish quickly and fills in gaps in the hedge row.

By year two, supplementary watering is rarely needed except in extended dry periods in July and August, which can occur in East Kent's coastal climate, where summers are often drier than the national average.

4. Keep the base clear

Grass and weeds growing into the base of a Hypericum hedge compete for water and can cause the lower stems to become drawn and leggy. Keep a weed-free strip of roughly 30 cm either side of the hedge for the first two years, either with the mulch described above or with hand-weeding. By year three, the spread of the hedge itself suppresses most weeds.

5. Watch for rust disease

Hypericum can develop rust (Melampsora hypericorum), a fungal disease that appears as orange-red pustules on the undersides of leaves from June onwards. It rarely kills an established plant but weakens the foliage and reduces the display. Collect and bin (not compost) affected leaves and avoid overhead watering. Improving air circulation around the base of the hedge helps. The annual hard cut removes all affected growth and gives the plant a clean start the following season.

6. Divide or layer every five to seven years

After several years of the annual cut-and-regrow cycle, sections of a Hypericum hedge can become crowded and woody at the centre, with reduced vigour. Lifting and dividing the most congested plants, or layering a few stems to create new plants that fill gaps, refreshes the hedge without having to replant from scratch. This is best done in autumn after the flowers and seed heads have finished, or in early spring before the annual cut.

What Hypericum looks like in years one to three

Year one: After the initial cut-back (if the hedge came as established plants) and mulching, new growth fills in through spring. By July, the first flowering display. If planted as small bare-root or pot-grown specimens rather than established plants, the first year is about root establishment and the display may be modest.

Year two: The annual cut in late February, followed by noticeably stronger growth than year one. The hedge begins to look like a hedge rather than a row of plants. The July to August flowering is the best of the first three years as root systems are established and vigorous.

Year three: A fully established hedge. The annual cut produces a dense, bushy growth habit by mid-summer, with continuous flowering from July through September in a typical East Kent season. At this point the hedge is largely self-sufficient: annual cut, mulch, occasional weeding. The October seed heads attract some bird activity before the cycle repeats in late February.
