Hedges for chalky soil in East Kent: which species thrive and which will struggle

The North Downs escarpment runs straight through East Kent, and many villages between Barham and Sandwich sit on thin, pale, alkaline soil overlying chalk. Plant the wrong hedging species here and they yellow and sulk for years. Plant the right ones and they establish quickly and need almost no coaxing.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

East Kent chalk: what you're dealing with

Chalk soils in East Kent typically have a thin layer of topsoil, anywhere from 10 to 40 centimetres, over solid or broken chalk. The chalk itself is porous and freely draining, which means water runs straight through in winter and the soil dries quickly in summer. The pH is alkaline, usually 7.5 to 8.5.

Villages particularly affected include Eastry, Woodnesborough, Ash, Worth, Barham, Denton, Wingham and parts of the valley running west from Sandwich. Sandwich itself and the coastal strip around Deal tend to have heavier brickearth and marine sediment, which is better-drained and less extreme than pure chalk but can still be alkaline.

The practical consequences for hedging are three: waterlogging in winter is rarely a problem; summer drought stress in the first few years after planting is common; and acid-loving species simply will not grow. Within those constraints, East Kent chalk is surprisingly productive. Some of the most vigorous beech and yew in the county grow on North Downs chalk downland, because that is what they evolved on.

A UK horticulturist's practical guide to hedging for different soil types covers the key differences between chalk, clay and sandy soils and is worth watching before you buy.

Species comparison: chalk in East Kent

The table below organises common hedging species by chalk suitability. "Thrives" means the species is naturally adapted to alkaline, free-draining ground and performs as well or better here than on richer soils. "Tolerates" means the species will grow adequately on chalk if given reasonable establishment support. "Avoid" means the species needs acid or neutral soil and will not thrive on alkaline ground regardless of what you do to it.

Species Chalk suitability Notes for East Kent
Beech (Fagus sylvatica) Thrives Chalk downland native. Very well-suited to thin alkaline soils. Needs watering in first two summers.
Yew (Taxus baccata) Thrives Superb on chalk, slow-growing but very long-lived. Tolerates part-shade beneath larger trees.
Hawthorn (Crataegus monogyna) Thrives Fast native. Excellent chalk-downland species, wildlife-friendly, establishes reliably.
Field maple (Acer campestre) Thrives Classic chalk-downland hedgerow component. Good autumn colour. Works well in mixed native hedges.
Blackthorn (Prunus spinosa) Thrives Vigorous on chalk. Provides dense stock-proof cover and early spring blossom. Spreads by suckers.
Box (Buxus sempervirens) Thrives Native to chalk downland in southern England. Slow but reliable on chalk. Subject to box blight — see our box blight guide.
Dogwood (Cornus sanguinea) Thrives Native chalk-downland shrub. Good for wild hedges and wildlife. Red winter stems.
Privet (Ligustrum ovalifolium) Tolerates Grows well on chalk with reasonable establishment watering. Semi-evergreen. Very common in East Kent gardens.
Hornbeam (Carpinus betulus) Tolerates Prefers moist soils. Grows on chalk but slower than beech. Good alternative where beech has struggled.
Leylandii (× C. leylandii) Tolerates Will grow on chalk but performs better on deeper soils. May show summer stress if topsoil is very thin.
Viburnum tinus Tolerates Generally tolerant of chalk. Evergreen, winter flowers. Good for informal or coastal hedging.
Griselinia Tolerates Coastal-adapted and generally tolerant. May struggle in prolonged drought on very thin chalk.
Rhododendron Avoid Needs acid soil (pH 4.5–6.0). Will yellow and decline on chalk. No fix for this.
Camellia Avoid Needs acid soil. Chlorosis and poor performance on chalk.
Pieris Avoid Acid-loving ericaceous species. Will not perform on alkaline chalk.
Photinia (Red Robin) Tolerates Tolerant of chalk. Slower on alkaline soils than on neutral. Worth trying in sheltered spots.

The best choices for East Kent chalk: detail on the top four

Beech

Beech is the natural hedgerow species of the chalk downland that covers much of East Kent, and it shows in the results: beech grown on chalk here often establishes more reliably than beech grown on waterlogged clay further north. The roots push through the chalk subsoil relatively quickly and find moisture even in dry summers. The marcescent leaf habit (keeping dead leaves through winter) gives an attractive buff-bronze appearance from October to April, which appeals to many householders.

The most important thing with beech on chalk: water thoroughly in the first two summers. The thin soil layer over chalk dries out in June and July, and newly planted beech without an established root system cannot reach the deeper moisture that mature trees access. One deep watering per week in dry weather during the first two summers is enough. After that, beech on chalk largely looks after itself.

Bare-root beech plants, available from November, are the most economical option. At 40–60 cm plant spacing they form a solid hedge in four to six years.

Yew

Yew (Taxus baccata) is slower than beech but arguably the most permanent hedging option available in East Kent. It grows 20–30 cm per year on good chalk, which feels slow at first but once established, a yew hedge requires just one cut per year and will outlive the person who planted it by centuries. Historic yew hedges around the churches and manor gardens of Eastry, Woodnesborough and Ash are testimony to this.

Unlike beech, yew tolerates part-shade, which makes it useful on the north or east side of a wall, or where a building casts morning shade across the boundary. It also regenerates from old wood, which means it can be renovated aggressively if it outgrows its space, something beech and leylandii cannot match.

Note: all parts of yew are toxic to humans, horses, cattle and dogs. This matters during planting and clipping. Wear gloves when handling fresh clippings, bag and bin them rather than leaving them on the ground, and do not use yew where livestock or grazing animals might reach the cuttings.

Hawthorn

For a wildlife hedge, boundary hedge or rural boundary on chalk, hawthorn (Crataegus monogyna) is the first recommendation. It establishes faster than any of the previous two, is completely hardy, tolerates chalk superbly, and provides dense, spiny cover that acts as a stock barrier as well as a visual screen. Spring flowers (May blossom) support early pollinators; autumn berries (haws) provide food for fieldfares, redwings and other winter thrushes that pass through East Kent from October.

Mixed native hedgerows on chalk often include hawthorn, blackthorn, field maple, hazel and dogwood together. This mix is authentically local to the North Downs landscape and far better for wildlife than a monoculture of any single species. Bare-root mixed native hedgerow bundles are available from November through February.

Field maple

Field maple (Acer campestre) deserves to be more widely planted on East Kent chalk. It is native to the chalk downlands, provides reliable autumn colour (yellow-gold in October), supports insects and birds, and responds well to clipping into a formal or semi-formal hedge. It is slower-growing than hawthorn and faster than yew, landing in a useful middle ground.

Field maple is very common in the hedgerows of the chalk valleys running south and west from Sandwich, which is good evidence that it belongs in this landscape. Where a formal single-species hedge is wanted on alkaline ground, field maple is an underused alternative to beech.

Improving the soil before planting

Chalk soils do not need lime (they have more than enough already). What they benefit from is organic matter: well-rotted manure, garden compost or leaf mould dug into the planting trench. This improves water retention on the thin topsoil layer, feeds soil biology and gives newly planted root systems a richer immediate environment than raw chalk.

Dig the planting trench 40 cm deep and 40 cm wide. If you hit chalk within the first 20 cm, break it up as much as possible with a spade or bar. Mix organic matter through the soil you remove and use this enriched mix to backfill around the plants. Apply a 10 cm mulch of composted bark or wood chip along the full length of the new hedge, keeping it off the stems, to retain moisture through the first summer.

Do not add fertiliser at planting time on chalk. The organic matter provides enough nutrient for establishment, and a high-nitrogen fertiliser on chalk in autumn or winter is wasted.
