Renovating an overgrown yew hedge

An overgrown yew hedge can be brought back into shape, often dramatically. Unlike most conifers, yew regrows from old wood. This guide walks through the decisions: is renovation the right call, how hard is safe to cut, and what to do in what order.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Yew (Taxus baccata) is unique among the common UK hedging conifers: it will regrow from old, brown wood after hard cutting. Leylandii will not. Lawson cypress will not. Thuja generally will not. Yew will, reliably, given the right conditions and timing.

This matters because it means a yew hedge that has been allowed to grow far beyond its intended dimensions can often be brought back to a manageable size without being removed. Many of the best formal gardens in East Kent have yew hedges that have been renovated one or more times over their lives. The old rectory gardens around Eastry and the formal boundaries in Sandwich contain yew that has been cut hard and come back dense and green.

That said, renovation is not always the right choice. Work through the decisions below before you commit to a hard cut.

Before you cut: check the nesting season

Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Yew makes excellent nesting cover and is used by many species. Nesting season runs approximately 1 March to 31 August. The safe renovation window is September to February. Renovation cuts made in this window are both legal and, as it happens, the most effective.

Decision 1: Is it really yew?

This sounds obvious, but it matters enormously. Yew is not the only dark-green, dense-leaved conifer grown as a hedge in East Kent. Leylandii, Lawson cypress and western red cedar all look similar at a distance, particularly when overgrown. The critical difference is what happens when you cut them back to old wood.

Yew leaves are flat, dark green above and lighter below, arranged in two rows along a twig. The bark is reddish-brown, often flaking in thin papery strips on older stems. If you scratch the bark with your thumbnail, the wood underneath is pale cream or white. Yew stems are flexible; old stems are hard but not brittle.

Leylandii and other conifers with scale-like foliage (overlapping flat scales rather than needle-like leaves in rows) will not regenerate from brown wood. If you are not certain which species you have, do not attempt a hard renovation cut until you are. If in doubt, ask someone who knows conifers to identify the plant before you cut.

Decision 2: Is the hedge alive and healthy?

Before deciding how much to cut, assess the overall health of the hedge. Walk along it and look for:

  • Dead sections: brown, lifeless patches with no green growth anywhere on the stem. These will not regrow after cutting.
  • Phytophthora dieback: a section that has died suddenly, often with the roots gone rotten when you dig nearby. This is usually caused by waterlogged soil. Renovation will not succeed here unless drainage is improved first.
  • Gaps at the base: bare stems with no foliage at ground level. Renovation can often encourage new growth into these gaps from adjacent live growth, but it is a slow process.
  • Otherwise healthy growth: if the hedge is green and vigorous throughout, renovation is very likely to succeed.

A hedge with one or two dead sections in an otherwise healthy run can still be renovated. Work around the dead sections and treat them separately: either remove them and replant, or use the adjacent healthy growth to fill the gap over time.

Decision 3: How overgrown is it?

The degree of overgrowth determines the approach:

Lightly overgrown: the hedge is 20–30% larger than its intended dimensions and has not been cut for two or three years. A single renovation cut back to the target dimensions in one season is safe and will produce a good result. Carry out the cut in late August or September, or in February.

Moderately overgrown: the hedge is roughly twice the intended width or significantly taller than you want, and has been left for five or more years. Use the two-season approach (see below). Do not cut both sides hard in the same season.

Severely overgrown: the hedge is very much larger than intended and has been unmanaged for many years. Consider whether renovation is realistic or whether removal and replanting is the more practical option. Very old, very large yew hedges can still be renovated, but the work is substantial, the recovery is slow, and in some cases the structural value of the old hedge (privacy, windbreak) disappears for several years during the process. If the hedge is a significant garden feature, it may be worth getting professional advice before cutting.

The two-season renovation approach

For moderately overgrown yew hedges, the standard approach is:

Season one, late August to September (or February): cut one side of the hedge hard, back to the desired finished width, exposing old brown wood on that face. Leave the other side, the top and the front uncut. This keeps green foliage on three faces while one face begins its recovery.

Season two, same window a year later: once new shoots are appearing on the cut face, cut the other side back similarly. The top can be reduced at this stage too if height is an issue.

By the end of year two, all faces are on their way to recovery. The cut surfaces will look sparse for another two to three years before the new growth fills in to a dense, finished surface.

Cutting the top

Reducing the height of a yew hedge follows the same principle as cutting the sides, with one practical difference: the top cut is more visible and the finished line is harder to correct later. Mark the target height with a string line before cutting. If you are reducing height by more than 60 cm, consider doing it in two stages over two seasons rather than all at once, particularly if the hedge is a prominent feature.

After a hard top cut, yew grows back from the exposed wood. The new growth initially shoots vertically from the cut surface. These vertical shoots can be tipped back lightly in year two and shaped into the finished surface in years three and four.

What to expect during recovery

In the first four to eight weeks after a renovation cut in the growing season, small new shoots begin to appear from the cut surface. These are initially bright, pale green and sparse. By autumn of year one, the cut face is covered with these shoots but still looks rough.

Year two: the shoots thicken and begin to resemble hedge foliage. A light clipping in late August of year two helps tighten the structure. Do not cut hard in year two; the new growth needs to build up bulk before it can support a formal finish.

Years three and four: the surface becomes progressively denser. By the end of year four, a well-renovated yew hedge can look presentable from a normal viewing distance. Full recovery to a tight, formal surface generally takes four to six years.

When to call in professional help

Yew renovation involves working close to thick, hard stems with power tools, often at height. If the hedge is tall, the top cut requires a ladder or platform, and the volume of waste material can be substantial. Yew clippings are toxic to livestock and many animals, so disposal needs to be managed.

For large hedges, complex renovation or any work that requires working above head height, using a professional saves time and reduces risk. We carry out yew renovation across Sandwich, Deal, Worth, Ash, Woodnesborough and Eastry. Get in touch to discuss what your hedge needs.

Disposing of yew clippings

Yew clippings are toxic to cattle, horses and most livestock. Do not leave them where animals can reach them. Do not compost them in a heap that might be accessible to animals. Place in sealed green-waste bags for council collection, or arrange disposal with the contractor if you are having the work done professionally.
