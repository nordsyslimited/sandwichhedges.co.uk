Weed control around a new hedge: the first three years

The most common reason a newly planted hedge takes years longer than it should to establish is not poor plants or bad soil. It is grass and weeds left to compete unchecked in the planting strip.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Weed control checklist: new hedge establishment

  • Before planting: clear all perennial weed roots from a 60–90 cm strip along the planting line
  • At planting: lay a weed-suppressing membrane or biodegradable mulch mat, slitting for each plant
  • At planting: cover the mat with 7–10 cm of composted bark or wood chip mulch
  • Gap at stems: keep a 5 cm clear gap around every plant base to prevent collar rot
  • Year 1–2: hand-weed every four to six weeks from March to October
  • All years: never let grass re-establish contact with plant stems
  • Each spring: top up the mulch layer to maintain 7–10 cm depth
  • Year 3: reduce weeding frequency once the hedge is shading the base

Why weed competition matters more than most people expect

When you plant a row of bare-root whips in November or December, they look like sticks in the ground. The gap between soil and the first leaf buds is months away. It is easy to assume there is nothing much happening beneath the surface that weeds could interfere with.

In reality the opposite is true. Those roots are under immediate competitive pressure from whatever is growing in the surrounding soil. Grass in particular is remarkably aggressive at drawing down soil moisture from the top 20 cm, which is precisely where newly planted hedge whips will concentrate their early root growth. A well-established lawn or grass strip can reduce the available soil moisture around young hedge plants significantly even during weeks when rainfall looks adequate on paper.

The practical consequence is that hedge plants competing with grass grow noticeably more slowly in their first two years. In parts of East Kent with chalk-based soils, particularly around Sandwich, Ash and Eastry where drainage is fast and summer moisture retention is low, the gap in establishment speed between weeded and unweeded hedging plants is wider still. Chalky soils drain quickly and retain less moisture to begin with, so competition from grass tips new hedge plants into drought stress faster during dry spells from May to September.

Getting the first three years right is not about being fussy. It is about whether a hedge planted this winter looks like a hedge by the time it enters its third growing season, or whether it is still a row of straggly stems at the same height it was in year one.

Before you plant: clearing the strip

The single most effective thing you can do for weed control around a new hedge is to clear perennial weed roots from the planting strip before the plants go in. Annual weeds are manageable after planting. Established perennial weeds, particularly couch grass, bindweed and ground elder, are far harder to deal with once they are growing through or alongside a row of young hedge plants.

Clear a strip of 60–90 cm along the full length of the planting line. Remove every piece of couch grass root (white, rhizomatous, spreading) and every piece of bindweed root (white or cream, thicker, brittle and easily snapped). A single couch grass root left in the ground will re-establish and spread; a broken bindweed fragment will regrow. Take time to sift the top 15–20 cm of soil by hand if necessary.

If the site has a severe perennial weed problem and planting is not imminent, a glyphosate-based contact weedkiller applied to actively growing weeds in September or October will give a reasonable kill before winter planting. Allow at least three weeks between application and planting.

Mulch mat and organic mulch: the two-layer approach

A weed-suppressing membrane or biodegradable mulch mat laid along the planting strip is the most effective long-term weed control measure for a new hedge. The mat blocks light from reaching annual weed seeds, reducing germination significantly. It also retains soil moisture, which is a secondary benefit for newly planted hedge plants during dry spells.

Biodegradable jute or coir mulch mats are preferable to plastic weed membrane where the site allows, particularly along hedging where wildlife access at the base is desirable. Plastic membrane is more durable but should be removed after three to four years once the hedge is established, as it can restrict root development and earthworm activity in the long term.

Slit the mat at each plant position rather than cutting full circles. The mat should run continuously along the planting strip, overlapping at joins by at least 15 cm to prevent weeds pushing through.

Cover the mat with 7–10 cm of composted bark chip or wood chip mulch. This holds the mat in place, adds organic matter to the soil surface as it breaks down, retains additional moisture and makes the planting strip look presentable from day one. Keep a 5 cm clear gap around every plant stem: bark mulch piled against young stems can cause collar rot, particularly in wet autumns and winters.

Hand weeding in years one and two

Even with a mat and mulch layer, weeds will appear in and around the mulch. Annual weeds germinate in the mulch itself; perennial weeds push up through the slits at plant bases; grass works its way in from the edges. A weeding round every four to six weeks from March to October is realistic for a new hedge in its first two growing seasons.

Year one requires the most vigilance. The hedge plants are small, the mulch mat is new and may not yet be fully bedded, and grass from the surrounding lawn or border will be actively trying to re-establish in the cleared strip. By year two the hedge itself begins to shade the ground along the base, which reduces annual weed germination significantly.

Use a hand hoe or long-handled weeder rather than a fork wherever possible. Disturbance of the soil around young hedge roots is worth minimising, particularly near the base of the plants where fine feeding roots are establishing. For weeds growing through the mat at plant slits, hand pulling is safer than any tool.

Dealing with grass re-establishing at the edges

Grass grows back into the cleared strip from both edges, particularly if the hedge is planted alongside a lawn. A string trimmer used carelessly at the base of young hedge plants is one of the most common causes of damage to new hedge whips: the spinning line strips bark from the base of the stem efficiently, and the damage is not always immediately obvious.

Maintain a clear edge between the mulch strip and any surrounding lawn with a half-moon edging iron, keeping the edge defined throughout the growing season. If a strimmer must be used to tidy the edge, keep it well away from plant bases and work from outside the mulch strip inward, never the other way around.

Once grass re-establishes contact with young plant stems it competes directly for moisture from the soil immediately around the base. Keep the 60–90 cm strip grass-free for the first three growing seasons as a minimum.

Topping up mulch each spring

The organic mulch layer breaks down over winter, dropping from 10 cm to perhaps 5–6 cm by the following March. Top up to 8–10 cm each spring, before the main flush of annual weed germination in April. Fresh composted bark chip is effective; well-rotted wood chip or leaf mould also works well and adds more organic matter to the soil as it breaks down.

Avoid fresh, unrotted wood chip on a new hedge. The decomposition process consumes soil nitrogen temporarily as it proceeds, which can deplete nitrogen availability for the hedge plants in the immediate planting strip. Composted or aged bark chip, which has already gone through the main decomposition phase, does not have this effect.

When to reduce weeding

Most hedging species establish well enough by the end of year three to begin shading their own base, which suppresses annual weed germination naturally. Beech, hornbeam and yew, which are slower to establish, may need a fourth year of mulch maintenance before you can rely on shade to do the work. Privet, laurel and leylandii are faster and will typically shade the base by year two or three.

Persistent perennial weeds, particularly bindweed, may need ongoing attention even after the hedge is established. Bindweed that has established below a hedge can be difficult to clear without disturbing the hedge roots. Painting the growing tips of bindweed with a glyphosate-based gel weedkiller is the most targeted approach once the hedge is established, keeping the chemical away from hedge foliage.

Once the hedge is established and shading the base adequately, the annual weeding routine can step down to a once-over in spring and autumn to remove any persistent perennial weeds that have come back through the mulch or from the edges.
