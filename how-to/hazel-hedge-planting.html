How to plant a hazel hedge in Kent

Hazel is native to Kent, happy on chalk, and one of the best hedging plants for wildlife in East Kent gardens. Here are the seven steps that give bare-root hazel whips the best start.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Hazel (Corylus avellana) is one of the most valuable hedging plants for wildlife in East Kent. It produces catkins from January, provides dense cover and nesting sites by April, and bears cobnuts by September: food for dormice, squirrels, fieldfares and a range of moths and butterflies. The countryside around Sandwich Bay, Ash and Woodnesborough has had hazel in its hedgerows since before Domesday.

As a garden hedge it is less common than beech or privet, partly because it is not evergreen and partly because it is vigorous enough to need an annual cut. But for people who want a wildlife hedge rather than a formal screen, it is one of the most rewarding options available. The catkins alone are worth it in a coastal Kent January.

Planting is straightforward. Bare-root hazel whips are cheap, available from November to March, and establish quickly if the initial hard cut is made correctly.

Plant now, do not trim until after August

Planting is fine from November to March, but once the hedge is established and nesting season begins around 1 March, it must not be cut until after 31 August. Hazel is an excellent nesting habitat. Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 it is an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. The initial hard coppice cut described in Step 7 should be carried out in November to January, well before nesting begins.

What you will need

  • Bare-root hazel whips (60–80 cm or 80–100 cm), 3 per metre of hedge length
  • Garden spade and fork
  • Garden compost or well-rotted manure
  • Rabbit guards or spiral guards if rabbits are present
  • Canes (optional, for support in exposed sites near the coast)
  • Mulch (wood chip or cardboard and straw) for the first winter

Step 1: Choose the right time

November is the ideal month for planting bare-root hazel in East Kent. The soil is still warm enough for some root establishment before hard frosts, the ground is workable after autumn rain, and the plants are fully dormant. Bare-root stock is available from most UK nurseries from November through to late February or early March.

Avoid planting in February or March if the soil is dry. East Kent has a relatively dry late-winter climate, and hazel planted just before or during a dry March may not establish well without additional watering.

Step 2: Mark out the line and prepare the trench

Use a garden line or string to mark the hedge line accurately before you dig. A hedge that wanders even slightly will look crooked within a year or two.

Dig a trench approximately 30 cm wide and 25–30 cm deep along the full length. For a double-staggered row (useful for a wildlife or stock-proof hedge), dig two parallel trenches 30–40 cm apart, staggering the planting positions between rows. For a garden boundary hedge, a single row is sufficient.

Step 3: Improve the soil

Hazel grows on the chalk downland of East Kent with minimal soil improvement, but incorporating compost into the planting trench makes a significant difference in the first two years. Work a generous layer of garden compost or well-rotted manure into the base and sides of the trench. On thin chalk with low organic matter, this is particularly important for moisture retention during the first dry summer.

Do not add fertiliser at planting. Bare-root whips need root contact and moisture, not a nutrient boost. Excess nitrogen in the first year can cause lush top growth on a plant with too few roots to support it.

Step 4: Heel in or plant immediately

If your bare-root plants arrive before you are ready to plant, heel them into a temporary trench in a sheltered spot, keeping the roots covered with soil and moist. Do not leave bare roots exposed to wind or frost, even for a few hours. Roots that dry out will not recover fully.

When you are ready to plant, check the roots: if they look dry, soak them in a bucket of water for 30–60 minutes before planting. Hazel roots are fibrous and recover quickly from mild desiccation if soaked promptly.

Step 5: Set out the spacing and plant

Space plants 30–35 cm apart (roughly three per metre) along the trench. Place each whip in the trench at the depth it was grown: look for the soil mark at the base of the stem, which shows the original nursery planting depth. Plant to roughly this depth, or very slightly deeper on chalk to give the roots more soil cover.

Backfill around each plant, firming the soil down with your heel as you go. This removes air pockets that allow roots to dry out. Work along the row rather than finishing one plant at a time: it is easier to keep even spacing and backfilling depth.

Step 6: Protect and mulch

If rabbits are present on the site, fit spiral guards or mesh rabbit guards around each stem immediately after planting. Around the Sandwich Bay area and on farmland edges near Eastry and Worth, rabbits are a persistent problem for newly planted whips and will strip the bark at the base of unprotected stems over winter.

Mulch the entire planted strip to a depth of 5–8 cm with wood chip, cardboard and straw, or a thick layer of leaf mould. Keep the mulch slightly away from the stems to prevent rotting. Mulching suppresses weeds, retains moisture in spring and insulates the roots against late frosts.

Step 7: Make the hard coppice cut

This step is the one most people miss, and it is the most important. In the first winter after planting (November to January), cut every stem back to 15–30 cm from the ground. This is not trimming: it is a severe coppice cut, leaving almost nothing above soil level. It looks drastic. Do it anyway.

The purpose is to force the plant to develop a dense, multi-stemmed base from which all future hedge growth comes. A hazel whip left uncut will grow as a single-stemmed shrub, producing a hedge that is thin and gappy at the base within a few years. After the coppice cut, the plant throws up multiple new shoots in spring, and after two or three seasons the base of the hedge fills in properly.

In the second winter, cut back again by roughly one-third. By the third year the hedge should be developing the density you need and can be managed with a standard annual trim from late August onwards.
