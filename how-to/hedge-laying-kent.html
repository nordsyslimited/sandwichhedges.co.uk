Hedge laying in Kent: a step-by-step guide to a traditional craft

Hedge laying turns an overgrown or gappy hedge into a stockproof, wildlife-rich boundary. Here is how it works, when to do it in Kent, and the tools and technique needed to get the plashing cut right first time.

Updated August 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Hedge laying is one of the oldest forms of land management in Britain. Its principle is simple: cut the main stems of a neglected or overgrown hedge most of the way through, bend them at a low angle and weave them between upright stakes to create a dense, self-supporting barrier. The cut stem lives, draws sap through the thin tongue of bark left intact, and throws up vigorous new growth that thickens the hedge from the base. Done well, a laid hedge is far denser and more wildlife-rich than the same hedge managed only by annual trimming.

Country Living magazine commissioned a demonstration of the traditional technique in their video "How to lay a hedge with master of the craft Paul Lamb", published November 2024 on the Country Living UK YouTube channel. Country Living is published by Hearst UK, with over 1.4 million readers. The guide below covers the full process in the context of Kent hedgerows, with notes on local species, timing and what to expect in the first year after laying.

Before you start: PPE and nesting season

Personal protective equipment: Hedge laying involves sharp hand tools and occasional chainsaw use on larger stems. Wear cut-resistant gloves (EN388 rated), eye protection, steel-toecap boots and, if using a chainsaw for any cuts, full chainsaw PPE including chaps, helmet and visor. Check the base of the hedge for wasp nests before beginning, especially in autumn. If using electric tools, fit an RCD to the circuit.

Nesting season: Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 (WCA 1981), intentionally damaging or destroying an active bird's nest, or disturbing nesting birds, is a criminal offence. The active nesting season runs from 1 March to 31 August. The safe window for hedge laying is September to February. Inspect the hedge carefully for active nests before starting. If you find a nest with eggs or young, stop work on that section until the nest is vacated.

What hedge laying achieves

A conventionally trimmed hedge keeps its shape but rarely thickens at the base. Over years of annual cutting, the internal structure becomes woody and hollow, with gaps at the bottom where animals can pass through. Hedge laying reverses this. By cutting the main stems and laying them horizontally, you force new growth from the base upward, filling the gaps and creating a barrier that livestock cannot push through. In East Kent, laid hedges were historically used as stockproof boundaries between fields from Sandwich Bay to the Ash and Eastry Plateau, and many of the older hedgerows visible in the countryside today were last laid rather than planted.

Which species lay well in Kent

Not all hedgerow species are suitable for laying. The technique works only on deciduous native species with the cellular structure needed to survive the plashing cut.

  • Hawthorn (Crataegus monogyna): the classic hedge-laying species in Kent and across England. Pliable, tough, and produces prolific new growth after laying. The thorny stems become part of the stockproof barrier. See our hawthorn hedge care guide for general management notes.
  • Blackthorn (Prunus spinosa): extremely tough and thorny; slightly less pliable than hawthorn but lays well. The suckering habit means it fills gaps quickly after laying.
  • Field maple (Acer campestre): very pliable stems that rarely split during laying. Good for filling gaps between hawthorn sections.
  • Hazel (Corylus avellana): the most forgiving species for beginners; stems bend easily without snapping. Often used for the binding rods (binders) laid along the top of the stakes.
  • Elm (Ulmus spp.) shoots and ash (Fraxinus excelsior): both lay well when stems are young. Less commonly the main species in a Kent hedgerow but often present in mixed boundaries.

Species that cannot be laid include conifers (leylandii, thuja, lawson cypress), cherry laurel, privet and any evergreen shrub hedge. These do not have the right cell structure to survive the plashing cut.

When to lay a hedge in Kent

The traditional hedge-laying season runs from late October to the end of February. The practical limits are driven by two factors: leaves need to have fully dropped (so you can see the stem structure clearly and work without obstruction), and work must be complete before new growth begins in March. In Kent, November and December are usually the best months, when the ground is firm enough to work without compacting it badly and the days are long enough to make reasonable progress. January works well in mild years. February is possible but slightly risky on a long hedge if mild weather brings early bud burst.

The WCA 1981 nesting season callout above sets the legal limit: no hedge-laying work should begin before the previous year's nesting activity has ended (1 September at the earliest; in practice late October is the safe start, after the last broods of the season have fledged).

Tools you will need

  • Billhook: the primary tool for making the plashing cut. A Kent-pattern billhook has a short, curved blade well suited to working in close quarters. Keep it sharp: a blunt billhook tears rather than cuts and makes the plashing cut far harder to control.
  • Pruning saw: for thicker stems (over 5cm diameter) where a billhook cut is impractical.
  • Loppers: for removing side growth and clearing the working area before laying begins.
  • Chainsaw (optional): useful for very large-diameter stems, but requires appropriate certification and full PPE. Not needed for most garden or small-field hedges.
  • Stakes: hazel or ash poles, 1.8 to 2m long and 3 to 4cm diameter, sharpened at one end. Drive them at roughly 50 to 60cm spacing along the line of the hedge after laying the pleachers.
  • Binders: long, flexible rods of hazel or willow, typically 2 to 3m, woven between the tops of the stakes to lock the structure in place.
  • Mallet or post driver: for driving stakes without splitting them.
  • Knee pads: you will be working low to the ground for extended periods.

Step 1: Survey the hedge and clear side growth

Walk the hedge before starting and identify the main stems (pleachers) you plan to lay. Aim for one pleacher every 20 to 30cm along the hedge line. Stems should be no more than about 10cm in diameter at the base; anything larger is very difficult to lay without splitting. Thick, dead-centre stems that cannot be moved should be cut out completely rather than laid.

Clear all side growth (the smaller branches coming off the main stems) with loppers and a billhook. You are creating a clear working space around each pleacher before you make the plashing cut. Stack the brash to one side for chipping or burning later; in Kent, green hawthorn brash is best chipped rather than burned, as burning close to the hedge can damage roots.

Step 2: Make the plashing cut

The plashing cut is the defining skill of hedge laying. It is made at the base of the pleacher stem, 5 to 15cm above ground level. The aim is to cut approximately 75 to 90 per cent of the way through the stem, leaving a thin tongue of bark and sapwood intact on the far side. This tongue is the lifeline of the laid stem: it carries sap from the root system to the length of the pleacher, keeping it alive and promoting new growth.

How to make the cut:

  1. Stand to one side of the stem, not directly behind it in the direction of lay.
  2. Strike downward and at a low angle with the billhook, beginning on the side of the stem that will be under compression when bent.
  3. Work the blade progressively through the stem, checking regularly to judge the depth remaining.
  4. Stop when you can feel the stem beginning to give. Test by applying gentle pressure in the direction of lay.
  5. If the stem moves without cracking, the cut is deep enough. If it snaps rather than bending, the tongue was too thin.

On thicker stems (over 6cm), use a pruning saw to make a partial cut first, then finish with the billhook to create a clean, slanted heel. The key rule: err on the side of leaving slightly more wood rather than less. A tongue that is marginally too thick bends more slowly; one that is too thin snaps and the stem is wasted.

Step 3: Lay the pleachers

Once the plashing cut is made, bend the stem gently in the direction of lay (traditionally upslope in Kent, or in the direction that gives the most stable angle against the prevailing wind). The target angle is 30 to 45 degrees from horizontal: low enough to promote vigorous upright growth but not so low that the stem lies flat and loses vigour.

Work along the hedge in sequence, laying each pleacher before moving to the next. Overlap adjacent pleachers by about 20cm so that the finished hedge is a continuous woven mat rather than a series of isolated laid stems. Where stems are close together, alternate the direction of the base cuts slightly so the tongues do not all point the same way and weaken the same face of the stem.

Step 4: Drive stakes and weave binders

Once all the pleachers are laid, drive stakes at 50 to 60cm intervals along the hedge line. Push them down firmly beside the laid stems rather than through them, using a mallet or post driver to avoid splitting the tops. The stakes run vertically and will hold the laid stems in position while the new growth establishes.

Weave the binders (long hazel or willow rods) between the tops of the stakes in a continuous run along the hedge. Start each binder at a stake, twist it once around the stake to lock it, then work it horizontally between adjacent stakes, pressing it down onto the laid stems below. Overlap binder rods by at least 30cm where they join. The finished top of the hedge should be a neat, level line of interlocked binders that prevents the stakes from moving.

Step 5: Trim and clear up

Trim any stubs of side-growth that stick up above the binder line. Rake and remove all brash from around the base of the hedge. If the ground has been compacted by working boots, lightly fork the soil in the root zone to restore drainage. Do not apply fertiliser at this stage; the hedge has enough stored energy to produce its first flush of growth without additional feeding. Apply a mulch of composted wood chips around the base to suppress weed competition and retain moisture through the following summer.

Aftercare: the first two years

In the first spring after laying, you will see a vigorous flush of new vertical shoots emerging along the length of every laid pleacher. Leave these completely alone for the first full growing season. The hedge needs all its energy directed into this new growth.

In the second year, lightly trim the new vertical growth to encourage branching and thickening. Do not trim hard; reduce by no more than one third. From the third year onward, the hedge can be managed by regular light trimming in the conventional way, and it will be significantly denser and more stockproof than before laying. In a good hawthorn hedge laid in November, the difference is visible by the following May.
