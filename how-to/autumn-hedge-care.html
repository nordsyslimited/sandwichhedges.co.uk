Autumn hedge care: final trim timing and what to do by hedge type

How you close out the season matters as much as the cut itself. Evergreens, deciduous hedges and conifers each need a different approach to timing, cut depth, mulching and frost protection -- compared here in one place.

Updated August 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Autumn is the last chance to leave your hedge in good shape before winter. Get the timing right and the hedge goes into its dormant period tidy and settled. Get it wrong -- trim too late, cut too hard, or skip the mulch -- and you can end up with frost-damaged growth, brown patches that take years to recover, or a hedge that starts the following spring in worse condition than it ended this one.

The complication is that the right approach varies quite a bit by hedge type. A rule that applies to beech does not apply to leylandii. What is fine for privet in October is risky for thuja. The comparison table below gives you a quick reference, followed by a section on each hedge group.

Nesting season ends 31 August -- but check before you cut in September

The Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 covers active bird nests: it is an offence to damage or destroy one. The main nesting season runs 1 March to 31 August, so September is the first genuinely safe month for hedge trimming. However, late-season nesters (pigeons, robins) can still be active in early September. Always check before cutting: look and listen for signs of occupation, and wait for any active nest to be abandoned before trimming that section.

PPE for autumn hedge trimming

  • Safety glasses or goggles for any powered cutting.
  • Gloves (leather or cut-resistant synthetic) for handling cut stems, especially hawthorn, holly, berberis and pyracantha.
  • Ear defenders with petrol or corded electric tools.
  • Stout footwear. Wet autumn leaves and cut clippings underfoot are a genuine slip hazard.

Formal evergreen broadleaf hedges: privet, laurel, box, photinia, holly, griselinia

These are the hedges that most East Kent gardens rely on for year-round screening: a privet running along a terrace in Sandwich, a laurel backing onto a rear garden in Deal, a box parterres around a front path in Worth or Ash. All of them are evergreen and broadly cold-tolerant, but they do have a vulnerability to late trimming.

A trim in late September or early October stimulates a small flush of new growth. That new growth is soft and frost-tender. East Kent's first hard frosts typically arrive in late October or November -- mild by inland standards (the coastal position of Sandwich and Deal keeps temperatures a degree or two higher than Ashford or Canterbury inland), but still enough to burn new soft shoots. By making the final trim no later than mid-October, you give the new growth enough time to harden slightly before frost arrives.

In autumn, trim lightly. This is not the time for a hard renovation cut. Take back this season's growth to maintain the hedge's existing shape; do not cut into older wood. The hedge will come back into growth in spring and can be assessed then for any harder work needed.

Box in autumn. Box blight is more of a problem in warm, humid autumns than in cold dry ones. After trimming, clear clippings from inside the base of the hedge and from the surrounding soil, as spores overwinter in dead leaf matter. Do not trim box in wet conditions if blight has been a problem. See our box hedge care guide for more on blight management.

Deciduous hedges: beech, hornbeam, hawthorn, rosa rugosa

Deciduous hedges are the most forgiving group in autumn, because they naturally go dormant as day length shortens. Once the leaves have fully turned and started to drop (typically October into November in East Kent), the hedge is not actively growing and is far less sensitive to the stress of cutting.

Beech and hornbeam, which are common in the gardens around Sandwich Bay and the larger properties in Eastry and Woodnesborough, hold their dead leaves through winter if trimmed after leaf turn -- a characteristic that provides privacy even when fully dormant. This makes late autumn (October or November) a particularly good time for the final formal trim on beech and hornbeam, as the dead leaves set hard against the cut surface and form the winter screen.

Hawthorn and rosa rugosa can be left until late autumn or even early winter for any renovation work. These are tough species and are not troubled by cold cutting. The one thing to watch with rosa rugosa in October is the hips: if you want to leave them for birds (and they are excellent wildlife food through winter), delay the final tidy until late autumn when the hips have been cleared naturally.

Hard renovation cuts on deciduous hedges are best left to January or February, when the hedge is fully dormant and the wound-healing response in spring is at its most efficient. Autumn is for maintaining the outline, not for major reshaping.

Conifer hedges: leylandii, thuja plicata, lawson cypress

Conifers require the most careful autumn timing. The fundamental rule -- never cut into brown wood, because conifers will not regenerate from old growth -- becomes even more consequential in autumn, because cold-damaged cut surfaces on a conifer produce persistent brown patches that the hedge cannot grow through.

Finish all conifer trimming by the end of September. A trim in October, when nights are already cold in East Kent, risks a week of cold wet weather before the cut surface can harden. The result is browning on the cut face that may persist for years and cannot be corrected short of waiting for the adjacent green growth to gradually cover it.

In autumn, trim only the current season's extension growth. A light trim maintains the shape and removes the wispy new growth without opening up the hedge surface to cold. If the conifer hedge needs heavier work -- reducing height or width -- do that in late summer (August), when more time remains for the cut surface to harden before winter.

For leylandii specifically, autumn is also the time to check whether it has been trimmed close to its legal obligation under the High Hedges Act 2003 if it borders a neighbour's property. See our leylandii rules guide for the detail on height limits and neighbour rights.

Yew: the conifer exception

Yew (taxus baccata) behaves differently from other conifers in a useful way: it will regenerate from old wood, including from cuts into bare brown stems. This makes yew the most forgiving of the conifer group and the one that can be cut with more confidence into October.

Even so, finish yew trimming by mid-October in East Kent to be safe. Yew is slow-growing and the cuts made in autumn are the ones that define the hedge's shape for the following two to three years. Take time to get the lines right. The final cut of the year on a formal yew hedge is worth doing carefully.

Mulching: worth doing on every hedge type

Once trimming is done for the season, applying a mulch along the hedge base is one of the highest-value jobs in the autumn garden. A 5 to 7cm layer of bark chip, well-rotted garden compost or leafmould laid along the full length of the hedge (keeping it away from the stems themselves) does three things: it insulates the root zone from the worst frosts, it retains soil moisture over the dry periods that often follow the first autumn rains, and it suppresses weeds that would otherwise compete with the hedge roots through winter.

Mulching is particularly beneficial in the light sandy soils around Sandwich Bay and Deal seafront, where moisture runs off faster than in the heavier loam soils inland. Hedges on these sandier soils tend to be under greater drought stress at the end of a dry summer, and a good autumn mulch sets them up better for the following spring. For more on feeding and supporting hedges through stress, see our feeding hedges guide.

Feeding: not in autumn

Do not feed any hedge in autumn. Feeding stimulates soft new growth that is exactly what you do not want heading into winter. Feed in early spring instead -- from late February for fast-growing species (privet, leylandii) and from March for slower evergreens and deciduous types. If your hedge has come through a difficult summer and looks visibly stressed, the mulch will do more good this side of winter than any fertiliser.

The RHS has produced a practical video on preparing the garden for winter that covers pruning timing and mulching technique for hedges and other garden plants. Useful watching if you are doing the whole garden in one autumn session: How to prepare your garden for Winter on the RHS YouTube channel.
