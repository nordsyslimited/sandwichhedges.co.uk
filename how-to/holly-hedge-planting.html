How to plant a holly hedge in Kent

Holly is one of the best-value hedging plants going: evergreen, wildlife-friendly, prickly enough to keep anything out, and perfectly suited to East Kent's chalky soils. Here's how to get it in the ground properly.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Holly (Ilex aquifolium) is a native British hedging plant that earns its place in almost any garden. It forms a dense, impenetrable barrier, produces berries that blackbirds and fieldfares rely on through winter, and once established it rarely needs more than one cut a year. It also tolerates the chalk soils that run under much of the Deal, Sandwich and Ash area, where some hedging plants struggle.

November is when bare-root holly becomes available from nurseries, which makes it the ideal month to plant. This guide walks through the full process, from choosing your stock to what to do in year one.

Bare-root or container-grown?

Bare-root holly is lifted from nursery fields while dormant, bundled and sold from November to March. It is cheaper than container stock, establishes well and is the preferred choice for planting a full run of hedging. The roots are more active after planting than those of a pot-bound plant, so bare-root stock often catches up with container plants within a season.

Container-grown holly can be planted year-round, but autumn and winter are still the best times. Summer planting is possible but needs daily watering for several weeks and is riskier on free-draining chalk.

Step 1: Choose your plants and check the condition

Order from a reputable UK hedging nursery. When the plants arrive, check that the roots are moist and the stems are pliant, not dried out or brittle. Bare-root stock should be planted within a day or two of arrival. If you can't plant immediately, heel the plants into a shaded corner with the roots covered in soil or damp compost.

For a dense garden boundary, buy plants in the 40–60cm height range. Smaller plants are cheaper and often establish more vigorously, but taller stock gives an earlier sense of privacy.

Step 2: Prepare the ground

Mark out the planting line with a string line or canes. Strip the turf along a 60cm-wide strip and remove any perennial weeds, particularly bindweed and couch grass, which will compete hard in year one. On compacted ground, fork over to 30cm depth. Holly does not need rich soil, but it does need reasonable drainage and enough depth to get roots down.

On East Kent chalk, the topsoil can be thin. Dig out the trench 30–40cm deep and work in some garden compost or well-rotted manure before planting. It makes a real difference in the first summer.

Step 3: Dig the trench or individual holes

For a row of three or more plants, a continuous trench is quicker and ensures even soil improvement along the whole line. Dig 30–40cm wide and 25–30cm deep. For single plants or small groups, individual holes work just as well: dig each hole to 1.5 times the root spread.

Step 4: Set your spacing

For a formal boundary hedge, plant 2 to 3 plants per metre. At 50cm spacing in a single row you get a decent screen in 3 to 4 years. A double staggered row at 35–40cm centres fills in faster, though it costs more up front. In Worth, Ash and the villages around Sandwich we see a lot of double-row holly planted in the 1980s and 90s, now providing complete year-round privacy.

Step 5: Plant at the right depth

The root collar (where the stem meets the roots) should sit at soil level, not buried. Fan the roots out into the bottom of the trench or hole. Backfill with the soil mixed with organic matter, firming it in with your boot as you go to remove air pockets. Holly prickles make this awkward: wear thick leather gloves throughout.

Step 6: Water in and mulch

Even in November, water in well after planting. Then apply a 10cm layer of bark mulch along the full planting strip, keeping it 5cm clear of the stems. The mulch holds moisture, suppresses weeds and moderates soil temperature. It matters most in the first summer, when dry spells on chalk can stress young plants badly.

Step 7: First-year aftercare

Inspect the plants a week after planting and re-firm any that have shifted in wind. In the following spring and summer, water during dry spells: once a week deeply is better than little and often. Keep the mulch topped up. In rural sites or gardens bordering fields, a temporary tree guard may be worth fitting to prevent rabbit damage to the base of stems.

Resist the urge to trim in year one. Let the plants put energy into the roots. Light shaping along the sides can begin in the second autumn, September or October, once the plants are well established. See annual holly hedge care for the full maintenance schedule.

Holly and the nesting season

Planting is fine year-round, but all trimming and shaping must respect the bird nesting season. Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to damage or destroy an active nest. Nesting season runs approximately 1 March to 31 August. The safe cutting window is September to February. This applies to holly of any age.
