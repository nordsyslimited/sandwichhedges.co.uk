How to take hedge cuttings and propagate hedging plants

Free plants from your existing hedge, or from a neighbour's, to fill gaps and extend boundaries. Hardwood cuttings in particular are about as beginner-friendly as propagation gets.

Updated September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

If your hedge has a gap, or you want to extend it without spending much, taking cuttings from an established plant is the most straightforward option. Most common hedging plants propagate easily from cuttings. You do not need a greenhouse. You do not need special equipment. You need clean secateurs, a pot of gritty compost and a bit of patience.

The RHS demonstrates the core technique clearly in their propagation guides on the RHS YouTube channel, covering the stages from cutting preparation through to potting on. The method below is the version we have found most reliable for the hedging species common in Kent gardens.

Nesting season and taking cuttings

Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs roughly from 1 March to 31 August. If you are taking cuttings during that period, check the hedge section carefully before cutting. Hardwood cuttings (taken November to February) sidestep this concern entirely and are the easier method for winter hedge work.

Before you start: what you will need

Equipment checklist

  • Sharp, clean secateurs (wipe the blades with disinfectant before you start)
  • Small pots or deep cell trays
  • Gritty, free-draining compost (equal parts peat-free compost and horticultural grit works well)
  • Hormone rooting powder or gel (optional but helpful on slower-rooting species)
  • A cold frame, unheated polytunnel, or a sheltered north-facing spot for hardwood cuttings
  • Labels and a waterproof pen

Hardwood cuttings: November to February

This is the method for most deciduous hedging: hornbeam, beech, hawthorn, hazel, blackthorn, privet, forsythia and field maple. It is the most forgiving method because the plant is dormant and the cuttings are not under water stress.

Step 1: select the right stems

Look for pencil-thick stems from the current year's growth (growth that appeared this spring and summer, now hardened off after summer). They should be straight, healthy and free of any signs of disease or pest damage. Cut them cleanly from the parent plant, ideally at a point where the stem meets older wood.

Step 2: prepare the cuttings

Cut each stem into sections 20 to 25 cm long (roughly the length of a standard pencil to your thumbnail). Make a clean, slanting cut just above a bud at the top, and a straight cut just below a bud at the bottom. The slanting top cut helps rain run off and serves as a reminder of which end is which. Remove any leaves that remain lower down the cutting.

Step 3: dip in rooting hormone (optional)

Dip the bottom 2 to 3 cm of each cutting into hormone rooting powder, tap off any excess and knock the base gently on the bench. For gel formulations, dip and withdraw in one smooth motion. Privet and forsythia will root without hormone powder; hornbeam, beech and hawthorn benefit from it.

Step 4: insert into gritty compost

Insert the cuttings into prepared pots of gritty compost, burying roughly two-thirds of their length. Space them about 5 cm apart. Firm the compost around each cutting so there are no air gaps around the stem base. Water in gently.

Step 5: place in a sheltered spot and leave alone

Hardwood cuttings do not need a heated propagator. Place them in a cold frame, an unheated greenhouse or a sheltered spot against a north-facing wall (where temperature fluctuations are smaller). Leave them there until spring. Rooting happens slowly over winter; do not disturb them or try to check for roots until the following March or April.

Step 6: pot on or plant out

By spring, most successful cuttings will show signs of new growth at the top. At this point, check carefully for roots (tip the pot gently to see if the compost holds together around a root ball). Those that have rooted can be potted on into larger individual pots of standard compost and grown on for a further season in a sheltered spot before planting in their final position. Those that have not rooted can be discarded or left a little longer; some species are simply slow.

Semi-ripe cuttings: July to September

This method works well for evergreen hedging: privet (particularly for summer propagation), box, cherry laurel, Portuguese laurel, lonicera nitida, griselinia and escallonia. The wood is not quite as soft as spring growth but has not yet fully hardened.

Step 1: choose stems from this year's growth

Look for stems that have just started to firm up at the base but are still flexible at the tip. They should be 8 to 12 cm long with healthy foliage. Cut just below a leaf node.

Step 2: prepare and wound the cutting

Remove the lower leaves, leaving two or three at the top. Some propagators lightly wound the bottom centimetre of the stem by scraping a thin sliver of bark from one side with the edge of the secateurs blade. This exposes more cambium and can improve rooting rates, particularly on laurel.

Step 3: hormone dip and insert

Dip in hormone powder or gel, then insert into gritty, free-draining compost with the lower leaves clear of the surface. Semi-ripe cuttings lose water faster than hardwood ones, so covering the pot with a clear polythene bag held away from the foliage (use a loop of wire to keep it off) helps maintain humidity during rooting.

Step 4: bottom heat (optional)

A heated propagator set to 18 to 20°C significantly speeds up rooting for semi-ripe evergreen cuttings. It is not essential, but if you have one, use it. An unheated greenhouse or cold frame will work; expect rooting to take longer, around eight to twelve weeks rather than four to six.

Step 5: harden off and pot on

Once you can see healthy new growth forming at the tip and roots showing through the drainage holes, remove the polythene bag and begin hardening the cutting off to outdoor conditions gradually over a week or two. Pot on into a larger container of standard compost and grow on through the following winter before planting in late spring.

Kent-specific notes by species

Privet (Ligustrum ovalifolium)

One of the easiest hedging plants to propagate. Takes well from hardwood cuttings in winter or semi-ripe cuttings in late summer. No heating required, no rooting hormone strictly necessary. A reliable introduction to propagation for anyone new to it.

Hornbeam (Carpinus betulus)

Hardwood cuttings in December or January, with rooting hormone. Takes a full growing season to root properly; do not rush it. Hornbeam is common on the heavier clay soils around Woodnesborough and Eastry, and it is worth the wait as a native hedging plant that browns less in winter than beech on wet ground.

Hawthorn (Crataegus monogyna)

Hardwood cuttings November to January. Success rate is variable but the cutting cost is zero, so take more than you need. Also possible from seed if you collect berries in autumn, clean them and cold-stratify over winter, but cuttings are faster.

Field maple (Acer campestre)

Hardwood cuttings in December. Slow to root but very reliable once established. Field maple is one of the native species most worth propagating, given how well it suits the chalk and clay soils of East Kent.

Beech (Fagus sylvatica)

Hardwood cuttings in winter. Use hormone powder. Success rate is lower than privet or hornbeam, so take three or four times as many cuttings as you need finished plants. Alternatively, seed-raised beech is easy: collect beechmast in October, sow in gritty compost over winter, and the seedlings appear in spring.

Box (Buxus sempervirens)

Semi-ripe cuttings in August with bottom heat. Box propagates readily but is currently under pressure from box tree moth and box blight across Kent. Before propagating, inspect the parent plant carefully for signs of either problem. Do not take cuttings from a plant showing blight symptoms (brown patches, white fungal strands inside stems) as you risk spreading it.

Video reference

The Royal Horticultural Society's propagation guides cover the core techniques used across most of the species above. The RHS YouTube channel (youtube.com/@TheRHS) has a step-by-step cutting demonstration that shows the preparation and potting stages clearly. The RHS has 78,000 subscribers and produces UK-specific horticultural advice from their gardens at Wisley, Harlow Carr, Hyde Hall and Rosemoor.
