Renovating an overgrown holly hedge in Kent

Holly is one of those hedges that looks indestructible and mostly is, but let it run for a decade and it needs a proper rethink, not just a haircut.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Holly is one of the most resilient hedge plants in a British garden. It tolerates hard cuts that would kill a leylandii outright, and it regrows from old wood with a willingness that few other evergreens match. Overgrown holly hedges are common in older East Kent gardens, particularly around Sandwich, Deal and Eastry, where hedges planted in the 1980s and 1990s have occasionally been left to their own devices for fifteen or twenty years. The good news is that renovation is entirely achievable, as long as you pick the right window and work methodically.

Nesting season: the one rule everything else works around

Under the UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs roughly from 1 March to 31 August. Holly is a popular nesting site for blackbirds, dunnocks and wrens, partly because the dense, prickly growth offers good protection. The safe renovation window is September to February. If you need to cut between March and August, check the hedge thoroughly for active nests first and do not cut if any are occupied.

Before you start: kit and safety

Holly demands tougher PPE than most hedges. The prickles on cut stems and clippings are sharp for months and will go through light gloves. Use:

  • Eye protection (mandatory): flying clippings from a hedge trimmer carry further than you expect; with holly they are hard and sharp
  • Heavy leather or reinforced gloves: not the light pair you use for weeding
  • Long sleeves and trousers: a holly stem flicking back against bare skin is unpleasant
  • Ladder safety: if the hedge is tall, use a pole trimmer rather than a ladder wherever possible; if a ladder is needed, always have someone footing it and never overreach sideways
  • RCD adaptor for electric tools
  • Check for wasp nests before starting, particularly in August and September when nests are at their largest

Step 1: Assess the hedge before anything else

Before reaching for a saw, walk the inside face of the hedge. You are looking for green growth on the interior stems: even small sprouts of new growth on old wood tell you the holly is alive and willing to regenerate from that point. This matters because a renovation cut only works if there is live growth below the point where you cut. Holly, unlike some other hedge plants, will produce new growth from old brown wood provided there is some green tissue nearby.

If the interior is entirely bare and woody with no green shoots at all, a full hard cut on both sides carries more risk of the hedge not recovering. In that case, plan a staged renovation over two years, one side at a time. For hedges with any green visible inside, you can cut both sides and the top back hard in a single season.

Also assess the width. A holly hedge that has spread from 60 cm to 150 cm is a renovation job you can tackle yourself. Much wider than that, and the amount of material to remove and clear may make it worth calling someone in.

Step 2: Choose your cutting window

September to February is the renovation window. Within that period, two timings stand out depending on your priorities.

If you want berries for winter wildlife or Christmas decoration, do not cut in September or October: holly berries ripen through autumn and represent a significant food source for fieldfares, redwings and blackbirds arriving in East Kent from Scandinavia. Leave the berries until January or early February, then renovate after they have largely been stripped.

If berries are not a priority, September and October are the most practical months. Dry weather makes the prickly clippings easier to handle and bag, the hedge has the whole winter to settle before spring growth, and the soil is still warm enough to support some root activity after cutting.

Step 3: Gather the right tools

For a typical overgrown holly hedge you will need a hedge trimmer for the main face and top, loppers for stems up to around 4 to 5 cm in diameter, and a folding pruning saw for anything thicker at the base. Bring a skip or a large supply of thick bin bags: holly clippings do not compost well and the prickles stay dangerous for months. Do not leave them loose on a lawn or path. Bag them securely or skip them.

Ear defenders if using a petrol trimmer. And the heavy gloves mentioned above, throughout.

Step 4: How hard can you cut?

The rule is simple: never cut below the lowest visible green growth. Within that limit, holly is remarkably tolerant. For a hedge with green growth throughout the interior, you can cut both faces back by 30 to 40 cm in a single session and reduce the top to the height you want. The holly will recover over two to three growing seasons.

For a hedge where the interior is bare below a certain height, cut one face hard in the first winter and leave the other face and the top untouched. The untouched side keeps the hedge alive and photosynthesising while the cut face regenerates. Come back the following winter and cut the second face, then reduce the top if needed.

Avoid cutting during frosty weather. A fresh cut on holly stems in a hard frost can cause dieback beyond the cut point.

Step 5: What to expect after renovation

Holly is slow to show visible recovery. After a hard renovation cut, it can look disconcertingly bare for four to eight weeks before new buds push out from old wood. This is entirely normal and not a sign of failure. Resist the temptation to cut again or to assume the worst. By mid-spring following an autumn or winter renovation cut, you should see clear new growth beginning to push. The first full growing season after renovation the hedge will look sparse. By year two it fills noticeably; a full, dense finish takes three growing seasons.

Step 6: Post-renovation care

In the spring following renovation, apply a balanced general fertiliser along the base of the hedge and water it in well. A mulch of composted bark, 7 to 10 cm deep and kept clear of the stems, helps retain moisture and suppress weeds competing with the recovering root system. Holly is relatively drought-tolerant when established but a recently renovated hedge is under more stress than usual and benefits from watering in dry spells through the first summer after cutting. No other special care is needed. Keep the hedge on its normal annual cut schedule from year two onwards.
