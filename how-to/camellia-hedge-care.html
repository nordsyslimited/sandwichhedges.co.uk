Camellia hedge care in Kent

When to trim, which tool to use, and how to avoid accidentally cutting off next year's flowers. Camellia hedges are one of those East Kent treats that reward patience and a bit of good timing.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Camellias thrive in East Kent in a way they really cannot in most of northern England. The mild maritime winters around Sandwich, Deal, Worth and the coastal villages barely dip below freezing, which is precisely what camellias need: a winter cold enough to trigger dormancy but not hard enough to damage the flower buds that form through late summer and autumn.

Used as a hedge or informal screen, camellia gives you something few other evergreens offer: a proper flower display, rich glossy foliage year round, and enough structure to form a useful boundary. The catch is the timing. Trim at the wrong point in the year and you lose the flowers before they open.

Nesting season and camellia trimming: the overlap problem

The UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to damage an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs roughly 1 March to 31 August. For Camellia japonica, the ideal pruning window, immediately after flowering in late April or May, falls squarely inside nesting season. Always check the plant carefully for active nests before putting any tool near it. If there are birds in residence, leave it until September. The safe cutting window for camellia sasanqua, which flowers in autumn and winter, is April, which is also in nesting season. The same check applies.

Before you start: safety

Before-you-start checklist

  • Eye protection: camellia wood is dense and hard; secateurs under tension can spring material toward your face. Wear safety glasses.
  • Gloves: sturdy gardening gloves, especially for loppers on older stems.
  • If using an electric trimmer: use an RCD adaptor, keep the cable behind you, and unplug before clearing cut material from the blades.
  • Ladders: for tall camellias, a pole pruner keeps you on the ground. If a ladder is needed, have someone foot it, and never overreach sideways.
  • Check for wasp nests: camellias create dense, sheltered growth, particularly towards the base. Check before starting.

Japonica vs sasanqua: the timing difference

The two types most commonly used for hedging in Kent have quite different care calendars, and the most common mistake is applying the same timing to both.

Camellia japonica

This is the one most people picture when they think of a camellia: large flowers in shades of red, pink and white, opening from late winter through spring. In Sandwich and Deal, japonica typically flowers from late February through to early May, depending on variety and aspect. The correct timing for trimming is immediately after the last flowers fade, usually late April to early May. Trim too late and you risk cutting off the buds forming for next year, which appear on the new growth that follows flowering.

Camellia sasanqua

Sasanqua is smaller-flowered, usually autumn-blooming (October to December in Kent's climate), and faster-growing. Because it flowers on the current year's growth, the pruning window is the opposite: April, before growth begins, or immediately after any winter damage is visible. Do not trim sasanqua in autumn during its flowering period.

How to trim: secateurs vs hedge trimmer

You can use a hedge trimmer on an established camellia hedge, but with limits. Camellia leaves are large and leathery. A trimmer blade moving fast through them leaves brown, shredded edges that stay visible until the next flush of growth covers them. On a formal, frequently-trimmed hedge kept to a consistent size this is manageable because the new growth comes through quickly. On an informal screen where branches have grown out unevenly, secateurs or loppers give a cleaner, less ragged result.

The rule for either tool: only cut into growth that still carries leaves. Camellia does not regenerate readily from old, leafless wood. Unlike privet or hawthorn, which throw new shoots from ancient stems, camellia pruned back into bare grey wood often simply dies in that section. Keep your cuts within the green, leafy canopy.

Renovation: what to do if a camellia hedge has become overgrown

If an older camellia hedge has become very large or the base has gone bare, full renovation is risky. A phased approach over two to three years is safer: trim one side hard in year one, the other side in year two, taking each side back progressively rather than all at once. Feed after each cut with an ericaceous fertiliser and mulch the base to retain moisture.

If the base is genuinely bare and leggy, check whether the upper canopy has closed over and is shading the base out. Thinning the top can let light back in and encourage new basal growth, which is usually more effective than hard cutting alone.

Feeding and soil: the Kent factor

Camellias are acid-lovers: they want a pH below 6.5 and preferably closer to 5.5 to 6.0. Much of East Kent sits on chalk or heavy clay, both of which are alkaline. In Sandwich, Eastry and the villages inland, this can cause yellowing leaves (chlorosis) even on established camellias that are otherwise thriving. Apply a sequestered-iron feed in spring, use ericaceous compost around the base as a mulch, and avoid applying lime near the plant.

The sandier, slightly more acid soils near the coast, particularly in parts of Sandwich Bay and the lower ground around Deal, tend to suit camellias better without much amendment. On chalk gardens, a raised or mounded bed with ericaceous compost mixed in gives camellias the best start.

Frost protection: usually not needed in this corner of Kent

The camellia plant itself is hardy to around -15°C, well below anything East Kent typically sees. The risk is not the winter cold: it is late frosts hitting open flower buds in February or March, and cold easterly winds from the North Sea that can scorch newly-opened flowers and tender new growth in spring. Planting camellias on a sheltered west or south-facing aspect, with a wall or fence behind them, makes a meaningful difference to the flower display in Sandwich, Deal, Worth and the exposed coastal parts of Sandwich Bay.

Avoid planting where morning sun will hit frost-covered flowers: the rapid thaw damages the petals. A spot that stays in shade until mid-morning is fine.
