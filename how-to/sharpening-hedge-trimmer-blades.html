How to sharpen hedge trimmer blades

A dull hedge trimmer tears rather than cuts, leaves ragged edges and makes the motor work harder than it needs to. Sharpening the blades takes about twenty minutes and is straightforward if you follow the right steps.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Hedge trimmer blades are not designed to stay sharp forever. Resinous species like privet, leylandii and laurel accelerate wear; grit and debris in the hedge speeds it further. A blade that was cutting cleanly in August may be noticeably dull by the end of October. Knowing how to sharpen it yourself, rather than taking it in for service each time, is a useful skill.

This guide covers powered hedge trimmers (petrol, cordless and corded electric). For hand shears, see our separate hand shears sharpening guide. For end-of-season cleaning, oiling and storage, see hedge trimmer maintenance.

Before you handle the blades: safety first

Hedge trimmer blades are sharp, even when dull enough to need sharpening. Before starting:

  • Cordless trimmers: remove the battery completely.
  • Petrol trimmers: disconnect the spark plug lead.
  • Corded electric trimmers: unplug from the mains.

Wear cut-resistant gloves throughout. Thick leather gloves are good; dedicated cut-resistant gardening gloves rated EN 388 are better. Safety glasses protect against metal filings when using a file. Never rest the blade bar against your body when working.

Step 1: recognise when sharpening is needed

A hedge trimmer that needs sharpening shows several signs before the blades fail completely:

  • The cut material has ragged, torn edges rather than clean cuts. Privet and box are particularly easy to read: a sharp trimmer leaves a clean cut face; a dull one tears and shreds the tips.
  • The machine is working noticeably harder than usual, the motor sounds strained or the tool vibrates more than normal.
  • You can see flat, reflective spots along the top edge of the blade teeth when you look down the blade in bright light.
  • The hedge needs two or three passes to achieve what one pass used to.

At the start of each cutting season, and after any session cutting particularly resinous species, run a fingernail lightly across the bevel of two or three teeth. If the nail slides over a flat rather than catching slightly on a sharp edge, the blades are ready for a sharpen.

Step 2: gather your tools

  • A flat diamond file (medium or fine grit) or a small flat whetstone with honing oil. Diamond files cut harder steel more efficiently and are the better choice for modern hedge trimmer blades.
  • A flat file guide (optional but helpful for maintaining a consistent angle).
  • A soft-jaw vice or padded clamp to hold the blade bar steady. A Workmate-style bench with foam pads works well.
  • Cut-resistant gloves and safety glasses (as above).
  • Blade oil or light machine oil for after.
  • A clean cloth for wiping metal filings between teeth.

Step 3: secure the trimmer

Clamp the blade bar horizontally in the vice, with the teeth facing away from you. The body of the tool should rest on a stable surface or be supported so it does not rotate. You want the blade bar firmly held so both your hands are free, one for the file and one (in gloves) for steadying, and you are not fighting the tool to keep it still.

If you do not have a vice, lay the trimmer flat on a sturdy workbench with the blade bar overhanging the edge slightly, and weight or clamp the body. It is not ideal but it works for a single-person job.

Step 4: identify the bevel angle

Most hedge trimmer blades are bevelled at 30 to 45 degrees. Look closely at an undamaged tooth near the base of the blade bar: you will see the original bevel clearly as a shiny flat ground into the upper face of the tooth. That is the angle you need to match with the file. Do not try to change the angle; matching the existing bevel is what keeps the blade cutting geometry correct.

If you are unsure, hold a small protractor or angle gauge against the flat bevel of an undamaged tooth before you start, and note the angle. A consistent approach across all the teeth matters more than getting the exact degrees precisely right.

Step 5: file each tooth

Work along the blade from the base toward the tip, doing one tooth at a time:

  1. Position the file flat against the existing bevel of the tooth, at the matching angle.
  2. Apply light, consistent pressure and draw the file from the base of the tooth toward the tip in one smooth stroke. Lift the file clear on the return stroke; do not drag it back.
  3. Use three to five strokes per tooth for a moderately dull blade. More than ten strokes per tooth suggests the blade may be too far gone to sharpen effectively.
  4. Wipe the tooth with a clean cloth to remove metal filings before moving to the next tooth.
  5. Work all the teeth on the upper (visible) blade, then rotate the trimmer to access the lower blade and repeat.

Count your strokes and try to keep the same number per tooth: consistency gives an even cutting edge across the whole blade length. The last few teeth at the tip of the bar are the most critical for cutting performance, so do not rush them.

STIHL GB's guidance on blade maintenance (available on their YouTube channel @STIHLGreatBritain) covers the filing technique for their blade ranges and discusses the blade gap, which is also relevant to the next step.

Step 6: check the blade gap

Hedge trimmer blades work by the reciprocating action of two blades passing each other at a very small gap. After sharpening, hold the blade bar up to the light and look along it: the upper and lower blade teeth should just clear each other without binding. The traditional check is that a business card (roughly 0.3 mm thick) should just slide between adjacent teeth without excessive resistance.

If the gap has tightened during sharpening (unlikely from light filing), loosen the blade bar bolts by a quarter turn and recheck. If the gap is too wide (which can happen if the blade bar has been dropped or hit an object), this is a job for a service workshop, not a file.

Step 7: oil the blades

Before reassembly or storage, apply a light coating of blade oil or a purpose-made resin solvent spray along the full length of both blade surfaces. This lubricates the reciprocating action, reduces further corrosion and makes cleaning easier after the next use. Run the trimmer briefly at low speed (on the ground, pointing away from you, after reattaching the battery or reconnecting the spark plug) to work the oil between the blades.

Sharpen or replace: how to decide

Sharpening is appropriate for blades that are uniformly dull but structurally intact. Replace the blade set when you see:

  • Chipped or missing teeth: a file cannot restore metal that is gone. Chipped teeth also create irregular gaps that affect cutting quality across the whole blade.
  • Bent teeth: bent teeth change the blade gap and put extra load on the motor. A bent tooth cannot be safely filed straight.
  • A kinked or bowed blade bar: if the blade bar itself is not straight, the blades will not cut evenly whatever you do to the teeth.
  • A blade that needed many previous sharpenings: each sharpening removes a little metal. At some point the tooth geometry becomes too shallow to hold a useful edge. If the teeth look visibly shorter than on a new blade, it is time to replace.

Replacement blade sets for most branded trimmers are available from manufacturers and from garden machinery dealers. Fitting them is typically a straightforward task with a screwdriver and a 10 mm spanner.
