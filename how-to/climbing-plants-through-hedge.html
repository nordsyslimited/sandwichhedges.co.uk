Growing climbing plants through a garden hedge

Honeysuckle, wild rose and clematis all grow naturally through hedgerows in East Kent. Here is how to plant and manage them so they add colour, scent and wildlife value without getting out of hand.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Look at any old hedgerow between Sandwich and Woodnesborough in June and you will see honeysuckle threading through the hawthorn, dog rose arching out of the top, and in some sections old man's beard (Clematis vitalba) running across the whole lot like a net. That is not an accident: the deep-rooted structure of a hedge provides exactly the growing conditions these plants evolved for.

You can replicate this in a garden hedge deliberately. The result is a hedge with more scent, more flower from June through to August, and considerably more wildlife value. The catch is that some climbers stay politely in their lane and others will quietly demolish the hedge if you do not manage them. Getting the species choice right from the start saves a great deal of work.

Nesting season and training work

Training and tying in new growth, removing long runners and cutting climbers back to the hedge profile are all best done in the dormant season. The Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 protects active bird nests from 1 March to 31 August: handling and pulling at climber growth during this window risks disturbing nests hidden in the hedge and the climber. Do any significant management of climbers through a hedge in October to February.

Before you start: tools and safety

Planting at a hedge base and training climbers through it involves working close to thorny hawthorn, blackthorn and rose growth. Wear heavy leather gauntlets rather than thin gloves. Use long-handled loppers rather than reaching deep into a thorny hedge with secateurs. Eye protection is recommended: thorns at face height are a genuine hazard when leaning into a hedge. Wash hands after handling clematis (can cause skin irritation in some people).

Step 1: choose the right climber for your hedge type

Not all climbers suit all hedges. A formal clipped privet or beech hedge in a small suburban garden in Deal does not suit a vigorous clematis; the trimming process will constantly cut it back to the hedge profile and the plant will never look intentional. An informal or mixed native hedge in a larger Sandwich or Eastry garden is a different matter: climbers look natural and controlled management is simpler.

Honeysuckle (Lonicera periclymenum): the best all-round choice for most Kent hedges. Native, manageable, fragrant from June to August, berries from late summer. It twines clockwise around existing stems rather than gripping or pushing, so it does not physically damage the hedge. In a formal hedge, it will show at the base and flower at the top; in an informal hedge, it threads through naturally. Suits hawthorn, blackthorn, hazel and beech hedges well.

Dog rose (Rosa canina): native to Kent hedgerows, already present in many older garden boundaries. If you are planting fresh, choose Rosa canina or Rosa arvensis (field rose). The arching canes grow 2-3 metres a year and need cutting back in autumn. The hips produced from September are one of the best wildlife contributions any hedge plant makes: fieldfares and redwings arriving on the Kent coast each October go straight for rose hips. Not suitable in a very narrow formal hedge where the thorny arching canes become a maintenance problem at eye level.

Clematis montana: blossom in April and May, usually white or pale pink. Less wildlife value than native species but less vigorous than Clematis vitalba, so easier to manage in a garden setting. Suits hawthorn and hornbeam hedges. Can be cut back hard in June after flowering without damaging the plant. Not native to Kent but not aggressive once established.

Clematis vitalba (old man's beard): entirely native to Kent's chalk and clay soils, growing wild along banks near Woodnesborough and Eastry. Highly vigorous: it can cover 5-6 metres of hedge top in a single season if left unchecked. Suited to larger rural hedges and farm boundaries, not a well-managed suburban garden hedge of 15 metres. Cut hard every 2 years. The feathery seed heads in autumn and winter are distinctive and attractive, but this plant earns its name by taking over.

Step 2: plant at the hedge base in autumn

Autumn is the ideal planting time. November is particularly good in East Kent: the soil is still warm from summer, rainfall keeps roots moist, and the hedge is dormant so competition for light and water is reduced.

Plant at the outer base of the hedge rather than attempting to plant in the interior. Dig a planting hole 30-40cm from the hedge base line, enriched with compost. Set the plant at the same depth as it was in the pot or bare-root packaging. Water in. You do not need to push a cane into the hedge immediately: let the plant establish its root system in the first season before expecting much aerial growth.

Space planting points at least 2-3 metres apart along the hedge run. One honeysuckle every 5-6 metres is ample for a 20-metre hedge; more than that and the whole hedge starts to flower at the same time, which is satisfying but leaves no variety through the season.

Step 3: guide the first season's growth

In the first spring and summer after planting, the climber will put on new growth but may not find the hedge on its own. Use a short bamboo cane angled from the planting hole toward the hedge to direct the first main stem into the lower branches. For honeysuckle, once the tip has reached the hedge, it will find its own way upward by twining. Dog rose needs its longest canes loosely tied into the hedge with soft garden twine; it does not twine and will fall away from the hedge without support in the first year.

In the first season, do not cut the climber. Let it establish. Any trimming of the hedge in the first year should avoid the immediate area around the planting point to avoid accidentally damaging the new plant's roots or stem.

Step 4: ongoing management

Once established (after the second growing season), manage the climber at the same time as the annual hedge trim: cut back any growth that is projecting well beyond the hedge face, remove very long runs that have reached the top of the hedge and are hanging over, and tie in any canes that are pulling away from the hedge structure.

For honeysuckle: a light cut back to the hedge profile after flowering in August keeps it tidy without removing next year's flower buds, which form on the previous year's wood. In the dormant season, remove any very old, thick woody stems from the hedge interior if they are getting tangled.

For dog rose: cut the longest arching canes back in October-November after the hips have been taken by birds. Do not cut flush with the hedge profile; leave 30cm of stem as a framework for next year's growth.

For clematis: cut back in November after the seed heads have dispersed. Old man's beard can be cut to 30cm from the ground every 2-3 years to keep it in control.

Plants to avoid in a garden hedge

Russian vine (Fallopia baldschuanica, also called mile-a-minute): extremely vigorous. Once established in a hedge, it will swamp 10-15 metres of hedge in a single season and is very difficult to remove without taking large sections of hedge with it. Do not plant this anywhere near a hedge.

Wisteria: woody stems that thicken over years. In a garden hedge, wisteria stems physically constrict and split hedge branches over time as both plants grow. Lovely on a wall; not suitable for a hedge.

Ivy (Hedera helix): a more nuanced case. Native ivy in small amounts provides berries and overwintering habitat for insects, and the autumn flowers feed bees late in the season when little else is available. However, well-established ivy in a hedge is very difficult to remove later, can shade the interior to the point of die-back, and adds significant weight that can pull sections of an older hedge apart. In a rough native hedgerow on a rural boundary, some ivy is fine. In a domestic garden hedge, it is difficult to manage once it gets going.
