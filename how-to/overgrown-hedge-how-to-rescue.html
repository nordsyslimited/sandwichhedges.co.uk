How to rescue an overgrown hedge

When the correction needed is more than just a top level: renovation cuts, phased reduction and what to expect from a hedge brought back from too big.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

An overgrown hedge is not a failed hedge. Most hedges that have got out of hand can be brought back to a manageable size without losing them. The process is slower than people expect, and that is the point: a hedge that has been building bulk for five or ten years cannot and should not be corrected in a single season.

This guide covers the phased reduction approach: how it works, which species tolerate it, and what to expect at each stage. It is distinct from renovation of a hedge that has been neglected and is structurally compromised (thinning from the base, dead sections, bare stems). That situation is covered in our guide to renovating a neglected hedge.

The two most common situations we see in Sandwich, Deal and the villages of East Kent: a privet that has been allowed to grow to 3 metres and now takes half the light from the garden, and a leylandii screen planted in the 1990s that is now 8 metres tall and 2 metres wide. Both are recoverable, by different methods.

Before you start: safety first

Working with tall, dense hedges involves risks that routine trimming does not. If the hedge is over 2 metres, work from a stable platform (not a ladder leant against the hedge). Heavy branches from hard cuts can drop unpredictably. Wear eye protection and gloves. If the hedge runs alongside a public pavement or road, consider whether falling material could reach it. For hedges that require work at height, consider whether professional help is the right choice before starting.

Step 1: assess what you have

Before cutting anything, work out what you are dealing with. Walk around the hedge and assess: How tall is it compared to where you want it? How wide is it compared to where you want it? Is the base still filled with growth, or is it bare stems from the ground up? Are there dead sections, or is the entire hedge alive but oversized?

If the base is bare for more than the bottom third of the hedge's height, the hedge has become drawn up and leggy, not simply oversized. That is a different problem: cutting it down to size will not restore a dense base. See our renovating a neglected hedge guide for that situation.

Step 2: know your species and its limits

Not all hedging plants respond the same way to hard cutting. The key distinction is whether the species will regrow from old, brown wood.

Species that tolerate hard cutting and will regenerate from old wood: privet, laurel, hawthorn, blackthorn, beech, hornbeam, holly, yew (slowly). These can be cut back hard in a single phase if necessary, though phased cutting over two years is gentler on the plant.

Species that do not regenerate from old, brown wood: leylandii and other conifers. Once you cut into brown wood on a leylandii, that section stays brown permanently. These require a different approach: reduce by no more than one third of the green growth in any one year, working over three or more years.

If you are unsure which applies to your hedge, see our guide to how hard you can cut back a hedge.

Nesting season: plan your timing carefully

It is an offence under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. The nesting season runs from 1 March to 31 August. Overgrown hedges are often used extensively as nesting sites precisely because they offer so much shelter. Plan renovation cuts for September to February where possible. If the work is urgent during the nesting season, the hedge must be checked carefully for active nests before any cutting begins.

Step 3: the phased reduction plan

The standard approach for most hedges (privet, laurel, hawthorn, beech) is a two-year programme. For conifers, allow three years.

Deciduous and evergreen broadleaf hedges: the two-year plan

Year 1 (September to February): Cut the top to your target height. Leave the sides at their current width. This is the more stressful cut: removing height exposes the hedge's interior to light and starts the regrowth response.

During the following growing season, the hedge puts energy into thickening up the top and upper sides. Leave it alone during this period. Resist the urge to tidy.

Year 2 (September to February, the following year): Now cut the sides back to your target width. The hedge has had a full growing season to recover from the top cut, and the regrowth has thickened the upper portion. The side cut now exposes a hedge with active regrowth already established at the top.

After Year 2, maintain the hedge at its new, reduced dimensions with a standard annual or twice-annual cut.

Conifers: the three-year plan

Never cut into brown wood on leylandii or most conifers. Plan for a three-year programme, cutting no more than a third of the green growth each year. Depending on how much reduction is needed, you may be taking a notional layer of green off each side and the top each year.

Crucially: once the target dimensions are reached, maintain them strictly. Leylandii allowed to grow beyond the last cut point will continue outward indefinitely; cutting back to previous cuts stays in the green zone. For detail on leylandii specifically, see our leylandii hedge guide.

Step 4: the cuts themselves

Use sharp, clean tools. Blunt blades tear and bruise, increasing the risk of disease entry. For large-scale reductions, a petrol hedge trimmer saves time, but for the initial hard cuts on thick stems, using a pruning saw or loppers on the thicker framework first (then the hedge trimmer over the resulting surface) works better.

For the top cut: establish your target height with canes or a taut string line before you start. A crooked top is hard to live with and harder to fix. Work from one end, checking back frequently.

For the side cuts: a taut string line from one end of the hedge to the other, set at your target width, gives a reference plane. Work with the string as a guide rather than a target: you want the face of the hedge just inside the string.

Step 5: aftercare

After a hard reduction cut, water during dry spells in the first growing season. Hedges stressed by large cuts in an already-dry summer can struggle to push the regrowth they need. A mulch of bark or wood chip along the base, 5 to 8cm deep, reduces moisture loss and suppresses competing weeds.

Do not feed with high-nitrogen fertiliser immediately after cutting: this pushes soft, sappy growth that is more susceptible to disease. A balanced slow-release fertiliser in spring of the following year is more useful.

When to call in help

Some situations are best handled by a contractor. A hedge over 3 metres tall that needs significant reduction involves working at height with large power tools, which carries real risk. If the hedge runs against a fence or boundary structure that could be damaged, professional assessment is worthwhile.
