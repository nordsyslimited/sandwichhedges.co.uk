Common mistakes when establishing a new hedge: what to avoid in the first two years

Most new hedges that struggle in East Kent do not fail because the species was wrong. They fail because of what happens in the first two growing seasons. Seven avoidable mistakes account for the vast majority of the problems we see.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Bare-root planting season opens in November and there is a predictable rush: people plant, feel they have done the hard part, and then watch some plants struggle or die in their first summer. Chartered landscape architect Bunny Guinness, who has spent over 25 years on BBC Radio 4's Gardeners' Question Time and 23 years as a Telegraph garden columnist, covers the key principles in: Top Tips to establish a Happy Healthy Hedge (Bunny Guinness, YouTube).

The seven mistakes below are the ones we see most often when we are called in to advise on or rescue a struggling new hedge in the villages of East Kent.

The seven mistakes at a glance

  1. Planting in waterlogged or frozen ground
  2. Not watering enough in the first summer
  3. Skipping the weed-suppressing mulch
  4. Not doing formative pruning in year one
  5. Planting too shallow or too deep
  6. No staking on exposed sites
  7. Wrong species for the soil

1. Planting in waterlogged or frozen ground

The bare-root window is November to March, but that does not mean all ground conditions within that period are suitable. Planting into saturated, waterlogged soil pushes roots into an anaerobic environment before they have established any new growth, and root rot can set in before the plant has a chance to push through spring. Frozen ground causes similar problems: the roots cannot make contact with soil particles, and thaw-refreeze cycles can lift and disturb newly planted stock.

The ideal planting window is November to early December: ground temperatures are still relatively warm from summer, the soil is firm enough to work, and plants have time to push initial roots before full dormancy. If November passes and the ground is genuinely waterlogged, wait for a dry spell in January or February rather than forcing it.

In the heavier clay soils around Worth, Ash and Woodnesborough, standing water after heavy autumn rain is common. Improve drainage at planting by digging a trench rather than individual holes and working in grit and well-rotted organic matter. Planting on a slight raised ridge, even just 5-10cm above the surrounding level, also helps on very wet ground.

2. Not watering enough in the first summer

This is the biggest cause of new hedge failure in East Kent, and it is entirely preventable. East Kent is one of the drier parts of the UK, receiving around 550–600mm of rainfall annually. The rain falls mostly in autumn and winter. By July and August, particularly on the sandy soils at Sandwich Bay and around Deal, the ground can be very dry indeed and a new hedge with limited root development has no reserves to draw on.

New plants need watering every two to three days during dry spells in their first summer. Water thoroughly: a slow soak to 20–30cm depth is far more effective than a daily splash. A leaky-pipe irrigation laid along the hedge line at planting removes the daily decision from the equation and means the hedge gets watered consistently even during a busy week. This is genuinely worth doing on a long hedge.

Continue monitoring in the second summer too, though by year two most species have developed enough root depth to cope with all but the driest conditions. Leylandii and other conifers on sandy soils around Sandwich Bay sometimes need watering into their second and even third summer on very freely draining ground.

3. Skipping the weed-suppressing mulch

Weeds compete directly with new hedge plants for water and nutrients in the critical establishment period. A 75–100mm layer of bark chip mulch in a 30–40cm wide strip along the hedge line suppresses weeds, retains moisture and gradually improves the soil as it breaks down. This is not an optional finishing touch; it is a practical intervention that directly improves survival rates.

Apply the mulch immediately after planting and keep it topped up through the first two years. Leave a small gap around the stems of each plant to avoid collar rot. On chalky soils, mixing some well-rotted compost under the mulch at planting helps water retention in the first critical summer.

The alternative, regular hand weeding, is time-consuming enough that it often does not happen. Mulch does the job without further effort once it is down.

4. Not doing formative pruning in year one

Most people plant a new hedge and then leave it alone for several years, waiting for it to reach height before cutting. This produces a line of tall, single-stemmed plants with sparse growth at the base and a gap at ground level that never really fills in.

Formative pruning in the first winter (December to February) addresses this. For most deciduous species, cutting the plants back by one-third to one-half of their height at the end of the first growing season encourages branching from low down, which is what produces a dense, gapless base. This seems counterintuitive, and people resist it, but the result over three years is a better hedge than one left to grow tall without intervention.

When formative pruning overlaps with nesting season

Formative pruning in year one is best done in December or January, comfortably outside the nesting season (1 March to 31 August under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981). If you miss this window and need to prune in spring, check for active nests first. A new hedge is less likely to have nesting birds in its first year, but it is worth checking regardless. The safe cutting window is September through to the end of February.

The one exception is leylandii and most other conifers. These are left unpruned for the first two years to establish height, with only the very tips of side shoots trimmed lightly to encourage bushing. Do not cut back the leading shoot of leylandii until it has reached its intended height.

5. Planting too shallow or too deep

Bare-root plants should be planted at the same depth they were growing in the field, which is visible as a soil mark on the stem. Plant them too shallow and the roots dry out at the surface and the plant rocks in wind; too deep and the stem can rot at the collar and the plant struggles to push new growth.

On sloping ground, dig across the slope rather than following it, so each plant sits level. On heavy clay in Worth or Ash, plant slightly high on a small mound rather than into the bottom of a hollow, where water will collect against the stem.

6. No staking on exposed sites

In gardens with exposure to south-westerly or north-easterly winds, particularly along the coast at Sandwich Bay or Deal seafront, new hedge plants rock in the wind during establishment. This breaks the fine root hairs that are just developing, set back establishment significantly, and can kill plants outright in a severe storm.

A simple windbreak of 50% shade netting on a post and wire frame, erected on the windward side for the first two winters, can halve establishment time on an exposed site. Individual spiral guards on bare-root plants protect against rabbit and hare damage, which is a real problem on the field margins around Worth, Ash and Eastry.

7. Wrong species for the soil

East Kent has significant soil variation within short distances. The chalk that runs inland from the coast near Sandwich suits beech, privet, hawthorn and most hedging species. The heavier clay soils around Worth, Ash, Woodnesborough and parts of Eastry suit hornbeam, hawthorn and field maple, but are less suitable for beech, which struggles in waterlogged clay. The sandy soils at Sandwich Bay drain very freely and suit any species that can handle summer drought, but need extra watering support in year one.

If you are not sure of your soil type, dig a hole 30cm deep after heavy rain and come back an hour later. Standing water that does not drain quickly means poor drainage and clay-tolerant species are the better choice. Soil that dries out visibly within an hour means free drainage and the more drought-tolerant options will perform better.
