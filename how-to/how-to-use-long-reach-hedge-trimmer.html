How to use a long-reach hedge trimmer safely

The right tool makes tall hedges much safer than a ladder. Here is how to set up, cut and clear up with a pole hedge trimmer, from a team that uses them across Sandwich, Deal and the East Kent villages.

Updated August 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Most garden accidents involving hedge trimmers happen on ladders. The combination of a fast-moving blade, an unstable platform and the temptation to overreach sideways is a bad one. A long-reach (or pole) hedge trimmer removes most of that risk: you stay on the ground, and the adjustable head reaches up to the hedge top for you.

The tool does ask something of you in return. The blade is now above your head, the weight distribution is different, and cable management (on corded models) needs a bit of thought. This guide covers everything in order: before you start, setting up, cutting technique, and clearing up afterwards.

Before you start: PPE and safety essentials

  • Eye protection is essential. Clippings from above come at you from angles a standard trimmer never creates. Wrap-around safety glasses or a visor are both fine.
  • Ear defenders. Petrol pole trimmers are loud. High-voltage cordless models are quieter but still worth protecting against over a long job.
  • Sturdy gloves. The pole vibrates, and dry clippings from tall hedges are sharper than they look.
  • Long sleeves and trousers. Flying debris from hedge tops comes from a wider arc than a standard trimmer produces at shoulder height.
  • Cable management (corded electric models only). Drape the cable over your shoulder so it runs behind you and away from the blade. Always plug into an RCD (residual current device) adaptor, not directly into the mains socket.
  • Check for wasp nests and brambles. Look into the hedge from underneath before you start. Wasp nests are common in tall, dense hedges from July onwards, and a nest disturbed by a running blade at head height is genuinely dangerous.

Nesting season: know the law before you cut

The UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs roughly from 1 March to 31 August. The safe cutting window for most hedges is September to February. A light trim during the nesting period is only acceptable if the hedge is checked first and found to be demonstrably empty.

Step 1: choose the right reach for the job

Most domestic long-reach trimmers extend to between 1.5 and 2.5 metres total reach from the ground. Add your own arm height when fully extended and you can comfortably clear the top of a hedge up to about 2.8 to 3 metres without straining. If your hedge is taller than that, either look for a professional-grade model with additional extension pieces, or contact us.

The adjustable cutting head angle is equally important. A head that can lock at multiple positions lets you switch between cutting the face (vertical angle) and the top (nearly horizontal) without stopping to reset the whole pole. Check this before you buy or hire.

Step 2: inspect the hedge before you switch on

Walk along the hedge and look at it from underneath as well as from the side. You are looking for: birds or signs of an active nest (feathers, fresh droppings under the hedge, a bird flying in and out repeatedly); wasp activity; any wire, trellis or garden netting that has been engulfed by the hedge over time; and thick woody stems that will need loppers rather than the trimmer blade.

In Kent gardens, it is common to find old wires and rose trellises buried inside privet and laurel hedges that have been left to grow for several years without a hard cut. A trimmer blade hitting concealed wire is a sharp shock and can damage the blade. Probe with your gloved hand first if the hedge is very dense.

Step 3: set up your cable or battery before cutting

If you are using a cordless model: make sure the battery is fully charged and, for longer jobs, have a second battery ready. Most domestic lithium pole trimmer batteries last between 30 and 50 minutes of continuous use. That is enough for a medium garden hedge in Sandwich or Deal, but not a long run of tall laurel or privet.

If you are using a corded electric model: lay the cable out behind you before you start, not in front. Clip a loop of cable to your belt or shoulder strap so the cable hangs away from the blade path. Plug into an RCD adaptor at the mains end.

Step 4: cut the sides before the top

Always cut the sides of the hedge first, working from the bottom upward. This removes loose growth from the face before you make the top cut, and means any clippings from the top fall onto a tidier surface that is easier to sweep or rake up afterwards.

Keep the pole angled so the cutting head is slightly away from you, not directly overhead. This keeps the debris moving away from your face and reduces the chance of clippings falling into your collar. Sweep the blade upward in long, smooth strokes rather than short chopping movements.

Step 5: cut the face in overlapping horizontal passes

For the sides of the hedge, hold the blade nearly vertical and sweep in wide, overlapping horizontal passes, moving along the hedge face. Overlap each pass by about a third so there are no missed strips. Work at a pace that lets the blade clear its cut rather than jamming on thick growth.

If the hedge face is genuinely overgrown (more than 15 to 20 centimetres of new growth), take two passes: a rough first pass to remove the bulk, then a second finer pass for the finish. Trying to take too much in one go puts strain on the motor and produces a rougher result.

Step 6: cut the top with the head nearly flat

Set the cutting head to its most horizontal position (or as close to flat as the tool allows) and sweep along the hedge top in the same overlapping-pass technique. The blade should be angled very slightly so the leading tip is marginally lower than the heel: this drives clippings away from you and prevents the blade from digging in at one end.

Keep your arms relaxed and let the pole's weight do some of the work. Trying to force the blade into the hedge top tends to produce wavy cuts. A light, consistent pressure gives a flatter finish.

For tall hedges (over 2.5 metres), cut the top in two overlapping passes: one from each side of the hedge if you can access both sides, or two passes from the same side with a slight angle change between them.

Step 7: clear up as you go

Do not let clippings build up on the ground while you are still working. Fallen clippings underfoot change the surface under your feet and create a slip hazard, particularly on patios and paths. Rake or blow clippings to one side as you complete each section, rather than waiting until the whole job is done.

Once the trimming is finished, wipe the blade with a clean rag and apply a light coat of blade oil before storing. Sap from privet, laurel and leylandii will gum up the mechanism quickly if left on. For more on tool care, see our guide on sharpening hedge shears and secateurs.
