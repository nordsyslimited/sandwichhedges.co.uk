Fuchsia hedge care: hardy fuchsia versus escallonia, hebe and potentilla for coastal gardens

In mild coastal gardens from Deal to Sandwich Bay, hardy fuchsia grows where nothing else will and flowers for months. But it is not the only informal flowering hedge suited to exposed East Kent sites, and the choice between fuchsia, escallonia, hebe and potentilla comes down to a few practical questions: how much screening do you need year-round, how cold does the spot get, and how much time do you want to spend on it?

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Hardy fuchsia: what it is and what it does

Fuchsia magellanica is the South American species behind virtually all the hardy fuchsia hedges grown in British coastal gardens. Its most commonly planted form, F. magellanica 'Riccartoni', has been in cultivation in the UK since the nineteenth century and is naturalised along parts of the south and west coast. In East Kent it is not native but it behaves almost as if it were: the mild coastal winters around Deal, Sandwich Bay and Kingsdown rarely get cold enough to kill it to the roots, and once established it regrows so vigorously in spring that temporary die-back is barely a setback.

The dangling red-and-purple flowers appear from June through to October, a colour range and longevity that few other hedging plants match. In a typical East Kent autumn, a fuchsia hedge is still in full flower when most other flowering shrubs have finished. Long-tongued bees, particularly bumblebees, visit the flowers freely.

How to prune a hardy fuchsia hedge

This is the question that catches most people out: fuchsia is not pruned like most hedging plants.

In late winter or early spring, once the worst frosts are past (late February to April in coastal Kent), cut all the top growth back to a low framework, 15-30cm from the ground. Remove any frost-blackened stems entirely. This looks severe the first time you do it, but it is the correct approach: fuchsia flowers on the current year's growth, so cutting hard in spring produces vigorous new shoots that carry this year's flowers. A fuchsia that is only lightly trimmed gradually builds up a congested framework of old wood, produces shorter and shorter flowering stems, and becomes woody at the base.

In summer, a light tip-trim keeps the hedge line tidy without removing the flower buds. Do not cut hard in summer: you will sacrifice most of the season's flowers.

In exposed coastal spots that do get hard frosts (some of the more open gardens around Eastry and the inland villages), the top growth may be killed entirely. This is not a problem: cut out the dead wood in spring and the rootstock will reshoot from the base. The plant is more resilient than it looks when frosted.

Nesting season and fuchsia hedges

Dense established fuchsia is used as nesting cover by several garden birds, particularly wren, dunnock and blackbird. Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, disturbing or destroying an active bird's nest is an offence. Nesting season runs from 1 March to 31 August. The spring pruning of fuchsia falls right at the start of this period. Check the plant carefully before cutting in March and April, and if you find an active nest, leave it until the nest is empty before completing the prune.

How fuchsia compares with the alternatives

Fuchsia is one of four informal flowering hedges commonly used in exposed coastal East Kent gardens. Each has a different set of strengths.

Escallonia

Escallonia is the more practical choice where year-round screening matters. It is evergreen: unlike fuchsia, which drops its leaves in a hard winter, escallonia holds dense, glossy foliage through all but the worst conditions. The flowers (small, pink to crimson, carried in clusters) appear in early summer, usually June to July, with a second flush in some years. Escallonia is slightly slower to establish than fuchsia and less tolerant of the poorest soils, but once settled it clips well into a semi-formal hedge that takes an annual trim without losing its shape.

The choice between them: if you need a hedge that screens year-round, escallonia. If you want the longest and most conspicuous flowering season and can live with thinner winter cover, fuchsia.

Hebe (shrubby veronica)

The larger-growing hebe species (particularly Hebe salicifolia and H. x franciscana 'Blue Gem') grow well in coastal conditions and produce white or lavender flower spikes in summer. They are genuinely evergreen and windproof once established. The limitation for hedging is that large hebes can be damaged by hard inland frosts: fine in the warmest coastal pockets from Sandwich Bay southward, but unreliable in colder village gardens. Hebe also resents hard renovation pruning, unlike fuchsia and escallonia. Once a hebe has become leggy and woody, cutting it back hard often kills it rather than regenerating it.

Best suited to: sheltered coastal sites, front gardens in Deal and Sandwich, anywhere that rarely gets below -5°C.

Potentilla (shrubby cinquefoil)

Potentilla fruticosa cultivars are fully hardy, drought-tolerant and flower for an exceptionally long season (May to October on a good year). They are lower-growing than fuchsia or escallonia, typically 0.6-1.2m, which suits a front boundary or low dividing hedge rather than a privacy screen. The flowers are smaller than fuchsia's but come in a range from white to bright yellow to coral, and the plant requires nothing beyond a light annual prune in early spring to keep it bushy. Potentilla is largely pest and disease free, and it works in poorer soils without the feeding that escallonia sometimes needs to establish.

Best suited to: low boundaries, exposed front gardens, anywhere a neat, compact flowering hedge is needed rather than a tall screen.

Summary: which to choose

Plant Winter cover Flower season Frost hardiness Pruning
Hardy fuchsia Thin to none in hard winters June to October Good coastal; roots survive hard frosts inland Hard cut late winter/early spring
Escallonia Dense, evergreen June to July (+ second flush) Good; handles Kent winters well Annual trim after flowering
Hebe Dense, evergreen June to August Coastal only; dislikes hard frosts Light annual tidy; does not renovate well
Potentilla Thin (deciduous to semi-evergreen) May to October Fully hardy across Kent Light cut early spring; very low maintenance

For most coastal East Kent gardens that want colour, pollinator value and a manageable informal boundary, hardy fuchsia is a strong first choice from Deal southward and in sheltered village gardens around Woodnesborough and Ash. Where privacy through winter matters more than the summer flower display, pair it with a section of escallonia or add a low post-and-rail to fill the gap in winter months.
