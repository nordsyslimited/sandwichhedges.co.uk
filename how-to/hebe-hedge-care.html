Hebe hedge care and pruning

Hebe is one of the most reliable low-maintenance hedging shrubs for East Kent's coastal gardens. Here is how to prune it, which varieties to choose, and how to keep it healthy through winter.

Updated September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Hebe turns up all over the gardens around Sandwich Bay, Pegwell Bay and the Deal seafront, and for good reason. It tolerates salt wind, needs almost no attention between trims, flowers reliably from late spring through to September and stays green all year. As a low formal or informal hedge in a coastal spot, it is hard to beat.

The catch is that most people never prune it at all, and within three or four years a hebe hedge has gone open in the middle, leggy at the top and lost its shape. Five minutes after flowering, every year, fixes that. Here is the process.

Step 1: Identify your variety

The pruning approach and timing depends on the type of hebe you have. Broadly:

  • Small-leaved or whipcord hebe (Hebe ochracea, Hebe armstrongii, Hebe cupressoides): slow-growing, very hardy, tiny scale-like leaves. Rarely needs pruning. Trim lightly after flowering in May or June if it is getting uneven.
  • Mid-sized bushy hebe (Hebe 'Autumn Glory', Hebe rakaiensis, Hebe 'Red Edge'): the most common hedging type. Cut back by one-third after flowering in July to August.
  • Large-leaved hebe (Hebe 'Midsummer Beauty', Hebe speciosa): tall, less hardy, long flower spikes. Prune in late May or after each flush of flowers. Protect in hard winters.

If you are not sure what you have, photograph the leaves and flower and check against the RHS plant finder. Getting the variety right before pruning matters: the timing window is different for each group.

Step 2: Wait for flowering to finish

Hebe flowers are a valuable late-season nectar source for bees and butterflies. There is no benefit to cutting before the flowers are genuinely past their best. The flowers fade from their tips downward: wait until the whole spike is dull and dried, not just the tip. For mid-sized hedging types in East Kent, this usually falls between late July and late August.

Check for nesting birds first

Hebe is low-growing but can shelter nesting birds in a warm, sheltered corner of the garden. It is an offence under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 to damage or destroy an active nest. Look into and around the hedge before you start, especially if it is near a wall or fence. Nesting season runs roughly from 1 March to 31 August. If there are birds in residence, wait until they have fledged before trimming.

Step 3: Gather the right tools

Tools and PPE

  • Sharp bypass secateurs for single plants or a short run of hedge.
  • One-handed hedge shears or a lightweight cordless trimmer for a long low hedge.
  • Gloves: hebe sap is not a known irritant but gloves keep hands clean.
  • Eye protection when using a powered trimmer.
  • If using a corded electric trimmer, connect via an RCD adaptor. Keep the cable behind you and away from the cutting head.
  • Avoid cutting after heavy rain: wet stems clog blades and wet conditions encourage disease on open cuts.

Step 4: Make the cut

Cut back by one-third of the current season's growth. For a hedge that has been maintained annually, that means removing the soft growth from this year's spurt and leaving the firmer shoots from last year in place. The result should be a shapely, compact mound or a flat-topped edge depending on the hedge style you are going for.

If the hedge has not been cut for two or more years and is looking open or leggy, cut harder in spring (April or May, before flowering) rather than in summer. Take it back by up to half, making sure there are still leaves on the stems you are cutting. Unlike lavender, most mid-sized hebe varieties will break from older wood if there is some foliage present, so a renovation cut is usually recoverable.

Step 5: Tidy up and feed

Collect all the trimmings promptly. Hebe cuttings are soft and break down quickly, but left in a heap against the plant they can harbour grey mould (Botrytis), which spreads onto the stems. A light balanced fertiliser in early April sets the plant up well for the season. Avoid feeding in late summer or autumn, as it encourages soft growth that can be caught by the first frost.

Varieties suited to East Kent

Hebe rakaiensis

Compact and very tidy: grows to about 60 cm with white flowers in summer. Excellent choice for a low formal hedge in a coastal garden around Deal or Sandwich Bay. Hardy and neat with minimal attention.

Hebe 'Red Edge'

Grey-green leaves with a red margin in winter, giving the hedge colour year-round. Pale lilac flowers in summer. Medium size, up to 60 cm. Good tolerance of salt wind. Very popular in coastal East Kent gardens for the winter leaf interest.

Hebe 'Autumn Glory'

One of the more vigorous types: can reach 90 cm. Violet-purple flowers from midsummer to autumn, giving it a longer season than most. Less tidy than rakaiensis and needs cutting back more firmly to keep it in check. Reasonable hardiness.

Hebe ochracea (whipcord hebe)

Unusual scale-like golden foliage that looks almost like a conifer. Very slow-growing and virtually maintenance-free once established. Flowers are small and white. Most useful as an accent or edging plant rather than a continuous hedge, but the hardiest of the group in an exposed position.

Hebe for coastal positions

On the exposed coastal margins around Sandwich Bay and out towards Pegwell Bay, the wind can be severe enough to damage plants that are borderline-hardy in calmer spots. In those positions, choose Hebe rakaiensis or a whipcord hebe as the outer-boundary hedge and reserve the more floriferous large-leaved varieties for sheltered inner positions. A hebe hedge on the seaward side of a garden that gets full south-westerly wind will still need attention: check for salt scorch (brown leaf tips) after a rough storm and trim those tips back promptly.

In the more sheltered gardens of Sandwich town itself, Worth, Ash and Woodnesborough, any of the mid-sized hebe varieties should be fine without additional protection.
