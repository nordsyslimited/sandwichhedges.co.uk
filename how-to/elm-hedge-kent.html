Elm hedge in Kent

Kent had thousands of elm trees in its hedgerows before Dutch elm disease. Disease-resistant varieties are available again, and an elm hedge is a genuine option for wildlife-conscious gardens across East Kent.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

In the hedgerows and lanes around Sandwich, Ash, Eastry and Worth, old elm stools and stumps are still visible, evidence of how dominant elm once was before the 1970s epidemic. Dutch elm disease (DED), caused by a fungus spread by bark beetles, killed around 25 million British elms. The landscapes of East Kent, once thick with elm in every hedgerow, lost one of their defining species.

Disease-resistant cultivars have been available from specialist UK nurseries for more than a decade now. They are not cheap and not widely planted yet, but for a garden hedge in this part of Kent, an elm is a genuinely interesting option. It has strong wildlife value (particularly for the White-letter Hairstreak butterfly), it is naturally semi-formal in habit, and it connects the hedge to the pre-disease landscape of the county.

Choosing the right variety

The main decision is whether you want a formal clipped hedge or a looser, more naturalistic boundary. The varieties behave differently and the right one depends on how much maintenance you want and what the space allows.

Wych elm (Ulmus glabra)

The UK's native elm most naturally resistant to DED. Grows to a broader, more arching form than the English elm, so it suits a wider informal hedge better than a narrow formal one. Its large leaves are distinctive; it holds them late into autumn and displays vivid yellow autumn colour.

Ulmus 'Lutece'

A French-bred disease-resistant clone, one of the most reliable for UK gardens. Upright growth (less spreading than wych elm), good DED resistance confirmed over 30 years of trials. Available from several specialist UK nurseries. Grows to around 20m as a tree if left; as a hedge it is maintained at whatever height you want.

Ulmus 'Lobel'

Narrow, columnar habit, so less pruning effort to keep it tidy. Good for a formal clipped look. Well-suited to restricted spaces. DED resistance is considered good though slightly below 'Lutece' in some trial comparisons.

Planting

Elm is planted as bare-root whips between October and March. The plants arrive as 60-90cm whips and are planted at 2-3 per metre in a single row (45-60cm spacing) for a garden hedge. Mark out the line first, dig individual planting holes or a trench 30-40cm deep, spread the roots, backfill and firm.

At planting, give each plant the formative cut: reduce the main stem by one-third and cut side shoots back to around 10cm from the central stem. This looks drastic on a small whip but elm breaks very strongly from these cuts and the basal branching it produces is what gives the hedge its density in later years.

Nesting season: 1 March to 31 August

Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 it is an offence to cut or damage a hedge containing an active nest. The main establishment and formative cuts for an elm hedge fall in winter, which is the safest window. For any summer tidy, check the hedge thoroughly before starting.

First-year care

Water during dry spells in the first summer. East Kent has lower than average rainfall compared with much of England, and a new hedge on well-drained chalk (common around Sandwich and Eastry) can dry out more quickly than expected. A mulch of 7-10cm depth around the base of each plant helps retain moisture and suppress weeds.

In late August of year 1 (after checking for nests), lightly trim the sides of the hedge. Do not cut the top yet. The goal in year 1 is to encourage side branching, not height.

Maintenance from year 2 onwards

From year 2, elm is maintained like hornbeam: cut once or twice a year. The main cut falls in late August to early September, with an optional lighter tidy in late June if the hedge is growing vigorously and there are no active nests. Use a hedge trimmer for large sections; a battered profile (slightly wider at the base) keeps the lower sections in light and prevents the base from thinning.

Elm can be cut hard if it outgrows its space. It is a coppice species and responds well to renovation cuts in winter, breaking strongly from old wood. If you have an old elm stool producing sucker growth in a hedgerow boundary (common in East Kent), you can train those suckers into a new hedge by reducing them to 30-40cm from the ground in winter and then managing the regrowth as a hedge from the second year.

Common questions

Can you still plant elm hedges in the UK?

Yes. Dutch elm disease wiped out most of the UK's mature elm trees in the 1970s, but disease-resistant cultivars are now commercially available. Varieties such as Ulmus 'Lutece', Ulmus 'Lobel' and wych elm (Ulmus glabra, the native species most naturally resistant to DED) can all be used to establish a hedge in Kent. Plants are sold as bare-root whips from specialist UK nurseries between November and March.

How fast does an elm hedge grow?

Disease-resistant elm cultivars typically grow 30 to 45 cm per year once established, depending on the variety and soil. Wych elm is at the slower end. Expect a 1.5-metre hedge within four or five years from bare-root whips. The rate is similar to hornbeam: steady rather than fast, but the resulting hedge is dense and long-lived.

When should an elm hedge be cut?

Elm hedges are deciduous and best cut in late summer (August to September) after the nesting season, or in winter (January to February) during dormancy. Avoid cutting in spring or early summer when the foliage is new. For the first formative cut after planting, reduce the main stem by one-third and cut side shoots back hard in the winter at planting.

Is elm good for wildlife?

Yes, particularly for the White-letter Hairstreak butterfly (Satyrium w-album), whose caterpillars feed only on elm leaves. This butterfly was severely affected by Dutch elm disease and disease-resistant elms are actively helping its recovery across the UK. Elm seed clusters also attract goldfinches and other finches in late spring before the leaves are fully out.
