Planting a hedge in sandy coastal soil: what works in East Kent

Sandy, free-draining soil looks easy to work with but it is genuinely demanding for newly planted hedges. Water drains through fast, nutrients wash out quickly, and young plants can struggle in their first dry spring. Getting the soil preparation and species choice right makes the difference between a hedge that takes hold and one that slowly fails.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981: nesting birds

It is an offence to damage or destroy the nest of any wild bird while it is in use or being built. Nesting season runs roughly 1 March to 31 August. For planting, this is relevant if you are cutting back existing vegetation to prepare the planting site during this period. Check for active nests before removing any existing scrub or overgrowth. The September to February window is the safest for clearing and planting work.

Sandy soils in East Kent: where they are and what they do

The most distinctively sandy soils in this part of Kent are found along the coastal belt: Sandwich Bay, Sandown Castle, the approach roads to Kingsdown and Deal seafront. Parts of the ground around Worth and Ash also run light on their sandier patches, though much of inland East Kent is chalk-based rather than pure sand.

Sandy soil warms up early in spring (useful), drains freely (useful up to a point), and is easy to work (useful). The problem for hedges is water retention. In a dry April or May, newly planted hedge roots in sandy ground can be in serious moisture stress within a week of the last rain, while the same plants in a clay garden would barely notice. The same free-draining character that makes sandy soils comfortable to work in also means nutrients leach through faster than in heavier ground.

The good news is that several excellent hedging species are genuinely adapted to exactly these conditions, and with correct preparation and mulching, you can establish a good hedge in sandy East Kent soil without exceptional effort.

Species that work: what to plant

Choose from this list with confidence for sandy, free-draining conditions, including exposed coastal positions near Sandwich Bay and Deal:

  • Sea buckthorn (Hippophae rhamnoides): native to British dunes and shingle beaches, genuinely at home in sandy coastal ground. Thorny, fast-growing, brilliant orange berries in autumn and winter. Excellent for wildlife. Will spread by suckering if not managed, which can be useful for covering a bank.
  • Tamarisk (Tamarix tetrandra or T. ramosissima): feathery pink flowers in spring or late summer, outstanding salt-wind tolerance. Commonly planted along the Kent coast and rightly so. Needs a harder prune than most to stay dense; annual cutting is recommended.
  • Rosa rugosa: tough, thorny, produces large, fragrant flowers (pink or white) and big scarlet hips in autumn. Establishes readily in sandy soil, resists salt wind, provides excellent security as well as wildlife habitat. Informal only: it does not clip into a neat shape.
  • Hawthorn (Crataegus monogyna): the backbone of the traditional East Kent mixed hedgerow, more drought-tolerant than its reputation suggests. Plant as bare-root in autumn for best results on sandy ground. Combines well with blackthorn, field maple and dog rose for a species-rich native hedge.
  • Griselinia littoralis: a New Zealand evergreen widely used on the Kent coast. Pale grey-green leaves, reasonable growth rate, handles salt-laden winds well. Not the fastest-establishing in sandy soil but reliable once settled. Gives a more formal appearance than the native species.
  • Elaeagnus x ebbingei: fast-growing evergreen with slightly silvery leaves and tiny, intensely fragrant autumn flowers (easy to miss until you smell them). Very coastal-tolerant and better than average on free-draining soils.
  • Escallonia: semi-evergreen, pink or red flowers in summer, good coastal and salt tolerance. Used widely in Kent seaside gardens. Prefers reasonable drainage.

Species to avoid or plant carefully in pure sand

Hornbeam (Carpinus betulus) prefers heavier, moisture-retentive soils and tends to establish poorly in very light sandy ground. It can work if you are generous with organic matter and maintain mulching, but it is not the first choice here. Beech is more tolerant than hornbeam on lighter soils but still benefits from improved preparation in the sandiest positions. Cherry laurel in very light, dry sand can fail in its first dry summer unless watered attentively.

Step 1: Test and assess the soil

Before ordering plants, do a simple water test. Dig a hole about 30 centimetres deep where the hedge will go, fill it with water, and see how quickly it drains. If it empties within ten minutes, the soil is very free-draining. If it is still there an hour later, it is much less of a concern. You can also simply squeeze a handful of moist soil: if it falls apart immediately rather than holding any shape, it is predominantly sand.

Also note whether there is a layer of firmer or heavier subsoil beneath the topsoil. Sometimes the top 20 to 30 centimetres is light sand over a more moisture-retentive stratum below. In this case the soil challenge is less severe, as roots that penetrate deep enough will find better conditions.

Step 2: Dig the planting trench

Dig a trench the full length of the hedge line, at least 60 centimetres wide and 45 to 60 centimetres deep. This is the most effort-intensive step, but it is the one you can only do properly once. Digging deep and wide gives you the chance to improve the full root zone, not just a narrow planting hole.

If you are planting a double row of native hedgerow plants (common practice for denser coverage), make the trench 75 to 90 centimetres wide to accommodate both rows.

Step 3: Improve the soil

Mix well-rotted compost or manure into the excavated soil at roughly one part organic matter to three parts soil by volume. Do not use raw wood chip or unrotted compost, as these can tie up nitrogen in the soil as they break down. Well-rotted material makes a real difference in sandy conditions: it improves water retention and provides slow-release nutrients as it continues to break down.

On very light sand near the coast, some gardeners also add a small quantity of horticultural clay granules or moisture-retaining gel, though these are not essential if you are willing to mulch thoroughly and water in the first season.

Step 4: Plant and firm in

Plant bare-root stock in autumn (October to November) or pot-grown stock at any time the ground is workable and not frozen. For most hedging species, bare-root plants at 60 to 90 centimetre planting spacing are the practical choice: they are cheaper, easier to handle in bulk, and establish at least as well as pot-grown in sandy ground where roots can spread freely.

Set each plant to its previous soil mark (visible as a faint colour change on the stem), firm the soil in well around the roots, and water generously even if the day is overcast or cool. Air pockets around roots are the enemy of establishment in any soil, and especially in free-draining ground where drying can happen quickly.

Before you start: safety checks for planting work

  • If digging near a boundary, check the position of any underground services (gas, water, electricity) before you begin. A utility search through your local authority or a tracing service is worth doing for a new hedge line.
  • Wear sturdy gloves when handling bare-root thorny species (hawthorn, blackthorn, Rosa rugosa). The thorns are substantial and cuts in soil carry infection risk.
  • If using powered tools (cultivator, strimmer to clear the existing line), wear eye protection and ear defenders, and keep bystanders clear.

Step 5: Mulch thoroughly

Mulching is the single most important step for a hedge in sandy soil and the one most often skipped. Lay a 10-centimetre layer of well-rotted bark, compost or wood chip along the entire hedge line immediately after planting. Keep the mulch a centimetre or two away from each stem to avoid collar rot.

A good mulch on sandy soil can halve your watering needs in the first summer. It also suppresses competing grass and weeds that would otherwise take moisture and nutrients before the young hedge plants can. Renew the mulch each autumn in the first three years.

Step 6: Water in the first season

Even autumn-planted hedges in East Kent can hit a dry spell in the following spring before roots have spread far. Check the soil moisture under the mulch every few days during a dry spell, particularly from March to June. If the soil is dry to 10 centimetres under the mulch, water the hedge line thoroughly rather than lightly: deep watering encourages roots to grow downward, light watering keeps them shallow where they will dry out fastest.

Once a hedge has been established for 18 to 24 months and roots have spread into the surrounding soil, watering is rarely needed in Kent conditions. It is the first two summers that matter.

A note on autumn planting in East Kent

Autumn planting (mid-October through November) is clearly the better approach for sandy East Kent soils compared with spring. Autumn rain handles establishment watering, the plant is not in active growth and under water stress, and root development over winter gives the hedge a head start before the first dry spell. The coastal mild winters around Sandwich Bay and Deal also mean the ground rarely freezes hard, so bare-root establishment continues through most of the winter months.
