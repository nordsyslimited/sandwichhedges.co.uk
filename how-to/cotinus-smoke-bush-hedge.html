Smoke bush (Cotinus coggygria) as an informal garden boundary

Cotinus earns its place with months of hazy plumes, outstanding autumn colour and very little fuss. Here is how to prune it, when to prune it, and whether your East Kent garden has the conditions it needs.

Updated October 2026 · By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Cotinus coggygria, known widely as smoke bush or smoke tree, is one of those shrubs that looks like it should be difficult to grow and turns out to be the opposite. The famous plumes are not flowers: they are the feathery, elongated stalks left after the flowers fade, which persist through July and August in shades of pinkish-grey. Then, in October and November, the leaves turn orange, scarlet and crimson before they fall. In East Kent's free-draining chalk and loamy soils, it rarely needs more than one considered prune each year.

Common cultivars include 'Royal Purple' (deep purple-bronze leaves, plum-coloured smoke), 'Grace' (very large leaves, exceptional autumn scarlet), 'Notcutt's Variety' (reliably purple through summer) and the straight species form, which has green leaves and reliable orange autumn colour. All respond to pruning in the same way.

Before you start

Cotinus sap can irritate sensitive skin. Wear sturdy gloves whenever you are handling cut stems. Secateurs should be sharp: a clean cut through woody growth heals faster than a crushed or torn one. If your blades are dragging rather than cutting, sharpen or replace them before you start.

1. Choose your pruning goal: plumes or foliage

Smoke bush offers two quite different effects depending on how you manage it, and you need to decide which you want before you pick up the secateurs.

Light annual prune (plumes and autumn colour): remove only dead, damaged and crossing stems in February. Leave most of the plant intact. The shrub produces plentiful feathery plumes in summer and puts on a reliable autumn display. This is the right approach for an established plant in a garden where the dramatic summer effect is part of the reason you planted it.

Hard stooling (bold foliage, less smoke): cut all stems back to 30 to 40 cm from the ground in late February, every two or three years. You lose almost all the smoke plumes for that season, but the replacement shoots produce noticeably larger leaves with stronger, more saturated colour. 'Grace' grown this way produces leaves the size of a hand. Many gardeners prefer the bold foliage effect, particularly in a small garden where a 4-metre plant would be unwieldy.

You can also combine both: stool half the plant and leave the other half, alternating in subsequent years. This gives some plumes every season whilst keeping the foliage within bounds.

2. Timing: late February, before the buds break

The window for pruning is late February into early March, before the new growth begins. Pruning in autumn is not recommended: fresh cuts can die back if a cold spell follows, and you remove the bark and stem tissues the plant relies on to store energy through winter.

If you leave the job until March or later, check the shrub for any early nesting activity before you cut. Blackbirds and robins can begin nesting from mid-February in a mild year, and a dense cotinus is a plausible site.

3. Check the shrub before cutting

A quick visual check costs very little time. Look into the centre of the shrub for any sign of a nest bowl, and listen for alarm calls if you disturb the stems. If you find an active nest, wait until the young have fledged before resuming. Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, intentionally damaging or destroying an active nest is a criminal offence.

4. Remove dead, damaged and crossing stems first

Whether you are doing a light tidy or a full stooling, start by taking out the obvious: stems that died over winter, stems rubbing against each other, and anything growing inward towards the centre. Use sharp secateurs for growth up to about 1 cm in diameter. For thicker stems, use loppers. A pruning saw is only needed if the base stems have grown very large over many seasons without any stooling.

Always cut to a healthy outward-facing bud, a main junction, or the base of the stem. Leaving a long stub invites die-back and looks untidy.

5. Stooling: how to cut hard without harming the plant

Stooling sounds alarming if you have not done it before. In late February, cut every stem back to 30 to 40 cm from the ground, just above a node or bud if you can find one. The stumps should look like a low crown of short, blunt-ended stubs. It seems drastic.

Cotinus responds well. By late April new shoots will be pushing from the base of each stub and from lower on the trunk. By July, on a well-established plant, the new growth will be a metre tall and the leaves will be notably larger and more richly coloured than in a lightly pruned year. Do not stool a plant in its first two years: let it establish first, then begin the rotation once it is settled in.

6. Aftercare: mulch and water after a hard prune

After stooling, apply a 5 to 7 cm layer of bark mulch around the base, keeping it clear of the cut stubs themselves. This retains moisture during the dry spring conditions that are common in Sandwich and along the Deal coast, and suppresses weeds whilst the plant is channelling its energy into new shoots.

Water well if April and May are dry. Cotinus is drought-tolerant once established, but a newly stooled plant is effectively regenerating from the base and will appreciate help during its first growing season after a hard cut.

Smoke bush rarely needs feeding beyond an annual mulch of garden compost or bark chippings. It does best in moderately infertile, well-drained soil: too much nitrogen drives leafy growth at the expense of the autumn colour and the smoke effect.
