Pittosporum hedge care in Kent: varieties, coastal tolerance and trimming

Pittosporum is the evergreen that coastal Kent gardeners reach for when privet fails and leylandii feels like overkill. But not every variety survives an East Kent winter inland, and getting the trimming timing right is different from most other hedges.

Updated August 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

We see pittosporum in gardens along the coast from Sandwich Bay to Deal: a dense, small-leaved evergreen with a naturally neat habit that clips well and tolerates the salt wind that shreds less resilient choices. Inland, though, the picture is more complicated. A hard frost in Eastry or Worth can defoliate or kill a pittosporum that would have been perfectly happy fifty metres from the sea.

This guide compares the main pittosporum varieties for East Kent conditions, sets them alongside the most common coastal hedge alternatives, and covers trimming timing and technique.

Pittosporum varieties: which one for Kent?

The genus includes many dozens of species, but for hedging in Kent you are really choosing between two main ones.

Pittosporum tenuifolium

The most commonly planted hedging pittosporum in Britain. Small, wavy-edged leaves on near-black stems, with a naturally columnar form that clips into a formal hedge without much effort. Growth rate is moderate, roughly 30 to 40 cm per year in a sheltered coastal spot. Frost hardiness is quoted as RHS H4 (down to approximately -10°C), though in practice sustained frost below -5°C with wind chill can damage exposed specimens.

In East Kent: fine at Sandwich Bay and on Deal seafront. Inland, choose a sheltered south- or west-facing run and mulch the base in the first two winters.

Pittosporum tobira

Larger, rounder leaves with a glossy finish, and strongly scented white flowers in late spring. More drought-tolerant than tenuifolium and marginally more salt-tolerant, but slightly less frost-hardy (RHS H3, down to about -5°C). Better suited to coastal strips where frosts are short-lived and mild. Rarely planted as a formal hedge in Kent: the large leaves look coarse when clipped, and its natural habit is a rounded shrub rather than an upright screen. More useful as a boundary plant than a garden-dividing hedge.

Comparison: pittosporum vs coastal alternatives

The most common question we hear from coastal clients is whether to go with pittosporum, griselinia or escallonia. The honest answer is that all three work along the East Kent coast, but they suit different situations.

Our verdict for Sandwich Bay and coastal Deal

Pittosporum tenuifolium is the best choice where you want a formal, dense, small-leaved evergreen screen that clips cleanly. Its natural colour range (silver-green, bronze, purple-tinged) offers more variety than griselinia's uniform apple-green. Griselinia is the safer choice if you want faster establishment and are less certain about shelter. Escallonia is the pick for a lower, flowering boundary hedge where neatness matters less than colour and pollinator value.

Our verdict for Ash, Woodnesborough and inland Eastry

Inland East Kent sits far enough from the coast that the moderating maritime influence fades. Frost can be more severe and persistent than along the Deal and Sandwich Bay strip. Griselinia is the better default choice for most inland gardens: it establishes quickly and the same frost events that might kill a pittosporum will leave griselinia merely looking tired in February before recovering in March. If you are set on pittosporum, plant it against a south- or west-facing wall or fence for the first two winters and mulch well.

When to trim pittosporum

Nesting season: check before you cut

The UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs roughly 1 March to 31 August. The safe cutting window for pittosporum is September to February for a single autumn trim, or a light tidy in mid-May (after any late frosts) followed by a second cut in August if you want two passes. Before cutting in May, look carefully into the hedge for active nests.

Pittosporum tenuifolium grows most actively from late spring into summer. The trimming windows that work well in East Kent are:

  • Late May to early June: a light shaping after the main flush of spring growth. Do not cut hard in May: late frosts can occur in inland Kent, and fresh cuts on a cold night can damage new growth. Wait until the risk of frost has passed, usually mid-May in most of our area.
  • Late August to mid-September: the main annual trim. By late August the nesting season is effectively over and growth has slowed. This is the best time for a more thorough cut. Avoid cutting after mid-October: you want the plant settled before winter.

Before you start: safety

  • Eye protection: essential when using a hedge trimmer on any evergreen with woody stems.
  • Ear defenders for petrol or corded electric trimmers.
  • Gloves: pittosporum sap can cause mild skin irritation in sensitive individuals.
  • RCD adaptor on any extension lead for a corded electric trimmer.
  • Ladder safety: use a tripod ladder on soft ground rather than a step ladder, and have someone footing it.
  • Wasp check: inspect the hedge for nests before starting, particularly in August and September.

How hard can you cut pittosporum?

Pittosporum tenuifolium responds well to moderate clipping but does not regenerate readily from old bare wood the way a deciduous shrub does. The rule is: never cut back further than the last year or two of growth, and always leave some green foliage on every branch you cut. If you cut back into bare wood, those stems are unlikely to break into growth again.

If a pittosporum hedge has been allowed to get out of hand, gradual reduction over two or three years is safer than a single hard cut. Take 15 to 20 cm off the overall dimensions each year rather than cutting to the size you want immediately.

What to do after frost damage

Frost damage on pittosporum typically shows as brown or collapsed foliage from late January into March. In most coastal East Kent gardens this is superficial: new growth pushes through in April. Resist the urge to cut the brown foliage off in February. Wait until mid-April, assess what is genuinely dead, and cut back to the nearest healthy green growth. Plants that still have some live green in the structure will usually recover. Plants that have gone fully brown to ground level rarely do.

If you lose a pittosporum to frost and want to replant the same spot, consider griselinia or a more frost-tolerant pittosporum such as 'Irene Paterson' (which tends to be slightly hardier than the species). In a sheltered Sandwich Bay garden the loss of a single plant in a hard winter is not necessarily a reason to change species entirely.

Varieties worth knowing

Pittosporum tenuifolium 'Silver Queen': grey-green leaves edged white. Particularly popular in coastal gardens in Deal and Sandwich Bay where the silver tones read well against sea-light. Slightly less vigorous than the plain species, so allows a slightly slower-growing hedge.

Pittosporum tenuifolium 'Tom Thumb': compact, dark purple-bronze foliage. Rarely grows beyond 1.2 m as a hedge. Useful for low-boundary or front-garden hedging where a full-height screen would block light.

Pittosporum tenuifolium 'Garnettii': cream and grey-green variegation with pink tints in cold weather. Slightly more frost-tolerant than plain tenuifolium according to RHS trials. A reasonable choice for inland Kent gardens where the plain species feels risky.
