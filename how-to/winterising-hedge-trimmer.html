Winterising your hedge trimmer: end-of-season storage

The hedge trimmer that went away in good order in November is the one that starts in March. Twenty minutes now saves a frustrating April morning and an expensive repair bill from a dealer who has a queue of the same problem.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

The last cut of the year in East Kent typically falls somewhere between September and November, depending on the species and the weather. Once the hedge work is done and the tools are going away for winter, a bit of attention now means a straightforward start in spring rather than a broken tool at the worst moment.

This guide covers both petrol trimmers and battery-electric trimmers. The steps are different enough that it is worth reading the right section for your machine. The one step that applies to both is blade maintenance, so that comes first.

Safety before starting

Before cleaning or servicing any hedge trimmer:

  • Petrol trimmer: let the engine and exhaust cool completely before handling. Never clean near an open flame or ignition source. Petrol vapour is highly flammable. Remove the spark plug cap before any work near the blade.
  • Battery trimmer: remove the battery before any blade cleaning or inspection. A hedge trimmer blade with a live battery installed can start with a brush of the trigger.
  • Both types: blades stay sharp after a season's cutting. Wear heavy leather gloves when handling the blade bar. Keep a blade guard on whenever the trimmer is not in active use.

Step 1: Clean the blade bar immediately after the last cut

Sap and plant resin left on the blade for weeks or months can harden and corrode the metal. The best time to clean the blade is when you come in from the last job, while the material is still fresh.

  1. Remove the battery (battery trimmers) or remove the spark plug cap (petrol trimmers).
  2. Use a stiff brush or old toothbrush to remove clippings and debris from between the blade teeth and from the blade bar surface.
  3. Wipe both faces of the blade bar with a cloth dampened with a small amount of white spirit, methylated spirits or purpose-made resin remover. This dissolves the sticky sap residue that a dry brush cannot shift.
  4. Dry the blade bar completely with a clean cloth before applying any lubricant.

Do not use a jet washer on the blade bar or drive housing: water forced into the drive mechanism and bearings causes corrosion and shortens the life of the machine significantly.

Step 2: Inspect the blades for damage

With the blade clean, work along both edges looking for:

  • Nicked or chipped teeth: caused by cutting into wire, stones or buried timber in the hedge base. Small nicks that do not affect the blade profile are acceptable. A tooth that is significantly chipped or misaligned should be dressed by a professional saw dealer before the next season.
  • Bent blade bar: hold the trimmer at arm's length and look down the length of the blade. A twist or bend is visible and affects cut quality. This requires a dealer inspection.
  • Loose blade bolts: check any accessible blade bar fasteners. Loose bolts allow blade movement and wear the drive mechanism.

If you find damage that needs attention, autumn is the best time to book a dealer service. Saw shops across East Kent get busy in spring when everyone is trying to get their tools ready at the same time. Bringing a trimmer in over winter typically means a shorter wait.

Step 3: Lubricate the blade bar

A light coating of oil protects the blade metal from moisture over winter and keeps the teeth moving freely.

  1. Apply a thin, even coat of light machine oil or blade oil to both faces of the blade bar using a cloth. Do not flood the blade: a thin wipe is sufficient.
  2. Work the blade manually a few times (by hand, with gloves, battery removed or spark plug cap removed) to distribute the oil between the teeth.
  3. Apply a small amount of oil to the blade drive mechanism through any lubrication points indicated in your machine's manual.
  4. Wipe off any excess oil from the blade guard before fitting it for storage.

STIHL, Husqvarna and Bosch all produce purpose-made blade oils for their tools. A general light machine oil works equally well. Avoid heavy grease on the teeth: it collects plant debris and is harder to remove before the next use.

Step 4: Petrol trimmer fuel management

This is the most important step for petrol tool owners. Modern pump petrol in the UK (E10 since 2021, E5 prior) contains ethanol, which absorbs moisture from the air and degrades in the fuel system within a few weeks to months. Stale ethanol-blended petrol leaves a varnish residue that blocks the small jets in a hedge trimmer carburettor. A blocked carburettor is the single most common cause of a petrol two-stroke or four-stroke trimmer failing to start after winter storage.

You have two options:

Option A: Drain the fuel system. Run the engine until it stops from fuel starvation, then remove the fuel cap and drain any remaining fuel from the tank. Run the engine again from empty until it stalls a second time. This empties the carburettor float chamber. Store dry.

Option B: Use a fuel stabiliser. Add the manufacturer-recommended amount of fuel stabiliser (available from any tool dealer) to the last tank of fresh, high-octane petrol. Run the engine for five to ten minutes to distribute the treated fuel through the carburettor. Store with this fuel in the system. This is adequate for storage up to about six months.

Option A is more reliable if you are storing through a full winter and are not certain when you will use the tool again. Option B is convenient and works well if you cut in September and expect to be back using the trimmer by March.

A note on E5 fuel: if your trimmer sees heavy seasonal use, switching to an alkylate petrol (sold as Aspen 2 or Aspen 4 depending on engine type, available online and from some tool dealers) eliminates the ethanol problem entirely. It costs more per litre but degrades far more slowly in storage, starts cleaner, and is kinder to carburettors and fuel lines over the life of the machine.

Step 5: Battery trimmer battery care

Lithium-ion batteries last longest when stored at around 40 to 60 per cent charge at room temperature. Most tool batteries have an indicator that shows approximate charge level.

  1. Remove the battery from the trimmer.
  2. Check the charge indicator. If the battery is near flat, charge it to around half. If it is fully charged, run a tool briefly to bring it down to around half before storage. This is less critical for short winter storage (three to four months) but matters more for longer periods.
  3. Store the battery indoors: a dry cupboard, utility room or insulated garage is suitable. The main thing to avoid is persistent cold below freezing and any source of moisture.
  4. Wipe the battery contacts with a dry cloth before storage. Dirty or corroded contacts are a common cause of intermittent connection problems.
  5. Check the battery charge once or twice over winter. If it has dropped significantly, top it up to around half again. Lithium cells that are left deeply discharged for months can be permanently damaged.

If the battery will not hold charge or shows a fault indicator before going into storage, autumn is the time to replace it rather than spring. Battery replacements often have a lead time, and discovering a dead battery in March when the hedges need cutting is avoidable.

Step 6: Store the trimmer correctly

A few simple storage habits extend tool life significantly:

  • Fit the blade guard. Most trimmers come with a plastic or rubber guard that fits over the blade bar. Use it. It protects both the blade and anything — or anyone — near the tool in storage.
  • Store off the floor. A tool hanging on a wall bracket, on a shelf or on hooks stays dry and is far less likely to be knocked over. Floor storage in a damp garage invites rust on unprotected metal surfaces.
  • Cover if the storage area is dusty. Dust drawn into the air filter of a petrol trimmer between seasons adds unnecessary wear on the next start. A cloth over the tool costs nothing.
  • Keep the manual nearby. Most warranty and service information, including oil type and mixing ratios for two-stroke engines, is in the manual. A stored trimmer and a stored manual in the same place is a habit worth forming.

Annual service: what to book in

A petrol trimmer used regularly through a season benefits from a professional service every one to two years. For a domestic machine used on a single garden, this typically means:

  • Air filter clean or replacement
  • Spark plug inspection and replacement if worn
  • Fuel filter replacement
  • Carburettor adjustment if idle or full-throttle running is rough
  • Blade bar inspection and sharpening if needed

STIHL GB has a useful series of maintenance videos on their YouTube channel for owners of STIHL tools. Searching for your specific model alongside "maintenance" or "winterising" will find the relevant content. Husqvarna UK has similarly detailed how-to guides for their range.

Battery trimmers have fewer annual service requirements, but the blade and drive mechanism benefit from the same blade inspection and lubrication routine described above. If the battery is more than three to four years old and is showing noticeably reduced runtime, that winter is the time to replace it rather than squeezing one more season out of a failing cell.
