Hedge plants and hay fever: high and low allergy species for Kent gardens

The hedge at the bottom of your garden may be doing more to your sinuses than you realise. A comparison of common Kent hedge species by their allergen risk, with practical advice for anyone who suffers.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Hay fever affects roughly one in four people in the UK, and the hedge bordering a garden can be a significant pollen source. The pollen calendar in East Kent starts in January with hazel catkins, runs through spring tree and shrub pollen, peaks with grass pollen in June and July, and trails off with late-summer flowering species in August. A hedge that flowers through several of these windows can extend the season for people who would otherwise get some relief.

The good news is that how a hedge is managed matters as much as which species it is. A privet hedge clipped before its flowers open is a very different proposition to a privet hedge left to bloom in June. This guide compares the common hedge species by their allergen risk and sets out what, if anything, can be done about each.

Trimming timing: hay fever and nesting season overlap

Nesting season runs from 1 March to 31 August. The proper cutting window is September to February. This creates an awkward overlap for allergy sufferers: the spring pollen season is also the nesting season, when hedge trimming is restricted. The practical answer is to do your main cut in autumn, before pollen season starts, and avoid trimming in peak pollen months unless the hedge is demonstrably empty of nests.

The comparison: common Kent hedge species and allergy risk

The table below ranks common hedge plants by the volume and type of pollen they produce, and by how that pollen behaves in the air.

High-allergen species in detail

Hazel

Hazel catkins appear in January and February, before most people have started thinking about pollen at all. The pollen is fine, light and wind-dispersed in large quantities, and because it comes so early in the year the immune system is not yet braced for it. For people who also react to birch pollen, hazel is a double blow as the two are related. A formal clipped hazel hedge produces far fewer catkins than an unmanaged hedge, since the new growth that carries them is cut off each autumn.

Leylandii

Leylandii pollen settles as a visible yellow-green dust in March and April, which gives a reasonable indication of how much of it is in the air. The pollen is allergenic for many people and the volume produced by a large, unmanaged hedge is considerable. Keeping leylandii trimmed back firmly each August reduces pollen production in the following spring, since the trimming removes the new growth that would carry pollen. This is one of the better practical reasons for annual leylandii maintenance beyond keeping it to a manageable size.

Yew

Yew pollen season runs from February to April and the pollen is wind-dispersed. Some people react to it quite severely. All parts of yew except the berry flesh are also toxic, so it is worth wearing gloves and a mask when trimming and clearing the cuttings. Despite this, yew is one of the best formal hedging plants available and its pollen, while irritating for sensitive individuals, is produced for a relatively short window each year.

Privet

Privet is interesting because it is insect-pollinated, so the airborne pollen load is not particularly high. The issue for allergy sufferers is the sap: privet sap contains ligustrin and other compounds that can cause respiratory irritation, skin rashes and eye symptoms when the hedge is cut. If you are sensitive, wear an FFP2 dust mask, eye protection and gloves when trimming, and shower and change clothes afterwards. Trimming privet in damp conditions rather than a dry, windy day also helps contain the sap particles.

Low-allergen choices for East Kent

If you are planting a new hedge and have hay fever in the household, the clearest low-allergen choices for East Kent conditions are:

  • Beech (Fagus sylvatica): reliable, formal, keeps its russet winter leaves. Modest pollen for a wind-pollinated plant. Suits the chalk and loam soils around Sandwich, Eastry and Worth.
  • Hornbeam (Carpinus betulus): similar to beech but tolerates heavier, wetter soils better. Good on the lower-lying ground around Ash and Woodnesborough.
  • Holly (Ilex aquifolium): insect-pollinated; pollen stays within a metre or so of the plant. Slow-growing but very dense and long-lived.
  • Bay laurel (Laurus nobilis): aromatic but insect-pollinated; not a significant hay fever trigger for most people, though the volatile oils can irritate sensitive airways. Worth noting it needs frost protection in exposed sites.

How trimming affects pollen exposure

A clipped formal hedge of almost any species produces less pollen than the same plant left to flower. Trimming removes the new growth that carries flowers before it has a chance to open. For hawthorn, hazel, privet and leylandii, an annual cut in September removes the season's growth that would otherwise flower the following spring or summer. This does not eliminate pollen production entirely but it substantially reduces it compared to an unmanaged hedge.

The act of trimming itself is a different matter. Disturbing foliage releases pollen, spores and sap particles into the air regardless of species. For sensitive individuals, the practical advice is:

  • Trim on a still, damp morning rather than on a dry, windy day.
  • Wear an FFP2 or FFP3 dust mask, not a simple surgical mask.
  • Wear eye protection: safety glasses or goggles.
  • Wear gloves and long sleeves, particularly for privet and laurel.
  • Shower and change clothes after trimming.
  • Consider having someone else do the trimming during peak pollen season.

East Kent: does the coastal location help?

The prevailing winds in East Kent come off the sea from the south-west. On days when the wind is from the sea, airborne pollen concentrations are lower than on still days or when the wind comes from inland. Sandwich, Deal and Sandwich Bay benefit from this reasonably often in spring and early summer, though the effect is inconsistent. The low-lying fields around Ash, Woodnesborough and Eastry, flanked by agricultural hedgerows, can have higher pollen counts on warm still days in June when the grass pollen season peaks. If hedge pollen adds to that, it can be a difficult few weeks.

Grass pollen is the dominant hay fever trigger for most sufferers in Kent, and no hedge choice eliminates that. But reducing pollen from hedges around the garden boundary is one part of managing total exposure, alongside monitoring pollen forecasts and keeping windows closed on high-count days.
