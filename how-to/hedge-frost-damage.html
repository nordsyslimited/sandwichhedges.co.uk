Frost damage to hedges: which species suffer and what to do about it

A hard Kent winter rarely causes blanket hedge deaths, but it does sort the reliably hardy plants from the ones that were always gambling on a mild year. Here is how to tell which camp your hedge is in, and what to do if the cold has already done its worst.

Updated September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

The East Kent coast is genuinely milder than much of the country. The proximity of the Channel moderates temperatures around Sandwich, Deal and Sandwich Bay, and we rarely see the sustained deep freezes that hit inland areas. But "milder" is not "frost-free," and the villages further from the coast: Woodnesborough, Ash, Eastry and Worth, can still experience nights below -5°C on clear, still winter evenings.

For hedges, this matters most if you have planted species that are only marginally hardy in the UK. Ken Crowther, award-winning broadcaster and Chartered Institute of Horticulture member, covers the frost-specific risks for laurel hedges in his World Radio Gardening video "Managing a laurel hedge and how to avoid frost damage" (youtube.com/watch?v=XkW8B9vQvSI). The comparison below covers the broader picture: which hedging species are reliably hardy in Kent, which are borderline, and which are best avoided on exposed or inland sites.

Before you cut back frost-damaged growth: check for nests

The UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest, regardless of the condition of the hedge. Nesting season runs roughly from 1 March to 31 August. If you need to cut back dead or damaged growth during this window, check the hedge carefully for active nests before starting. The safe cutting window is September to February. See our guide on wildlife-friendly hedge trimming.

What frost damage looks like

Frost damage can appear immediately after a hard night, or show itself gradually over the following week or two as damaged tissue dies back. The most common signs are:

  • Blackened or brown leaves that droop and do not recover after a few days of milder weather.
  • Wilting growth at the tips of stems, even when the soil has adequate moisture.
  • Bark splitting (frost crack), particularly on the south side of stems where alternate freezing and thawing causes expansion.
  • Discoloured, water-soaked patches on leaves that turn brown and papery as the tissue dies.
  • Dieback progressing inward from the branch tips toward the main stem over several weeks.

It is important not to confuse frost damage with other problems. Brown or scorched leaves in winter can also result from cold drying winds (desiccation damage), waterlogged soil around the roots, or salt spray in coastal locations like Sandwich Bay. The pattern of damage and the timing relative to a hard frost will usually distinguish these causes. Our guide to hedge health troubleshooting covers the broader diagnostic process.

Before you start cutting back: the safety checks

Before you start

  • Wear eye protection: secateurs and loppers can eject debris, particularly on dry, brittle frost-damaged wood.
  • Wear sturdy gloves: damaged woody stems are often sharp and splintered.
  • If using a powered trimmer to tidy up frost-damaged evergreens, use an RCD adaptor on any corded electric model, and check the cable for damage before starting.
  • Check for wasp nests and brambles growing through the base of the hedge before reaching in.
  • Do not use a ladder to reach frost-damaged growth at height without someone footing it. Prefer a pole trimmer or long-reach loppers where the hedge height allows. See our guide on using a long-reach hedge trimmer.

The scratch test: is the stem alive or dead?

Before cutting back any frost-damaged growth, use the scratch test. This is the most reliable way to determine where a stem is still alive and where it is dead.

  1. Use your thumbnail or the tip of a knife to scrape away a small section of bark from a suspect stem.
  2. Healthy living tissue beneath the bark is green, moist and slightly flexible.
  3. Dead tissue is brown, dry, or discoloured all the way through.
  4. On a partially damaged stem, work from the tip back toward the main stem, scratching at intervals. The point where green tissue returns is the point to cut just above.
  5. If green tissue is present at the base of a stem that appears dead above, the plant may well regenerate from the base given time and patience.

When to cut back vs when to wait

This is where a good number of gardeners make the wrong call in both directions: cutting back too early, or waiting so long that dead wood becomes a problem.

Wait if it is still winter or early spring

Cutting back frost-damaged stems before the risk of further frost has passed exposes fresh cuts to another freeze, which can kill back further into what was healthy wood. In Kent, this means waiting until mid-April at the earliest before doing any serious cutting back on tender species. If the weather remains mild in late March, a cautious scratch-test check is fine, but do not cut.

Cut when new growth is clearly emerging

Once you see new buds breaking or fresh growth beginning on the unaffected parts of the plant: typically late April to May in Kent, this is your signal. The plant has the energy to respond to pruning, and you can see clearly where the dead wood ends and the live wood begins. Cut just above a healthy bud or lateral shoot, making a clean angled cut with sharp secateurs to avoid crushing the stem.

Remove dead wood before summer

Leaving large amounts of dead wood on a hedge through the summer creates a habitat for fungal disease and boring insects. Once you have confirmed the extent of the damage in late spring, remove the dead material cleanly. On species like pittosporum, which can be badly cut back in a hard winter, the live regrowth from the base can be quite strong once dead material is removed and the plant has good light.

Prevention: what you can do before winter

Prevention is considerably less work than recovery, and most of it happens in autumn.

  • Mulch the root zone: A 5cm layer of bark chips, well-rotted compost or leaf mould over the root area in November insulates the soil, moderates temperature swings and retains moisture. Keep mulch clear of the stem base to avoid rot.
  • Stop nitrogen feeding after mid-July: High-nitrogen feeds push soft, sappy new growth that is much more easily damaged by frost. Feeding should stop by late July so that late-season growth has time to harden off before the cold arrives.
  • Fleece protection for new plants and tender species: Horticultural fleece draped over smaller hedges or individual plants on forecast frost nights can prevent serious damage. It is most practical for newly planted specimens in their first winter, when the plants are not yet fully established and are most vulnerable.
  • Site selection: Frost damage is much more common in frost pockets: low-lying areas, enclosed corners and sheltered hollows where cold air pools on still nights. The coastal strip from Sandwich Bay through to Deal is genuinely warmer than Woodnesborough and Eastry even on the same night. If you are planting a tender species, a south or west-facing aspect with some shelter from east winds is worth seeking out.
  • Choose hardier cultivars: Many tender species have hardier named cultivars. Griselinia littoralis is reasonably hardy for a coastal site; some pittosporum cultivars are tougher than others. If you have had repeated frost damage in a particular spot, the simplest long-term fix is to choose a fully hardy alternative, such as privet, laurel or a native mix.
In most Kent winters, even tender hedges come through without incident. It is the occasional sharp outlier: a clear, still night in January when temperatures drop to -7°C or below, that does the real damage. Most of the time, prevention is straightforward. When it does go wrong, patience is the most useful tool.
