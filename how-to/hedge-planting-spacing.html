Hedge planting spacing: how many plants per metre?

Get the spacing wrong and you either have a hedge that takes years longer to close up, or one that becomes bare at the base because the plants compete too hard. Here is the practical guide for the species most commonly grown in Kent gardens.

Updated August 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Spacing a hedge comes down to one judgement: how quickly do you want it to close up, against how much you want to spend now? Plant tight and you get faster cover but more competition between plants. Plant wide and you spend less at the outset but wait longer for a solid hedge and may get gaps at the base that never quite fill.

The figures below come from what we actually plant across gardens in Sandwich, Deal, Worth, Ash, Woodnesborough, Eastry and Sandwich Bay. They work in the chalky, freedraining soils near the coast and in the heavier clay ground inland. Adjust for very different conditions, but these are a reliable starting point.

Nesting season: check before clearing ground

If you are removing an existing hedge or overgrown shrubs to make way for new planting, the UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 applies. It is an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs roughly from 1 March to 31 August. Ground clearance work should be done outside nesting season, or the area should be checked carefully before any vegetation is removed. The safe window is September to February. See our guide to when to trim hedges in Kent.

Single row or double row?

For most garden hedges, a single row is sufficient. One row of plants, evenly spaced, will close into a solid hedge within two to four years for fast-growing species, and within five to eight years for slower-growing ones.

A double row means two parallel lines of plants, staggered so that each plant in the second row sits in the gap between two plants in the first row. Think of a zigzag pattern rather than two parallel lines. The two rows are typically 30-40 cm apart for small species, 40-50 cm for larger ones.

Double rows close up faster and produce a deeper, denser hedge, which is why they are often used for:

  • Native wildlife mixes (hawthorn, blackthorn, field maple, dog rose) where getting quick, dense cover matters for birds and small mammals
  • Tall windbreaks where depth adds structural strength
  • Situations where one species is being phased in to replace another that has died or been removed

For a standard garden boundary hedge that will be clipped annually, a single row in the right species at the right spacing is all you need.

Plants per metre: species guide

Privet (Ligustrum ovalifolium)

5 per metre in a single row (plants 20 cm apart). This is the spacing for a formal clipped hedge that closes up within two to three years. Privet is fast enough that wider spacing (3 per metre) also works, but you will wait an extra year or two for the hedge to feel solid.

Beech (Fagus sylvatica) and hornbeam (Carpinus betulus)

4-5 per metre in a single row (20-25 cm apart). These are the figures for formal hedge use. For a wildlife or informal hedge where some gaps are acceptable, 3 per metre is fine. Beech and hornbeam both establish well from bare-root whips planted November to March. See our guides to beech hedge care and hornbeam hedge care for trimming advice once established.

Yew (Taxus baccata)

3-4 per metre in a single row (25-33 cm apart). Yew grows slowly, and wide spacing means a long wait for a solid hedge. 3 per metre is the minimum for a formal hedge; 4 per metre speeds establishment. Very dense planting (5+ per metre) is not necessary and creates root competition that can slow growth further in the early years.

Cherry laurel (Prunus laurocerasus)

2-3 per metre for a tall privacy screen (33-50 cm apart). Laurel grows large and is better planted with room to expand than crammed tightly. Larger pot-grown specimens (60-80 cm) planted at 2 per metre will give a reasonable screen within two years. Bare-root whips at 3 per metre take a season longer to establish but cost significantly less. See our laurel hedge care guide.

Leylandii (x Cuprocyparis leylandii)

2-3 per metre in a single row (33-50 cm apart). Leylandii grows fast enough that tight planting is rarely needed. Wider spacing gives each plant room to develop lateral branches from the base, which produces a denser hedge at ground level. Very tight planting (5 per metre) tends to produce a hedge that thins at the base as competition for light increases.

Hawthorn (Crataegus monogyna) and blackthorn (Prunus spinosa)

5-6 per metre for a single-row formal or wildlife hedge (17-20 cm apart). These are the classic hedgerow species and are typically planted dense. For a native mix, use 5-6 per metre total across all species combined. A double row at 4 per metre in each row gives a mature hedgerow width that provides excellent wildlife habitat. See our guides to hawthorn hedge care and blackthorn hedge care.

Holly (Ilex aquifolium)

3-4 per metre in a single row. Holly is dense-growing once established but takes time to get going. It is worth giving each plant a little more room so it can develop its own shape before being clipped into line. See our holly hedge care guide.

Kent soil notes

East Kent has two predominant soil types that affect how you plant rather than how many you plant.

Chalk and flint soils (common around Sandwich, Deal and the coast) are free-draining and nutrient-poor. Before planting, dig in generous amounts of organic matter, including garden compost or well-rotted manure. Water newly planted hedges more frequently through the first growing season. Young plants on chalk can go backwards quickly in a dry July. Mulching helps significantly.

Heavier clay soils (more common inland around Worth, Ash, Eastry and the Stour valley) retain moisture and can waterlog in winter. Fork the subsoil before planting to open up drainage channels. Plant slightly proud of the surrounding soil level so the root collar sits above any potential standing water. Clay soils are naturally more fertile, and hedges often establish more confidently in them once past the first winter.

Most of the species in the table above tolerate both soil types with appropriate preparation. Yew and beech both do very well on chalk. Leylandii is more tolerant of heavy clay than its reputation suggests. Privet thrives almost anywhere.

How deep to plant

Bare-root plants should go in at the same depth as they grew in the nursery field. The soil mark on the stem (a change in colour between the above-ground portion and the root) is your guide. The root collar (where stem meets root) should sit at or just below the finished soil level. Planting too deep risks collar rot; planting too shallow leaves the roots exposed to drying out.

For pot-grown plants, match the surface of the compost in the pot to the surrounding soil level. Firm in well and water thoroughly immediately after planting, regardless of the weather. A soak at planting is not optional; it closes air pockets around the roots.

What to do straight after planting

The first 12 months after planting matter more than the spacing decision. The full establishment routine is covered in our guide to caring for a newly planted hedge, but in brief: water weekly through the first spring and summer, mulch along the full length of the hedge to suppress weeds and retain moisture, and do the first formative cut in the following late winter. Getting this routine right closes up gaps and sets the hedge up for the years ahead.
