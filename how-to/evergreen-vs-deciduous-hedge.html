Evergreen vs deciduous hedging: which to choose for your Kent garden?

Year-round screening versus seasonal interest, clipping twice a year versus once: the honest comparison from people who cut both every week.

Updated August 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

It is probably the most common question we get when people are planning a new hedge: should I go evergreen or deciduous? The short answer depends almost entirely on what you want the hedge to do. If you need solid, year-round screening, you want an evergreen. If you want something that earns its keep with wildlife and changes through the seasons, a deciduous hedge is hard to beat.

In practice, many Kent gardens have both, or a mix. But if you are starting from scratch along one boundary, this comparison will help you pick.

The comparison at a glance

Evergreen Deciduous
Winter screening Yes, full cover year-round Reduced (bare stems only)
Seasonal interest Generally low (some exceptions) Blossom, berries, autumn colour
Trimming frequency Typically once or twice a year Usually once a year
Wildlife value Good for shelter and roosting Excellent: berries, blossom, nesting
Establishment speed Varies by species Varies by species
Cost (bare-root) Moderate to high per plant Often lower per plant
Common in East Kent Privet, laurel, yew, leylandii, box, holly Beech, hornbeam, hawthorn, blackthorn, hazel

When to choose an evergreen hedge

Choose an evergreen if your main concern is privacy or noise buffering and you need it to work all year. A rear garden overlooked by neighbouring windows in Sandwich or a front boundary along a busy road in Deal is a strong case for evergreen. The screening does not drop in November and come back in May; it is just there.

Evergreens also work well on boundaries where the hedge is the only barrier between you and passing pedestrians or traffic, because the dense year-round growth provides a physical and visual barrier even in the depths of winter.

Popular evergreen species in East Kent:

  • Privet (Ligustrum ovalifolium). The most forgiving and fastest-growing evergreen hedge in most Kent gardens. Semi-evergreen (it may drop some leaves in hard winters). Needs two cuts a year to stay sharp but grows back strongly.
  • Cherry laurel (Prunus laurocerasus). Large, glossy leaves and fast growth. Good for screening larger gardens in Worth, Ash and inland East Kent. Use secateurs rather than a powered trimmer to avoid shredded leaves.
  • Yew (Taxus baccata). Slow, dense, fine-textured. The best evergreen for formal shapes. Will regenerate from old wood, unlike most conifers. Long-lived; a yew hedge in a Woodnesborough garden might outlast the house.
  • Box (Buxus sempervirens). Classic formal hedging and topiary. Slow-growing, so low-maintenance once established. Keep an eye on box tree moth and box blight.
  • Holly (Ilex aquifolium). Native, spiny and impenetrable. Good for stock-proofing or security boundaries. Slow to establish but very long-lived. Berries for birds in winter.
  • Leylandii. Fast and effective, but requires vigilance. See our leylandii guide for the rules on height and cutting.

When to choose a deciduous hedge

Choose deciduous if you want a hedge that gives something back: blossom in spring, berries in autumn, good nesting habitat in summer. A garden in Eastry or along the Stour valley, where the boundary abuts open countryside or another garden, often benefits from a native deciduous hedge rather than a solid wall of evergreen.

Deciduous hedges also tend to be more wildlife-friendly, cheaper to establish from bare-root whips in winter, and forgiving if you get the pruning timing slightly wrong. Beech and hornbeam are a particularly good choice where you want some winter privacy as well, because they hold their dead leaves (a warm copper-brown) through the cold months if trimmed in late summer.

Popular deciduous species in East Kent:

  • Beech (Fagus sylvatica). Holds dead leaves through winter when trimmed in late summer, giving better privacy than most deciduous species. Suits chalk and well-drained soils, common in the Sandwich and Deal area.
  • Hornbeam (Carpinus betulus). Similar to beech in its leaf-holding habit, but better on heavier clay soils inland (Worth, Ash, Eastry). More tolerant of wet winters.
  • Hawthorn (Crataegus monogyna). Native, thorny, and excellent for wildlife. Blossom in May, berries in autumn. Very tough and easy to establish from bare-root whips. The backbone of many native hedgerows in the East Kent countryside.
  • Blackthorn (Prunus spinosa). Earlier blossom than hawthorn, incredibly dense and spiny. Sloe berries in autumn. Good for exposed or coastal boundaries where it quickly forms an impenetrable barrier.
  • Hazel (Corylus avellana). Fastest of the native deciduous species. Catkins in February, nuts in autumn. Excellent wildlife hedge and good for a relaxed, informal look.
  • Field maple (Acer campestre). Excellent autumn colour. Tough on chalk and clay alike. Good mixing species in a native hedge.

Nesting season applies to both types

Whether your hedge is evergreen or deciduous, the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 protects nesting birds in it from 1 March to 31 August. The safe cutting window for most hedges is September to February. Before any trim, look carefully for active nests. See our wildlife-friendly trimming guide for the full legal position.

Mixing evergreen and deciduous

A mixed native hedge combining hawthorn, blackthorn, field maple, hazel, dog rose and perhaps holly combines the best of both. The structure is deciduous and changes through the seasons; the holly provides some year-round greenery and impenetrability; and the whole thing is rich in flowers, berries and nesting sites. This is the hedge you see running along field boundaries across East Kent, and for good reason.

If you want a formal, clipped appearance, a pure species choice is easier to manage, as different species grow at different rates and a mixed hedge is harder to keep looking sharp. If you are happy with a more informal look, a mixed hedge is excellent.

Trimming: the practical difference

Most evergreen hedges are trimmed twice a year: once in late spring (privet, box, laurel) and once in late summer or early September. Fast-growing species like privet may need three cuts in a good growing year. Slow-growing evergreens like yew typically need only one careful cut in late August or early September.

Deciduous hedges are generally cut once, in late summer or early autumn after nesting season ends. Beech and hornbeam are best cut in late July or August to retain their dead-leaf winter privacy. Hawthorn and blackthorn can be cut between September and February.

This is not a huge difference in overall effort, but it does mean that a fast-growing evergreen hedge requires more regular attention than a well-chosen deciduous one.

Lee Burkhill (Garden Ninja), a UK garden designer with over 100,000 YouTube subscribers, makes the point well in his hedge trimming guide: the key is matching the species to the maintenance effort you are prepared to put in, not choosing the fastest-growing option and hoping for the best.
