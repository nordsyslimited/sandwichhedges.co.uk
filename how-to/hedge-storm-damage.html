Storm damage to hedges: what to check and what to do

After a storm, the right response depends almost entirely on what type of damage you have. Work through these four questions in order and the answer usually becomes clear.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Lee Burkhill (Garden Ninja), BBC Garden Rescue presenter and award-winning garden designer, covers the fundamental cutting and assessment approach for hedges in his video "How to cut & trim hedges: the ultimate guide" (youtube.com/watch?v=_Dc3B2I7bu4) on his Garden Ninja channel. Understanding what healthy cutting looks like is the same starting point for storm damage: you are assessing what the hedge can do and what it needs.

East Kent gets weather from two directions that causes different types of hedge damage. Atlantic lows tracking up the Channel bring strong south-westerlies: these tend to lean and rock hedges on the exposed west-facing side. North Sea storms push from the north-east: less frequent in autumn but sometimes very sharp. In both cases, the damage assessment is the same. Work through the four questions below.

Question 1: Is it safe to approach?

Before you get close to a storm-damaged hedge, look at what is in and around it.

  • Overhead electrical lines. If any cables run through or near the hedge and the hedge has moved or fallen towards them, stay back and call your energy network operator (UK Power Networks for East Kent: 0800 31 63 105). Do not attempt to cut anything near a possible live wire.
  • Hanging, split or bent-over stems under tension. A large stem that is split partway through, or wedged against another and still under load, can spring when cut. Assess from a distance before approaching. If there is any doubt, wait for professional help.
  • Structures under fallen material. If the hedge has collapsed onto a greenhouse, car, or section of fence, treat the whole area as unstable until the weight has been removed from the top.

If any of these apply, stop, make the area safe from a distance if possible, and call us or another qualified contractor before proceeding.

Question 2: What type of damage is it?

This determines whether the hedge can be saved and what work is needed.

Type A: branches broken or stripped, hedge still standing

This is the most common and the most manageable. Wind has broken individual branches or stripped smaller stems from the main framework, but the plants themselves are still rooted and upright. The hedge looks a mess but the root system and main structure are intact.

What to do: remove the broken material cleanly with loppers or a pruning saw. Cut back to a healthy lateral or the main stem, do not leave stubs. Ragged tears can be cleaned up with a sharp pruning saw to encourage clean healing. Then leave it. Most hedges recover from branch loss more quickly than you expect, as the remaining stems push harder growth to compensate. A light feed and mulch in spring helps the recovery along.

Type B: hedge leaning, still rooted

Wind has pushed a section of the hedge over at an angle. The plants are still rooted but are leaning, sometimes significantly. The root plate may have partially lifted on the windward side.

What to do: act quickly, before the roots dry out. Firm the root zone back down by treading around the base, then stake the section upright using stout canes or temporary posts, tied loosely to avoid stem damage. Water well. For most broadleaved hedges, a hedge that was leaned and firmed back within 48 hours of a storm has a good recovery rate. After three to four weeks of settled weather, check whether the hedge has re-anchored itself by gently pressing against it; if it moves freely, it needs longer staking.

Leylandii note: leylandii that have been significantly leaned, especially mature specimens over 1.5 metres tall, are much less likely to re-establish. The shallow root plate common on East Kent clay-over-chalk soils does not anchor leylandii deeply, and a leaned specimen has often torn its main lateral roots. Honest assessment: if a tall leylandii has been pushed over 20 degrees or more, it is usually better removed and replaced rather than attempting to re-stake it.

Type C: sections fully uprooted

Part or all of the hedge has been completely lifted from the ground, root plate and all. The root zone is fully exposed or the plants are lying on the ground.

What to do: if the plants are small to medium-sized (under 1.5 metres) and the root ball is intact, replanting immediately is worth attempting. Dig the planting position as close to the original as the root ball size allows, replace and firm, stake well and water. Do this within a few hours of the storm if at all possible. The longer the roots are exposed, the lower the chance of success.

For larger uprooted specimens, particularly mature leylandii or any plant where the root plate has torn and fractured, removal and replanting is the better outcome. A re-planted young hedge at the right density will produce a better result within three to four years than a struggling uprooted mature plant.

Type D: scorching or leaf burn from salt-laden wind

After severe south-westerlies or any coastal storm in the Sandwich Bay or Deal coastline areas, hedges exposed to the prevailing wind sometimes show brown, crisped foliage on the windward side. This is salt scorch from sea spray carried on the wind, or physical wind dessication, not disease or frost damage.

It looks alarming but is usually much less serious than Type A to C damage. The hedge is alive and the root system is fine. Do nothing immediately. Wait until the following spring: most hedge species push new growth that covers the scorched foliage. If the damage is severe and the species is slow-growing (yew, box), a very light trim in spring to remove the worst of the dead material helps the appearance. Do not cut hard into damaged sections in autumn or winter, as this leaves the hedge even more exposed during recovery.

Question 3: Is it nesting season?

Nesting season applies during storm clean-up too

The Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 protects active bird nests regardless of the reason for the work. Even emergency storm clearance does not give a blanket exemption if a nest is in active use. Nesting season runs from 1 March to 31 August. Outside that window, check as a matter of habit before cutting into any section. Inside the nesting window, check thoroughly before any work that disturbs the interior of the hedge, not just the obvious damaged sections. If you find an active nest, leave that section until the birds have fledged.

In practice, the most significant storms in East Kent tend to arrive in autumn and winter, October through March, which puts most severe storm events outside or at the edge of the main nesting season. A September or October storm is usually clean to deal with, a March storm less straightforwardly so.

Question 4: What do the next few weeks look like?

Storm damage always looks worse immediately after it happens. The best approach in the first 24 to 48 hours is to deal with anything genuinely dangerous (hanging stems, fallen material blocking a path or road) and then step back and look again a week later.

Hedges that look completely destroyed after a storm are sometimes almost fine once the loose material has been removed and the remaining structure assessed properly. Hedges that look merely battered are sometimes more seriously compromised than they appear, particularly if the root zone is affected. A week of observation, and a walk along the hedge checking for new movement after the next rain or wind, gives a much more accurate picture than an immediate assessment in the aftermath of the storm.

Species recovery summary

Species Branch damage Leaning / partial uprooting Full uprooting
Privet Excellent. Regrows quickly from hard cuts. Good. Firm back and stake. Fair for young plants. Remove large ones.
Hawthorn Excellent. Good. Re-roots well if young. Moderate. Worth attempting for young plants.
Beech & hornbeam Good. Clean cuts heal well. Good if acted on quickly. Fair for plants under 2 m. Remove larger ones.
Laurel Good. Hard cuts produce quick new growth. Good. Deep roots help recovery. Moderate. Large root balls re-plant awkwardly.
Leylandii Moderate. Cannot regenerate from brown wood. Poor for mature specimens. Consider removal. Poor. Remove and replant.
Yew Excellent. Recovers from hard pruning. Good. Deep root system helps. Poor for large specimens. Re-plant young ones.
The most common mistake after a storm: cutting too much, too soon. Remove what is genuinely dangerous, then give the hedge a week before deciding anything else.
