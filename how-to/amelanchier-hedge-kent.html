Amelanchier vs elder vs guelder rose: three wildlife hedges compared

All three attract birds and pollinators, all three look good through most of the year, and all three grow happily in East Kent. So which one belongs in your garden boundary?

Updated October 2026 · By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

If you want a hedge that pulls its wildlife weight, three species come up again and again in Kent gardens: amelanchier (Amelanchier lamarckii, also called snowy mespilus or juneberry), elder (Sambucus nigra) and guelder rose (Viburnum opulus). They share several virtues: all flower in spring or early summer, all produce berries that birds eat, and all will grow on East Kent soils without special treatment. The differences are what matters. Growth rate, final size, soil tolerance and the amount of work you put in each year vary considerably between them.

This guide sets them side by side so you can make a straightforward choice, whether you are planting a new hedge in Sandwich or Ash, or deciding which to add to a mixed boundary in Deal or Woodnesborough.

1. Amelanchier: three seasons for the price of one

Amelanchier lamarckii starts the year with a flush of white blossom in late March and April, at a time when very little else is open. Early bumblebees and solitary bees find it immediately. The flowers are small and starry, produced in light clusters, and they last two to three weeks depending on the weather.

By June the berries are ripening: small, dark red to purple fruits that blackbirds, song thrushes and waxwings take quickly. The berries are edible for people too, with a mild apple flavour, but the birds usually get there first. In October the foliage turns orange and red, giving a third flush of colour before leaf drop.

As a hedge plant, amelanchier is moderate in pace (30 to 40 cm a year) and easy to keep at two to three metres with a single light prune once the berries are gone. It tolerates slight chalk, grows well on the greensand soils around Sandwich Bay and manages partial shade without sulking. The root system is not aggressive, which matters if you are planting near paths, walls or shallow utilities.

2. Elder: fast, free and almost too successful

Elder is the plant that turns up in Kent hedgerows and wasteland without invitation. That persistence is a genuine virtue when you need a hedge quickly: it grows 60 to 100 cm in a good year and will take coppicing back to stumps and bounce back within a season. On chalk, clay, damp ground or thin topsoil, it does not much care. It is the most adaptable of the three by a wide margin.

The flowers are flat cream-white heads in June, with the characteristic smell that is either pleasant or deeply unpleasant depending on who you ask. The black berries follow in August and September and are taken by a long list of species. Nesting birds use the dense, twiggy growth readily.

The drawback is the speed. A hedge of elder that goes unpruned for a season quickly becomes a thicket. The root system is vigorous and can find its way under nearby paving. Hard annual cutting is not optional; it is a condition of the arrangement. If you cannot commit to that, elder will gradually win.

3. Guelder rose: the classic native choice

Guelder rose is one of the few native shrubs that gives you white flowers in May to June, vivid red berries from August well into winter, and a decent show of autumn colour in orange and crimson. The berries are glossy and stay on the plant long after the leaves fall, feeding bullfinches, mistle thrushes and redwings into January.

Growth is moderate to vigorous (roughly 40 to 60 cm a year). It clips into a loose informal hedge without complaint, and will take a harder renovation prune if it has been neglected for several years. On damp or slightly heavy ground, it outperforms both amelanchier and elder; it is a strong choice for low-lying gardens near the Kent Levels or the Stour valley around Sandwich.

On very free-draining chalk it can struggle without extra organic matter at planting, though established plants manage well enough. Full sun suits it best, though it tolerates light shade.

4. Which suits your Kent garden?

The short version:

  • Small or medium garden: choose amelanchier. It grows at a pace you can control with one annual prune, its roots are not invasive, and the three-season display earns its place even if wildlife were not a factor.
  • Large boundary where you want maximum wildlife value at minimum cost: elder, but only if you commit to hard annual management. Plant from cuttings (they root easily) and cut it back firmly every winter.
  • Damp or clay ground, especially near a pond or a low-lying area: guelder rose. It thrives where amelanchier would sit and sulk, and the berries that persist into January make it the best of the three for winter feeding.
  • Chalk garden: guelder rose with some compost dug in, or amelanchier on greensand. Elder on chalk is fine without any preparation.

If you have the space, a mixed hedge containing all three (plus blackthorn, field maple or hazel) gives year-round wildlife value and is more resilient than any single species. A 30-metre boundary in Worth or Eastry could contain all three and still read as a coherent hedge rather than a jumble.

5. Pruning and the nesting season

All three of these shrubs flower before or during the nesting season. Amelanchier blossoms in March and April, elder in June, guelder rose in May and June. Pruning immediately after flowering to tidy growth or control size falls squarely inside the nesting season (1 March to 31 August in UK guidance).

Nesting season: Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981. It is an offence to intentionally damage or destroy an active bird's nest. The accepted nesting season is 1 March to 31 August, though late broods can extend into September. Before any hedge cut in this window, check the shrub carefully for active nests. If you find one, leave that section until the nest is empty. The safest windows for amelanchier, elder and guelder rose are late summer (after fruiting, once August is well advanced) or winter (November to February). A light post-fruiting tidy in late August or September is usually possible with care.

In practice, a light late-summer tidy of amelanchier or guelder rose once the berries are gone is low risk, particularly for shrubs in open positions where nests are easy to spot. Elder, if it is being cut hard, is better done in winter. When in doubt, wait until September and check before you cut.
