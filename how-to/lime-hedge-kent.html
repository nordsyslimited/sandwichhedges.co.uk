Lime trees for hedging and pleaching in East Kent

Limes have shaped formal English gardens for centuries. In East Kent, the right species in the right place produces a crisp, long-lived formal screen or a generous informal boundary that is easy to maintain.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Limes are not an obvious first choice for a garden hedge. They are associated with grand avenues and estate gardens, with the stately pleached screens at Hever and the lime walks of manor houses near Sandwich and Deal. But planted thoughtfully, in either the formal pleached style or as a more relaxed clipped boundary, a lime hedge is one of the most rewarding in an East Kent garden: tolerant of hard cutting, long-lived, and attractive to insects in summer.

The question most people ask is not whether to use lime but which lime, and in what form. The answers depend on three practical decisions, which this guide works through in turn.

Decision 1: What do you actually want?

The first question is the most important, and it splits into two very different types of planting.

A formal pleached screen

A pleached screen uses limes grown with a clear single trunk to head height (usually 1.8 to 2.4 metres), then trained outward on horizontal wires or a bamboo frame above that. The result is a raised canopy of woven branches on stilts: architectural, airy, and distinctive in a formal garden near Sandwich or Deal. The limes are planted 1.5 to 2 metres apart and clipped once or twice a year to keep the head tidy and the shape rectangular. Well-established pleached lime screens in East Kent take perhaps three hours a year to maintain.

Pleached screens are excellent for adding height and privacy above a fence line, for framing a view, or for providing a formal boundary without the mass of a traditional hedge. They are common in the more formal walled gardens of the Sandwich and Deal area. If your neighbour's wall or fence already sits at the boundary and you want height above it, pleaching is worth considering. Bear in mind that a pleached screen above an agreed boundary line can still be subject to neighbour discussions about height: see our guide to hedge heights and neighbour law for the relevant rules.

An informal clipped hedge

An informal lime hedge is closer to what most people mean by a garden hedge: plants spaced 60 to 90 cm apart, grown as a continuous mass and clipped once or twice a year. It is softer than a formal pleached screen, more relaxed, and suits a rural boundary in Worth, Ash, Woodnesborough or Eastry rather than a formal town garden. The maintenance is straightforward: a single cut in late summer each year keeps an informal lime hedge tidy.

Decision 2: How much maintenance can you commit to?

Lime hedges and screens are tolerant of a range of maintenance levels, but the amount of cutting you can realistically do affects which species and style suits you best.

Once a year

A single cut between late July and September suits Tilia cordata (small-leaved lime) in either pleached or informal form, and Tilia × europaea (common lime) as an informal hedge. Cutting once a year after the main extension growth has hardened keeps the hedge tidy through the autumn and winter, and lime is dormant enough in winter that the uncut outline holds well until the following summer.

Twice a year

Two cuts a year, one in mid-July and a second in late August or September, produces a crisper outline. This is worth the extra effort on a formal pleached screen where the visual impact depends on clean lines. For an informal hedge, a second light cut is optional, done mainly if the summer has been warm and growth particularly vigorous.

Decision 3: Can you live with summer honeydew?

This is the question lime growers wish they had been asked before they planted. Both Tilia × europaea (common lime) and Tilia platyphyllos (large-leaved lime) are heavily colonised by lime aphids in June and July. Aphid infestations on these species can be spectacular. The insects feed on the sap and excrete honeydew, a sticky liquid that falls like a fine rain on anything below: cars, patio furniture, paths, and people sitting beneath. Honeydew also supports sooty mould, which blackens leaves and surfaces.

If the hedge or screen is away from outdoor seating and parked cars, this may not matter. If it is over a terrace or beside a driveway, it is worth thinking carefully.

Honeydew is not acceptable here

Choose Tilia cordata (small-leaved lime). It is notably less susceptible to lime aphid than the common or large-leaved lime, and the difference in a garden setting is significant. Cordata produces a fine hedge or pleached screen, is native to Britain and grows well on Kent's chalk and loam soils, and its smaller leaf and slower growth rate make it easier to keep at a manageable size than common lime.

Honeydew is not an issue in my situation

Any of the three main species will work. Tilia × europaea (common lime) is the most widely planted, the most familiar, and the easiest to source. It has a long history in formal East Kent gardens and tolerates hard cutting extremely well. Tilia platyphyllos (large-leaved lime) is faster-growing and best suited to larger spaces: it is used for the pleached avenues at some of the grander Kent gardens near Hever and in the Weald. It is less suitable for a modest town garden where the vigour needs managing.

Nesting season: check before you cut

Under the UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs roughly from 1 March to 31 August. Lime trees and hedges provide good cover for nesting birds, particularly in the dense growth of an established informal lime hedge. The safe cutting window is September to February for major renovation work. The main annual trim for limes (late July to September) falls in the tail of the nesting season: check for occupied nests before cutting any section between March and the end of August.

Before you start: kit and safety

Cutting a pleached lime screen at height introduces specific hazards.

  • Eye and face protection: mandatory. Clippings from above travel downward.
  • Gloves: sturdy work gloves; lime stems can be surprisingly scratchy when dry.
  • Pole trimmer preferred: for pleached screens, a long-reach pole trimmer keeps you on the ground. Only use a ladder where the pole trimmer cannot reach, and have someone footing it.
  • RCD adaptor for electric tools: plug it in at the socket, not at the extension lead.
  • Ear defenders if using a petrol trimmer.
  • Check for wasp nests in the internal framework before cutting: lime screens provide good overwintering shelter for wasps late in the season.

Species profiles at a glance

Tilia cordata: small-leaved lime

Native to Britain, well-suited to Kent's chalk and loam soils, and less prone to aphid than the common lime. Smaller leaves, slower growth, and a naturally tighter habit make it the most manageable choice for a formal pleached screen in a typical East Kent garden. Cuts cleanly and holds its shape through winter. Suits both Sandwich's more formal walled gardens and rural boundaries in Worth and Ash.

Tilia × europaea: common lime

The workhorse of the British formal garden tradition and the lime most often found in older estate plantings near Sandwich and Deal. Very vigorous, tolerant of hard cutting and historically used for pollarding. The aphid problem is real and significant from June to July. Best suited to an informal hedge or a pleached avenue in a larger garden where the honeydew fallout can be avoided. Cuts made into old wood in winter regenerate freely.

Tilia platyphyllos: large-leaved lime

Faster-growing and larger-leaved than the other two, used for formal pleached avenues at scale. Suits a large property where the vigour can be used rather than fought. Kent's mild coastal winters, particularly around Sandwich Bay, mean even platyphyllos is not at meaningful frost risk. For most garden hedges, cordata or europaea is a more practical scale.

Trimming timing for limes in Kent

The timing for lime trimming is more specific than for most hedging plants, because lime leaves go from soft and easy to cut to tough and resistant over the course of a few weeks in mid-summer.

For a formal pleached screen, the main cut is in mid to late July: once the new season's extension growth has finished and the leaves have expanded fully, but before they toughen and start to brown at the edges. A second light trim in late August or September tightens the outline before winter and is worth doing on a screen where the visual lines matter.

For an informal hedge, a single cut between late July and September is usually sufficient. The nesting season caution above still applies: check before cutting anything in July or early August.

Avoid cutting limes in late autumn or early winter. Unlike beech or hornbeam, which are best cut in late summer and early autumn, lime is better left until the following July once the summer window has passed. Cuts made into lime in October or November do not callus over well before winter and can leave the hedge looking ragged into spring.
