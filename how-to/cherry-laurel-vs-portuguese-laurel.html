Cherry laurel vs Portuguese laurel: choosing between them

Both are popular hedging plants in East Kent. Both are evergreen. Both are easy to grow. But they suit different gardens and different situations. A side-by-side comparison so you can make the right choice before you dig a single hole.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Cherry laurel (Prunus laurocerasus) and Portuguese laurel (Prunus lusitanica) are not the same plant, though many people treat them interchangeably. The names add to the confusion: neither is a true laurel (the culinary bay laurel, Laurus nobilis, is a different genus entirely). Both are in the Prunus family, both are evergreen, and both form reliable screens in Kent gardens.

The differences matter, though. In Sandwich, Deal, Worth and the surrounding villages, we cut a lot of both species, and the right choice genuinely depends on what you are asking the hedge to do.

Check for nests before trimming either species

Both laurels are popular nesting cover. Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs roughly 1 March to 31 August. The safe cutting window for laurel hedges is September to February. If you are trimming between March and August, check the hedge carefully for active nests first.

When cherry laurel is the better choice

Speed matters above everything else. Cherry laurel is one of the fastest-growing hedging plants suitable for Kent conditions. If you need to screen a new boundary, block an eyesore or restore privacy quickly, cherry laurel will give you a working hedge in two to three years from planting. Portuguese laurel will take three to five years to reach a comparable height.

You have a shaded boundary. Cherry laurel thrives in shade, including the dense shade of north-facing boundaries between houses and the shadow cast by taller walls or fences. It is one of the most reliable evergreen hedges for difficult, shaded positions.

You need height and bulk. For a substantial screen of 2m or more, cherry laurel fills in quickly and densely. In gardens around Sandwich Bay where coastal exposure and height are both required, cherry laurel's vigour is an advantage.

The budget is tight. Cherry laurel plants are consistently cheaper per unit than Portuguese laurel, and faster growth means fewer plants needed to close the gap in the first few years.

When Portuguese laurel is the better choice

You want a neater, lower-maintenance hedge. Slower growth means fewer cuts. Portuguese laurel also suits hedge shears and a powered trimmer well, producing a clean finish on its smaller leaves that cherry laurel simply does not match with the same tools. For a formal, well-defined hedge that does not need cutting twice a year, Portuguese laurel is the easier long-term option.

Your soil is chalky or alkaline. On the chalk downs above Eastry, parts of Ash, and the downland south of Sandwich, cherry laurel can show chlorosis, yellowing of the leaves from iron deficiency in shallow chalk soils. Portuguese laurel handles alkaline conditions substantially better and will not need chelated iron treatments to stay looking healthy.

You want better wildlife value. Portuguese laurel's flowers attract pollinators more readily (they are longer, more prominent and produced in summer when other garden food sources are at their peak), and the berries are taken by blackbirds, song thrushes and other birds. Both species provide nesting cover, but Portuguese laurel's thinner, more vertical growth often provides slightly better winter food value through the berries.

You have deer pressure. The rural edges of Worth, Woodnesborough and the farmland around Eastry all see roe deer browsing. Deer will take cherry laurel very readily in the first years after planting; Portuguese laurel is significantly less palatable to them.

You prefer the appearance. This is subjective, but Portuguese laurel is widely regarded as the more ornamental of the two. The smaller leaf, the deep green with the contrasting reddish midrib, and the more upright branching habit give it a refinement that cherry laurel's large, blunt-ended leaves lack. In Sandwich's historic town gardens and the more formal gardens in Deal, Portuguese laurel tends to look more at home.

Where they overlap

For most practical purposes in a typical East Kent garden, either will do the job. Both are reliably evergreen through Kent winters (the mildest in mainland England, rarely sustaining temperatures below -8°C for more than a night or two), both establish quickly from container-grown plants, and both respond well to cutting back hard if they have outgrown their space.

One thing they share: both are toxic if leaves are ingested in quantity by humans or animals. This is worth knowing if you have dogs, horses or young children who might chew on hedge cuttings. It is not a reason to avoid either species, but it does mean the clippings should be composted with care and not left within reach of livestock.

Mixing the two

There is no reason you cannot use both in the same garden. A cherry laurel hedge on a shaded north boundary, with Portuguese laurel on a sunnier south or east boundary, is entirely sensible. The contrast in leaf size and texture is noticeable but not jarring, particularly if both are trimmed to a consistent profile. We see this combination regularly in gardens around Woodnesborough and Worth where the house orientation creates very different conditions on different sides.

If we are pressed for a single recommendation for a typical East Kent garden: choose cherry laurel if you need quick results and will not mind cutting twice a year. Choose Portuguese laurel if you are planting for the long term and would rather cut once.
