Viburnum tinus hedge care

Laurustinus is one of the few hedging plants that flowers all winter. Here is how to prune it, when to leave it alone, and why it earns its keep in East Kent gardens.

Updated September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Viburnum tinus, commonly called laurustinus, is the evergreen hedge plant that does something almost nothing else does: it flowers in January. While the rest of the garden is bare and dormant, a viburnum tinus hedge carries clusters of white blossoms opening from deep pink buds, with a faint almond scent on a still day.

We cut a fair number of them across Sandwich, Deal and the villages of East Kent. They are genuinely low maintenance, they tolerate the coast, they feed wildlife through both the flower and berry cycles, and they take hard pruning without complaint. Here is what to do and when.

Annual care: the seasonal pattern

November to March: leave it alone and enjoy the flowers

Viburnum tinus flowers from around November through to March, peaking in January and February. Do not touch it during this period. This is not pruning time, it is display time. The flowers are the whole point of this plant through the grim months.

April to May: the annual trim

Once the last flowers have faded, usually by the end of April in East Kent, this is your window. Cut back the longest shoots to keep the shape tidy, reducing overall growth by a third. You can be quite firm with it. Unlike many flowering shrubs, viburnum tinus responds well to a proper cut rather than a timid snip.

If you are keeping it as a formal hedge, run a line and trim all faces. If it is more of a loose, informal screen, a pair of loppers on the longest whippy growths is usually enough. Either way, finish by late May so the new growth has a full season to settle before winter.

Nesting season: 1 March to 31 August

Viburnum tinus is particularly popular with nesting birds because of its dense, year-round cover. It is an offence under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 to damage or destroy an active nest. Always check for birds before you trim, even outside the main nesting window. In practice, a quick look from underneath before you start takes thirty seconds and keeps you on the right side of the law.

Late August: optional light tidy

If the hedge has put on a lot of soft summer growth and looks shaggy, a light trim in late August does no harm. Do not take it back hard at this point, just neaten the outline. Viburnum tinus will set its flower buds from September onwards, so a late cut that goes too deep into new wood can reduce the winter display.

September and October: leave the berries

After the flowers come small, oval berries: first red-tinged, then turning to a striking metallic blue-black. These ripen through late summer and are taken by blackbirds, thrushes and starlings through the autumn. If you want the birds in your garden, leave the berries. If you need a tidy edge for a particular reason, a light surface trim is fine but try to avoid cutting off entire berry-laden shoots.

Before you start: a word on tools and safety

Tools and safety

  • Sharp bypass secateurs or loppers for annual tidying on smaller hedges.
  • Electric or battery hedge trimmer for a long formal hedge.
  • Wear gloves, safety glasses and sturdy footwear whenever using powered tools.
  • Do not work from a ladder alone. Use a solid platform and have someone on the ground if the hedge is tall.
  • Viburnum tinus sap can irritate skin. Wash hands after handling cut stems.

Hard renovation: rescuing an overgrown viburnum tinus hedge

This is where viburnum tinus really distinguishes itself from most evergreen hedges. You can cut it back to within 30 cm of the ground in April or May and it will regrow. Not every plant does this. Laurel does, yew does, photinia does. Most conifers do not. Viburnum tinus does, and does so vigorously.

If you have inherited a viburnum tinus that has got out of hand or is completely shapeless, hard pruning is a realistic option rather than a last resort. Reduce one side in year one, the other side a year later if you want to keep some screen while it recovers. Or cut the whole thing back at once if you have other privacy in place. Feed after a hard cut: a balanced granular fertiliser worked into the soil around the base speeds the recovery.

Planting a viburnum tinus hedge

Plant at 50 to 60 cm spacing for a single-row hedge. Pot-grown plants can go in any time, but autumn and early spring are the best times in East Kent because the soil is moist enough to help establishment. Bare-root viburnum tinus is available from late autumn and is considerably cheaper. Space at the same 50 to 60 cm interval.

Choose a position in sun to partial shade. Viburnum tinus will grow in full shade but flowers more freely with at least half a day of sun. It tolerates most soils including clay and chalk, which covers a lot of East Kent gardens.

Viburnum tinus in coastal Kent

We have cut viburnum tinus hedges metres from the sea at Sandwich Bay and along the Deal front. It handles salt spray and the persistent coastal wind that flattens more tender evergreens. In a very exposed position, plant a temporary windbreak of hawthorn or blackthorn on the weather side while the viburnum establishes, then remove the nurse shrubs after two or three years. After that, the viburnum stands perfectly well on its own.

The mild winters along the East Kent coast suit it well. It flowers more freely when temperatures stay above about minus five degrees Celsius through the winter, which is the norm from Sandwich down to Folkestone.

Wildlife value

Viburnum tinus sits near the top of the list for a garden hedge that actually does something for wildlife:

  • Winter flowers provide nectar for queen bumblebees emerging on mild days in February and March, before much else is in flower.
  • Dense evergreen cover makes it a favoured nesting site for blackbirds, robins and dunnocks. Expect to find nests in it if you have a mature hedge.
  • Berries are eaten by blackbirds, thrushes and fieldfares in autumn, adding to the hawthorn and holly berry crop of a Kent garden.

Common problems

Viburnum beetle

The main pest is the viburnum beetle, whose larvae notch the leaf edges from April to June, and whose adult beetles do the same in July and August. It looks alarming but rarely does lasting harm to a well-established hedge. Remove larvae by hand or with a forceful jet of water. Insecticide is rarely necessary unless an attack is severe and repeated.

Downy mildew

Grey-white powdery patches on the undersides of leaves, usually in humid conditions. Improve airflow by thinning the inside of the hedge with loppers. Remove badly affected material. Most established hedges shrug it off.

Poor or no flowering

Almost always caused by cutting too late in the year: October or November pruning removes the developing flower buds. Keep all main cuts to April and May, and any late tidy to late August at the latest.
