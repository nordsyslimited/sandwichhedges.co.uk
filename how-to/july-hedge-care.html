July hedge care in Kent: last watch before the main window

July is the last full month of the nesting season. The main cutting window is almost here. This is what to do with your hedges in the meantime, what to leave alone, and how to set yourself up for a clean August start.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Every July, the same thing happens. Hedges are visibly getting away from you. Privet has gone leggy. Box is starting to look ragged. Leylandii has put on another flush of new growth since the May tidy. The temptation to get on with it is real.

Hold on a little longer. The nesting season runs to 31 August and July is still well within it. Second and third broods are common across East Kent in July, particularly from blackbirds, house sparrows and pigeons. But there are things you can safely do this month if you approach them properly.

July is still nesting season

Under the UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, damaging or destroying an active bird's nest is a criminal offence, regardless of species or hedge type. The nesting season runs roughly 1 March to 31 August. In July, late nesters and second-brood birds are actively using hedges across East Kent. The safe main cutting window for most hedges opens in late August. Every section of hedge you plan to touch in July must be observed and confirmed empty first.

Privet: the July check

Privet is the most widely planted hedge in domestic gardens around Sandwich, Deal, Worth and the villages, and it is the species most likely to benefit from a July tidy. A light trim in early to mid-July, applied to a confirmed-empty hedge, keeps the privet compact and prevents the shape deteriorating ahead of the September main cut.

The key word is "confirmed." Stand back from the hedge and watch it for two to three minutes. Look from both sides and from underneath. If you see birds repeatedly flying in and out of the same section, leave that section alone: a second brood is likely in progress. In a normal East Kent summer, many first-brood privet nesters have fledged by early July, but late starters and second broods mean the hedge needs checking on the day, every time.

For the trim: a light pass with a trimmer or shears, taking off this season's new growth. You are not changing the shape or the height. That is September's job. Taking off the tips and any long wayward shoots is enough. If you did a June tidy, this July trim is a follow-on, not a full cut.

Box: second cut timing

If you gave box hedging its first cut in May or June, late July is when many gardeners do a second tidy. Box grows slowly relative to privet: one or two careful cuts a year is the standard, and the second cut in late July or early August keeps topiary and formal box edges crisp through the autumn.

The caution with box in July is moisture. Do not trim box in wet weather: the shears or trimmer can spread box blight spores across the whole plant. Wipe blades with a weak disinfectant solution between plants, particularly if any section is showing yellowing or die-back. A dry, overcast day in late July is ideal: shady enough to work comfortably, dry enough to be safe.

Leylandii and conifers in July

The window for conifer trimming runs from May through to September, so July is within it. If your leylandii has put on a second flush of new growth since a May or June tidy, a light pass in July is fine. Avoid cutting back hard or into old brown wood: conifers do not regenerate from old growth the way deciduous hedges do. Our conifer trimming guide covers the detail on how far back you can safely cut.

If the leylandii is a mature, very tall hedge along the boundary, check our leylandii guide before you cut. Height rules and the considerations around cutting back large conifers are different from a routine garden tidy.

What to leave alone in July

Yew, beech, hornbeam, hawthorn, blackthorn, laurel, holly and most dense broadleaf hedges should be left until late August. These species provide reliable nesting cover and are actively used in July. Cutting them gains nothing on the trimming front, since their annual cut is best done once in late summer anyway, and the risk of disturbing nests is high.

The mild coastal climate of East Kent, with warm July temperatures and relatively little frost even in recent winters, means these hedges are in strong growth in July. They will have one last growth spurt from now until September. Cut them in late August or early September and the new growth heals in properly before the cold arrives. Cut them now, in July, and you may get a second flush of soft growth before winter that is more vulnerable to frost.

Before you start: safety

  • Check for nests: observe the hedge for two to three minutes before touching it. Check from both sides and from underneath. Look for birds entering the same spot repeatedly.
  • Eye protection: safety glasses or goggles. Flying debris from a trimmer can cause serious eye injury.
  • Ear defenders: petrol and electric trimmers both exceed safe noise levels quickly. Use them.
  • Gloves: cut-resistant and close-fitting. July hedges can hide wasps, brambles and thorns under new growth.
  • Check for wasp nests: July is peak wasp nesting season. A large wasp nest in the hedge base can appear with very little warning. If you see one, leave that section alone.
  • RCD adaptor: for any corded electric trimmer. Garden ground can be damp even in dry periods.
  • Pole trimmer over a ladder: if the hedge is above comfortable reach, a long-reach pole trimmer is safer. See our guide on using a long-reach trimmer. If a ladder is unavoidable, have someone footing it and do not overreach sideways.

Preparing for the main August cut

For the hedges you cannot touch in July, use the month to prepare. Walk the hedge and note which sections have grown the most, whether there are any dead or diseased areas to clear before the main cut, and whether any tools need sharpening or servicing. Sharp blades make the August and September cuts significantly cleaner and faster.

If you are booking a professional hedge cut for September or October, July is the time to do it. Teams in Sandwich, Deal, Worth, Ash, Woodnesborough, Eastry and Sandwich Bay fill their September books faster than any other month. Leaving it until August to book typically means a wait into October.

The horticulturists Simon and Lorna at Walking Talking Gardeners, who hold RHS medals and degrees in horticulture from the University of Greenwich, put it well in their July pruning video: the plants you can safely tackle now need action before the growth gets away from you, but patience pays off on the ones that are worth leaving. Their video Stop! Cut These July Plants Back NOW before its too late!! is a useful general reference for July garden tasks in the UK.

Watering in July

East Kent's coastal climate has a drying east wind that can strip moisture from soils faster than rainfall replaces it in a warm July. Hedges planted in the last two years need watering during any dry spell lasting more than ten days: water deeply and slowly at the base, once or twice a week, rather than a light splash every day. Established hedges on most Kent soils do not need irrigation, but the chalk and sandy soils around Sandwich Bay and towards the coastal strip drain fast and young hedges are more vulnerable than you might expect.

Established hedges are worth inspecting for early signs of drought stress in July: browning leaf tips, slight wilting in the middle of the day, and a dusty look to privet leaves. These are not emergencies in July, but a deep watering and mulch refresh will set the hedge up better for the main cut in September.

July summary by species

Privet

Light tidy, confirmed empty. Main cut September.

Box

Second cut in dry weather, late July. Disinfect tools. Watch for blight.

Leylandii and conifers

Light tidy within the green. No hard cutting back.

Photinia and lonicera nitida

A light trim is possible on confirmed-empty plants. Main shape cut September.

Yew, beech, hornbeam, holly, hawthorn, laurel, blackthorn

Leave completely. Their cut is late August onwards.
