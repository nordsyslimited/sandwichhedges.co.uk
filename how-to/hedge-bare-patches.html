Bare patches in your hedge: why they happen and how to fix them

The wrong diagnosis leads to the wrong fix. Work through these four questions first, then apply the right remedy.

Updated September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Bare patches are one of the most common hedge complaints we encounter across East Kent gardens, and they get misdiagnosed more than almost any other hedge problem. People assume disease when it is usually profile, or they water and feed when the real problem is that the top of the hedge has been cut wider than the base for twenty years.

Before doing anything, work through these four questions in order. Each one leads to a different fix.

Before any cutting work: safety first

If any of the fixes below involve cutting, follow the standard safety precautions: wear eye protection (flying debris is the most common hedge-cutting injury), ear defenders for powered tools, sturdy gloves, and use an RCD adaptor with any corded electric trimmer. Check for wasp nests before cutting, particularly in late summer and autumn when wasps are at their most aggressive. For tall hedges, use a pole or long-reach trimmer from the ground in preference to a ladder.

The UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 protects active bird nests: the main nesting season runs 1 March to 31 August. Any cutting work involving bare-patch renovation should be done outside those months, or after a careful check that the hedge section is empty.

The four-question decision path

Question 1: Is the bare area concentrated at the base of the hedge?

Yes, bare mainly at the base. This is nearly always a profile problem. If a hedge is cut with the top wider than the base, the upper growth shades the lower branches over time. Starved of light, the lower foliage gradually thins and dies back, leaving the familiar "bare legs" below a full canopy.

Check the profile: look at the hedge from the end. If the sides lean outward as they go up (like a trapezoid, wider at the top), that is the cause. The fix is to retrain the profile to an A-frame shape, where the base is slightly wider than the top. This is not a quick job: take no more than 10 to 15 cm off the sides in a single cut. You may need two to three seasons of corrective cutting before the base recovers. Start in late summer or early autumn (outside the nesting season) on most species.

For more on profile cutting technique, see our guide to the right hedge cutting profile.

Yes, but the hedge is a conifer (leylandii, lawson cypress, thuja). The same profile principle applies, but for conifers the situation is worse: conifers will not regenerate from bare brown wood. If the lower branches have already died back, no amount of profile correction will bring them back. You are managing the problem, not reversing it. The options are to live with it, plant a screening plant in front of the bare section, or replant the hedge entirely.

No, the bare patch is not at the base. Go to Question 2.

Question 2: Is the bare area in a specific section or patch, not all the way along?

Yes, a discrete patch. This suggests a localised cause: disease, root damage, pest damage or physical injury (a vehicle, a bonfire, something resting against the hedge). The approach is to investigate that specific section.

Scratch the bark of a stem in the bare patch with your thumbnail. If the layer underneath is green or white, the stem is alive but not growing. If it is brown or dry throughout, the stem is dead. Dead material in a discrete patch suggests a disease or root event. Common causes in East Kent gardens:

  • Honey fungus: spreads through the soil from a nearby stump or infected tree. Look for bootlace-like black strands (rhizomorphs) in the soil around the base of affected stems. Often affects large patches suddenly. See our honey fungus guide for treatment options.
  • Phytophthora root rot: common in poorly drained soils, particularly on laurel and some conifers. Yellowing followed by sudden collapse. The soil around the base often smells faintly sour. Improve drainage before replanting.
  • Box blight: if the hedge is box, brown or stripped stems with white fungal patches are characteristic. See our box blight treatment guide.
  • Storm damage or physical injury: a branch torn, a vehicle reversing into the hedge, or a large bonfire against the stems. Usually obvious from the physical damage visible on closer inspection.

No, the sparse area runs broadly along the hedge. Go to Question 3.

Question 3: Is the whole hedge looking thinner or see-through compared with how it used to be?

Yes, the whole hedge seems sparse. This is usually a feeding or soil issue, or it follows a period of severe drought. Hedges in the free-draining chalk and sandy soils of East Kent around Sandwich, Deal and Eastry are particularly prone to drought stress in a dry summer: roots cannot access deep water reserves, and the hedge thins through the season rather than dying outright.

The fix is a combination of:

  • Deep watering: water slowly and deeply at the base (not a quick surface spray), particularly for any hedge planted in the last three years.
  • Mulch: a 5 to 7 cm layer of bark chip or garden compost along the hedge base, kept clear of the stems, dramatically reduces water loss from the root zone and keeps the soil temperature stable. Particularly important in the sandy Sandwich Bay and Deal coastal strip.
  • Spring feeding: a balanced slow-release granular fertiliser in early spring encourages vigorous new growth through the season. Do not feed in late summer or autumn: this pushes soft new growth into cold weather. See our hedge feeding guide.
  • Formative pruning: if the hedge has never been cut back hard, it may simply have developed a sparse outer shell with little inner density. Hard renovation cuts in winter on deciduous species (or carefully timed renovation on broadleaf evergreens) stimulate dense basal regrowth. See our formative hedge pruning guide.

No, the hedge has a specific dead zone with brown or blackened material. Go to Question 4.

Question 4: Has the hedge recently been cut very hard, or has it had a hard winter or drought event?

Yes, the brown section was cut hard recently. If the hedge was cut back into older wood during renovation, or if a conifer was inadvertently cut into brown wood, the bare area may simply be the consequence of that cut. For broadleaf hedges and deciduous species, wait: most will produce new growth from old wood given time (one to three growing seasons). Feed in spring, water in dry spells, and be patient.

For conifers, the answer is different. Leylandii, lawson cypress and thuja will not regrow from brown wood. The bare section is permanent. Fill the gap with a climbing plant (pyracantha, ivy, or a trained climbing hydrangea), plant a replacement section, or accept the bare area and manage the neighbouring growth to partially screen it.

No, the brown zone appeared after an extreme weather event (hard frost, drought, flooding). Frost can kill tender evergreen shoots and, in severe cases, whole sections of less-hardy species. Flooding kills roots through oxygen deprivation. Assess whether the stems in the affected section are alive (scratch test: green or white under the bark). If alive but leafless, feed and water and wait for the following spring. If dead, the section needs replacing.

When it is not DIY territory

If the bare patches are spreading steadily despite corrective work, if there is a persistent foul smell from the soil at the base, or if the hedge is a boundary feature of significant age and value, get a professional assessment before committing to full renovation or replanting. Some problems (honey fungus in the soil, deep compaction from adjacent hard landscaping) cannot be fixed by pruning and feeding alone, and the sooner you know that, the less money you spend on the wrong fix.
