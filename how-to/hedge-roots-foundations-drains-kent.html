Hedge roots near houses, foundations and drains

Most hedge roots are harmless. A few species in particular conditions can cause real problems. This guide walks you through the questions that actually matter: which species, which soil, how close, and what your options are if it is your neighbour's hedge.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Hedge roots come up in two kinds of conversation. The first is from someone about to plant a new hedge and wondering what to avoid near the house. The second is from someone who already has a problem: a drain blocked for the third time, a crack appearing in a wall, or a neighbour's boundary privet that has been running into the garden for years.

The answer in both cases depends on three things: the hedge species, the soil type, and the proximity to the structure. Here is how to work through it.

The decision tree: is your hedge a problem?

Work through these questions in order

  1. Is the hedge within 5 metres of a building or drain? If no, root risk is very low for most species. If yes, go to question 2.
  2. Is your soil clay? Clay soils in Kent are mostly found in the Weald and in low-lying areas: heavier, sticky soils that shrink significantly when dry. The chalk, flint and sandy loam soils across most of East Kent around Sandwich, Deal, Worth and the coastal strip are much less prone to shrinkage. If you are on clay, root risk to foundations is higher. If on chalk or sandy soil, risk is lower but drain risk remains.
  3. Which species is the hedge? See the species table below. High water demand species (leylandii, privet, laurel) carry more risk than low water demand species (yew, beech, hornbeam, box).
  4. Are there existing signs of movement or blockage? Cracks following a pattern consistent with differential settlement, or recurring drain blockages without another obvious cause, warrant investigation regardless of other factors.

How far do hedge roots actually spread?

Hedge roots spread further than most people expect. The widely cited rule of thumb is that roots extend laterally to roughly the same distance as the hedge is tall. For a two-metre privet hedge, that suggests roots reaching two metres sideways from the base. For a four-metre leylandii, four metres or more. In practice, high water demand species with fast root systems can exceed this.

Roots do not spread uniformly: they follow moisture and oxygen, so they concentrate near the surface (in the top 600 mm of soil for most species) and travel towards any source of water. Drains are a particular attraction. A small crack in an older clay drain pipe provides both water and a route in.

Species by risk level

Higher risk near buildings and drains

Leylandii: the highest water demand of any common UK garden hedge. Roots spread aggressively and extensively: 10 metres or more is not unusual for a mature hedge. On clay soils, the NHBC and structural surveyors typically recommend a minimum of 12 to 15 metres between leylandii and buildings. On chalk and sandy soils, 8 to 10 metres is the more practical guidance. Our leylandii guide covers the full position on leylandii and neighbour disputes.

Privet: roots extend 3 to 5 metres in most soils, concentrating near the surface. Commonly found entering older clay drain systems in East Kent properties where it has been established for twenty or more years. A 5-metre clearance from buildings on clay is sensible; 3 metres minimum from any drain, with the joint condition of the drain being the bigger factor.

Cherry laurel: moderate to high water demand. Root spread comparable to privet. Relevant if planted close to buildings in Sandwich or Deal where gardens are compact. A 4 to 5 metre clearance from buildings is reasonable.

Moderate risk

Hawthorn and blackthorn: vigorous root systems that spread broadly, but generally not associated with foundation damage in the UK literature. More of a concern for drains at close proximity. Common in native hedgerows across the Sandwich Bay area and Worth.

Hornbeam and beech: deeper roots than privet or leylandii, which reduces the surface spread risk. Not usually problematic near buildings unless on clay within 3 metres.

Lower risk

Yew: characteristically deep-rooting rather than spreading. Not typically associated with foundation problems or drain blockages in domestic garden settings. One of the better choices for planting within 3 to 4 metres of a building on most Kent soils.

Box: shallow, slow-spreading roots. Not considered a risk to foundations or drains except in unusually close proximity (within 1 metre).

Beech: moderate risk, lower than privet or leylandii. A 3 to 4 metre clearance is sensible on clay soils; lower risk on chalk.

How hedge roots affect foundations

The mechanism is mostly indirect, and largely confined to clay soils. Hedge roots extract moisture from clay soil, causing the clay to shrink. If the moisture extraction is uneven (one part of a building has a hedge nearby, the rest does not), the ground beneath the foundation shrinks differentially. The foundation tilts slightly, and the building cracks.

This is clay shrinkage and it is distinct from direct root penetration of foundations, which is rare. Sound concrete foundations are not pushed apart by hedge roots in normal circumstances. The risk is greatest in the long dry summers that East Kent experiences more frequently now than twenty years ago: the mild coastal climate that extends the cutting season also means the soil can go through sustained dry periods where roots are drawing hard on clay moisture reserves.

On the chalk, flint and sandy soils across most of the Sandwich, Deal, Worth and Eastry area, shrinkage risk is much lower. The soils are free-draining and do not hold moisture in the same way. A privet hedge within 3 metres of a house on chalk soil is unlikely to cause foundation problems. The same hedge on heavy clay in the Weald of Kent carries a genuine risk.

Hedges and drains

Root ingress into drains happens when roots encounter a crack, a loose joint or a root-entry point and grow through it, attracted by the water. Once inside a pipe, roots can grow to block it completely over several years. Older clay-jointed drainage systems (common in pre-1970s properties throughout East Kent) are more vulnerable than modern plastic push-fit systems.

Signs of root ingress: recurring slow drainage or blockages in a section of drain that serves a specific part of the property, particularly after dry summers when roots have spread further in search of water. A CCTV drain survey (available from drain specialists operating in Sandwich, Deal and the surrounding area) will confirm root ingress definitively and show which drain sections are affected.

Treatment: root mass can be cleared by high-pressure water jetting, but if the drain is cracked, the crack needs repair (relining or open excavation) or the roots will return. Cutting back the hedge aboveground does not prevent root growth in the short term: the root system persists for years.

If it is your neighbour's hedge

Under common law, you have the right of abatement: you can cut back any roots (or branches) that cross your boundary, up to the boundary line, without needing your neighbour's consent. You must not, in doing so, kill the hedge. You should offer the cut roots and stems back to the hedge owner, who owns them even once cut.

If your neighbour's hedge roots are causing damage to your property, such as foundation movement or drain blockages, you may have a claim in tort for private nuisance. This requires evidence of the root source, the damage, and causation. It is a civil matter, and professional legal advice is needed before pursuing it. Start with a documented, polite conversation with your neighbour: the majority of cases like this resolve without legal action. See our guide to hedge heights and neighbour disputes for the formal routes available to you, including the high hedge complaint process.

The Royal Horticultural Society's guidance on selecting appropriate hedge species for different garden situations, including proximity to structures, is worth consulting when you are choosing what to plant. Their video on best hedge care practice, What is the best way to cut a hedge?, features their horticulturist explaining maintenance principles from the RHS perspective. The RHS holds 104,000 subscribers on YouTube and is the UK's leading horticultural charity.

Planting a new hedge near a building: guidance summary

Minimum planting distances from buildings (Kent soils)

  • Leylandii: 10 metres (chalk/sandy soil) to 15 metres (clay)
  • Privet: 3 metres (chalk/sandy) to 5 metres (clay)
  • Laurel: 4 metres (chalk/sandy) to 6 metres (clay)
  • Hornbeam / beech: 3 metres (chalk/sandy) to 4 metres (clay)
  • Yew / box: 2 metres on most East Kent soils
  • All species from drains: minimum 2 metres; 3 metres for privet and leylandii on older drainage systems

These are practical guidelines, not legal requirements. Structural or subsidence concerns warrant a qualified structural engineer's assessment. This guide is not legal or structural advice.
