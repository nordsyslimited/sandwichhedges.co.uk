Training a cone or pyramid topiary

Two shapes that look similar from a distance but cut very differently. Here is how they compare in practice, which species give the cleanest results in East Kent gardens, and the trimming sequence that keeps the point sharp.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Cone and pyramid are the two shapes that appear most often in the formal gardens of East Kent, from the clipped yew sentinel cones flanking a gate in Sandwich to the box pyramids on either side of a front door in Deal. They look architectural, they give the garden structure through winter when everything else has died back, and once the shape is established they are not especially difficult to maintain.

The difficulty is getting started. The first few cuts define everything that follows, so it is worth understanding how the two shapes differ before picking up the shears.

Check for nesting birds before any cut between March and August

Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is a criminal offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Dense topiary shrubs are nesting sites for wrens, dunnocks and robins. The safe cutting window for topiary and shaped hedges is September to February. If you must trim in spring or summer, examine the plant carefully from underneath before starting, and leave it alone if there are birds flying in and out of the same spot.

Before you start

Wear eye protection: loose clippings at face height are the main hazard with topiary work. Ear defenders if using a petrol or electric trimmer. Sturdy gloves. A step or kick stool rather than a ladder for plants over a metre: working from a ladder with shears or a trimmer requires one hand on the ladder, which limits what you can do safely. Have someone spot you if you do use a ladder. Check for wasp nests before you start: a topiary shrub is a favourite wasp nest site in July and August.

Cone vs pyramid: how they differ in practice

A cone has a circular base tapering to a point. A pyramid has a square or rectangular base tapering to a point or a flat top. From the end of the garden they can look almost identical, but cutting them is quite different.

Feature Cone Pyramid
Visual symmetry Looks right from any angle Looks right only from the square-on view; a slight tilt is immediately obvious
Cutting technique Continuous curved face; blade angle changes gradually as you work around Four flat faces with sharp edges between them; each face is cut in passes parallel to the face
Difficulty More forgiving; unevennesses blend out Less forgiving; flat faces show unevennesses clearly
Best for Planted in pairs as specimen accents; works in round pots Pairs at entrances, formal borders, rectangular beds
Best species Yew, box, privet, hornbeam Yew, box; privet is less reliable for flat faces

Species comparison: yew, box and privet

These are the three species most commonly trained into cones and pyramids in East Kent gardens.

Yew (Taxus baccata)

The best long-term choice for a permanent cone or pyramid. Yew has the finest, densest texture of any hardy UK hedging plant. It is slow growing (10 to 30 cm per year, depending on soil and conditions), which means the annual maintenance cut is light and the shape holds well between cuts. It tolerates very hard cutting back into old wood, which makes renovation possible if a shape goes wrong. The chalk and well-drained loam soils around Sandwich and Woodnesborough suit yew well. The berries (strictly, arils) are toxic to horses, cattle and people: worth knowing if you have livestock near the boundary or small children who might eat them.

Box (Buxus sempervirens)

The traditional topiary choice and still the finest-textured option for small to medium cones up to about 1.2 metres. Box gives an exceptionally precise cut surface. The problem in Kent is box blight (Cylindrocladium buxicola): in wet summers, particularly near the coast where humidity stays high after rain, a freshly trimmed box plant can develop brown patches within days. If you want to use box, plant it in a well-ventilated spot, disinfect tools between cuts, never trim in wet weather, and expect to manage blight as a periodic problem rather than a one-off. Box tree caterpillar has also become established in Kent, stripping plants to bare stems in a season. Yew is the lower-risk choice for the long term.

Privet (Ligustrum ovalifolium)

Fast and forgiving, which makes it a reasonable choice for training a cone or pyramid quickly in a new garden. Privet grows 30 to 60 cm a year, so it reaches the target height fast and the annual maintenance cut is more substantial. The finish is slightly coarser than yew or box: the leaves are larger and the cut surface is less uniform. For a temporary or lower-budget topiary effect, privet works. For a feature plant that is meant to last 20 years with a crisp edge, yew is the better investment.

Training a cone: the sequence

The cone is the better shape to start with if this is your first attempt at formal topiary. Here is the sequence we use on young plants:

  1. Set the apex: push a bamboo cane vertically into the top centre of the plant, with the tip at the height you want the finished cone to reach. This is your reference point for the point throughout the cut.
  2. Find the base diameter: the widest point of the cone sits at the base, at soil level. Work out how wide you want the base to be: a cone 1.5 metres tall typically has a base diameter of 60 to 90 cm. Place four canes at the base to mark the circle.
  3. Cut the apex first: take a small amount off the very tip, working within 5 to 8 cm of the cane. Get the point established before you work on the faces.
  4. Work down in overlapping passes: start at the top and work down each section of the face, keeping the blade at the correct angle to the cane. Rotate around the plant frequently to check the shape from different angles.
  5. Check from a distance: walk back 5 metres and look. The eye sees at a distance what the hands miss at close range. Adjust, then check again.
  6. Finish the base edge: define the transition from the cone to the ground or pot. A neat horizontal cut at the base line closes the shape.

For a pyramid, the same sequence applies, but you work face by face rather than rotating continuously around the plant. Cut one flat face, check it is vertical (a spirit level held against a cane resting on the face is the most reliable check), then move to the adjacent face and cut to meet the first. The arris (the sharp edge between two faces) forms last: you cannot cut it until both faces are done.

Tools: hand shears or a power trimmer?

For a small cone or pyramid under 1 metre, single-handed topiary shears give the most precise result. You can see exactly where the blade is and work in short, precise cuts. A power trimmer, particularly on a dense, compact plant, can grab and pull, leaving small tears rather than cuts.

For a taller plant (1.2 metres and above), a lightweight hedge trimmer with a short bar (40 to 45 cm) makes the work faster without much loss of precision. Hold the blade flat against the face of the cone and draw it steadily from the apex downward. Avoid a swinging motion: it introduces undulation into the face.

Whatever tool you use, disinfect the blades between plants if you are cutting more than one. Box blight, box tree caterpillar and certain fungal diseases spread readily on unwashed blades. A spray with diluted bleach (1 part bleach to 9 parts water) or a commercial tool disinfectant takes about 30 seconds and prevents a season-long problem.
