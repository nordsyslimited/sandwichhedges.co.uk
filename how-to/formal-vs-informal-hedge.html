Formal vs informal hedges: which style suits your garden?

The choice between a clipped formal hedge and a looser, flowering informal one shapes how much time you'll spend maintaining it, how much wildlife it attracts, and what your garden looks like in winter. Here is how the two styles compare, and which plants suit each approach in East Kent.

Published September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

People often frame this as a question of taste, which it partly is. But it's also a question of how much time you want to spend on maintenance, what your neighbours are like, and what you actually want the hedge to do. A formal yew hedge in front of a Victorian terrace is a different thing from a hawthorn-and-blackthorn boundary on a rural plot, even though both are hedges.

We cut both kinds across East Kent, and the difference in how they behave, and what they need, is significant. Let's go through it properly.

What the terms mean

A formal hedge is clipped to a defined shape: straight sides, a level top, often with a slight batter (the sides angled inward so the base is wider than the top). The point is tidiness and geometry. Species used are usually those that respond well to regular clipping and hold a clean edge: yew, box, hornbeam, beech, privet, western red cedar.

An informal hedge is managed rather than sculpted. It might be trimmed once a year to keep it from spreading too far, or it might be left entirely to its own devices on a rural boundary. Species for informal hedges are often flowering or fruiting: hawthorn, blackthorn, dog rose, field maple, hazel, lavender, rosemary, Rosa rugosa. The point is ecological and aesthetic richness rather than precise geometry.

Side-by-side comparison

Formal hedges: the case for them

A well-kept formal hedge is one of the cleanest things a garden can have. Yew in particular, clipped once in August, holds its shape for twelve months and gives a sense of permanence that no fence ever quite achieves. Hornbeam and beech are good alternatives for people put off by yew's slower establishment: both clip beautifully and, if trained as a formal hedge, hold their dead leaves through winter, giving reasonable privacy even when deciduous.

Formal hedges also do something that's underrated: they make the rest of the garden look better by contrast. A clipped backdrop makes colourful planting pop in a way that a loose, informal boundary can't.

The argument against is the cost of precision. A formal hedge clipped badly looks worse than one left alone. If you're doing it yourself, you need a good eye, a line, and time. If you're paying someone else, the two or three clippings a year add up.

Informal hedges: the case for them

An informal hedge is the right choice for anyone who values wildlife, wants flowers and berries, or is working with a rural or cottage-garden setting where precise geometry would look out of place. A mixed native hedge of hawthorn, blackthorn, field maple and dog rose, left to grow and tidied back in late winter once a year, will support more insect species, bird species and small mammals than almost any other feature you could add to a garden.

Informal hedges also tend to be cheaper to establish because native whips are inexpensive and can be planted bare-root in winter at a fraction of the cost of semi-mature specimens. A mixed native hedge 20 metres long might cost £80 to £120 to plant in whip form, versus several hundred pounds for a formal yew or hornbeam hedge of the same length.

The argument against is the predictability problem. An informal hedge that isn't managed at all will gradually spread, swallow paths, and eventually lose its lower density as the base gets shaded out. Even the most informal hedge needs some attention every few years.

Which species for which style?

Best formal hedging species for East Kent

  • Yew (Taxus baccata): the gold standard for formal hedging. Slow to establish, but once away it clips to razor precision. Very long-lived. Tolerates chalk well (good for Sandwich Bay, Deal). Toxic berries, so check if you have young children or dogs. Can be cut hard to renovate.
  • Hornbeam (Carpinus betulus): better than beech on heavier clay soils (Worth, Ash, Wingham). Holds dead leaves in winter. Clips well. Faster than yew.
  • Beech (Fagus sylvatica): similar to hornbeam but prefers chalk and well-drained soils. Excellent on the chalk belt through Sandwich, Barham, Elham. Holds leaves better than hornbeam in exposed sites.
  • Western red cedar (Thuja plicata): a faster, cheaper alternative to leylandii that clips well and doesn't grow quite so aggressively. Better than leylandii for formal work.
  • Privet (Ligustrum ovalifolium): fast, cheap, tolerant of urban conditions. Semi-evergreen. Needs more frequent clipping than yew or hornbeam, but very forgiving of hard cuts.

Best informal hedging species for East Kent

  • Hawthorn (Crataegus monogyna): the backbone of native mixed hedges. Flowers white in May, berries red in autumn. Dense thorny growth gives good security. Tolerates virtually any soil in Kent.
  • Blackthorn (Prunus spinosa): even more impenetrable than hawthorn once established. White flowers in March before the leaves open. Sloe berries in autumn. Excellent for exposed coastal sites at Sandwich Bay.
  • Field maple (Acer campestre): often overlooked but a lovely native hedging plant. Yellow autumn colour. Clips or grows informally. Particularly good on chalk.
  • Rosa rugosa: not a native but one of the best informal hedging plants for coastal Kent. Salt-tolerant, flowers all summer, large red hips, fierce thorns. Perfect for Deal and Sandwich Bay front gardens.
  • Hazel (Corylus avellana): catkins in late winter, edible nuts in autumn. Good for wildlife, especially dormice and squirrels. Responds well to coppicing every several years to keep it dense.
  • Lavender (Lavandula angustifolia): for a low, scented, semi-formal boundary in a sunny spot. Particularly happy on East Kent's chalk. Needs the post-flowering clip we describe in our lavender hedge care guide.

The mixed approach: formal structure, informal planting

These two styles don't have to be mutually exclusive. One approach that works well in larger East Kent gardens is a formally clipped outer boundary hedge (for privacy and structure) with a looser, more informal inner planting of flowering shrubs in front of it. You get the clean line at the boundary and the ecological richness closer to the garden.

Another option is to plant a mixed native hedge but trim one face of it to give a reasonably flat boundary edge while allowing the other face and the top to grow more naturally. You get reasonable formality on the road side and wildlife habitat on the garden side.

The Royal Horticultural Society (RHS) on hedge planting and choosing the right hedge for your garden. The RHS YouTube channel (104,000+ subscribers) covers practical guidance across all styles and species.

Our take for East Kent gardens

If your priority is privacy and you have a town or suburban garden, a formal hedge of hornbeam or yew is hard to beat. Both suit East Kent's soils, both look good, and both stay dense enough year-round to give genuine screening from the road.

If you're on a rural plot, or if wildlife and low maintenance matter more than geometry, a mixed native informal hedge planted in winter as bare-root whips will cost a fraction as much to establish and will be genuinely transformative for local wildlife within three or four years.

If you're genuinely unsure, come down to one of the roads around Worth or Ash and look at the old hedgerows. The mixed native hedges that have been there for a hundred years are doing something a privet hedge cannot replicate. But then, neither can an old blackthorn hedge give you the clean green wall of a clipped yew. They're just different things.
