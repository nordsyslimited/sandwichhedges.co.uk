Why is my conifer hedge going brown? A diagnosis guide

Brown patches on a leylandii, thuja or cypress hedge almost always have a diagnosable cause. Work through these six questions in order to narrow it down.

Updated August 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

A brown patch on a conifer hedge is one of the most common calls we get from gardens in Sandwich, Deal, Worth and Woodnesborough. The patch appears, people panic, and often assume the hedge is dying. In our experience, that is rarely the case. Most browning has a diagnosable cause and a straightforward response. A handful of causes are permanent, and the sooner you identify those, the sooner you can make a sensible decision about what to do next.

The Royal Horticultural Society covers the most common cause (cypress aphid) and the key distinguishing symptoms in their video "Patches On Leylandii Hedges", produced on the Royal Horticultural Society YouTube channel (over 104,000 subscribers). The guide below covers the full diagnostic picture, including five additional causes the video does not address.

Before you start: one quick test

Before working through the questions below, scratch the bark of an affected brown branch with your thumbnail. If you see green or cream underneath, the branch is still alive and may recover. If it is dry and brown all the way through, that stem is dead. This tells you immediately whether you are dealing with a recoverable stress or permanent loss, and applies at every stage of the diagnosis below.

Work through these questions in order

Question 1: Did the browning appear suddenly in late summer (July to September), especially on the inside of the hedge?

YES → Most likely cypress aphid. Go to diagnosis 1.
NO → Continue to question 2.

Question 2: Are individual branches dying at random, and can you see resinous, orange-brown or sunken patches on the stems?

YES → Likely Seiridium cardinale fungal canker. Go to diagnosis 2.
NO → Continue to question 3.

Question 3: Is the browning on the side facing the prevailing wind, or did a dry period immediately precede it?

YES → Likely drought or wind scorch. Go to diagnosis 3.
NO → Continue to question 4.

Question 4: Was the hedge trimmed recently, and does the brown patch correspond exactly to where the trimmer cut?

YES → Cutting into old wood (the brown zone). Go to diagnosis 4.
NO → Continue to question 5.

Question 5: Is the browning starting at the base of the hedge, with the surrounding soil consistently wet and the roots soft or rotted when you dig around them?

YES → Likely Phytophthora root rot. Go to diagnosis 5.
NO → Continue to question 6.

Question 6: Is the browning restricted to a narrow band at the very base of the hedge, near a path, gate, lamp post or regularly used corner?

YES → Almost certainly dog urine. Go to diagnosis 6.
NO → The cause may be a combination of stresses or an unusual pathogen. Contact us for a site visit.

Diagnosis 1: Cypress aphid

Cypress aphid (Cinara cupressivora) is by far the most common cause of sudden browning on leylandii, thuja and lawson cypress hedges in the UK. The RHS identifies it as the leading cause of conifer hedge problems, and it accounts for well over half the browning investigations we carry out in gardens around Sandwich and Deal.

The aphids are small (2 to 4mm), dark brown or grey, and cluster on inner shoots near the base of affected branches. They are most active in late summer, and browning can spread rapidly across a section of hedge within two to three weeks if left untreated. The tell-tale signs alongside the browning are:

  • Sticky honeydew residue on foliage below the affected area
  • Black sooty mould growing on the honeydew
  • Insects visible on the inner stems when you part the foliage

What to do: Treat in autumn with a pyrethrum-based contact insecticide, directing the spray into the interior of the hedge. Dead foliage will not recover, but new growth will fill in from behind over one to two seasons if healthy wood remains underneath. Repeat the treatment the following June or July, before the summer population peaks. If you had aphid damage last year, treat prophylactically the following summer rather than waiting for browning to reappear.

Thuja plicata (western red cedar) suffers less severely from cypress aphid than leylandii. If you are replanting after significant aphid damage, it is worth considering as a replacement. See our thuja plicata care guide for detail on timing and maintenance.

Diagnosis 2: Seiridium cardinale fungal canker

Seiridium canker (Seiridium cardinale) is a fungal disease that attacks the bark of leylandii and other members of the cypress family. It is less common than aphid damage but significantly more serious, because there is no cure once a branch is infected. The visual clues that distinguish it from other causes:

  • Individual branches dying at random intervals along the hedge, not in a contiguous patch
  • Resinous, orange-brown or sunken lesions on the bark of affected stems
  • Foliage turning brown rapidly and remaining attached rather than dropping
  • A noticeable resin smell from the affected branches

What to do: Remove affected branches immediately using clean, disinfected pruning tools. Wipe blades with methylated spirits between each cut. Cut back to healthy wood at least 15cm below the visible canker. Do not compost the removed material; bag and bin it. There is no chemical treatment that cures an established infection. If the canker has reached the main stem of the hedge, that section cannot be saved. Remove and replant after a resting period of several months.

Diagnosis 3: Drought or wind scorch

East Kent gardens are exposed to two desiccating conditions: south-westerly gales off the English Channel in autumn and winter, and dry easterly winds off the Continent in spring. Both draw moisture out of conifer foliage faster than roots can replace it, particularly on the windward face of the hedge or when the soil has dried out after a dry summer.

Drought scorch tends to affect tips and the outer face uniformly. Wind scorch concentrates on whichever side faces the prevailing wind. In gardens at Sandwich Bay or along the Worth and Ash plateaux, wind scorch on the east face of a conifer hedge after a cold, dry spring is extremely common.

What to do: Water thoroughly in autumn before the ground hardens; this is the single most effective preventive measure. Affected foliage is dead and will not recover, but new growth will fill in from behind over the following season if the wood underneath remains alive (confirm with the scratch test). If wind scorch is a recurring problem, consider temporary windbreak netting on the exposed face during the establishment phase, or choose a more scorch-resistant species such as thuja plicata for future hedging.

Diagnosis 4: Cutting into the old wood zone

Conifers have a clearly defined green zone and a dead zone. The green zone is the outer layer of actively growing shoots. Behind it, further into the hedge body, lies a brown interior with no foliage and no dormant buds capable of regenerating. If the trimmer cuts back into this brown zone, whether by reducing the hedge by too much in a single session or by trimming an already-tight hedge further back, the affected area will not re-grow. Conifers have no dormant buds in old wood.

This is one of the few causes of conifer browning that is entirely preventable. The rule is simple: never remove more than one third of the current year's growth in a single trim, and always leave a visible layer of green foliage on every cut surface. Once you have cut into the brown zone, the patch stays brown permanently.

What to do: There is no recovery from this. The options are to let the hedge grow outward from the remaining green sides over two to three years, or to replant the section. If only a small area was over-trimmed, neighbouring green growth will gradually work its way across the gap. If a large section was cut back to bare brown wood, replacement is usually faster. See our guide to leylandii management for trimming limits and frequency.

Over-trimmed your hedge? Do not try to even it up by cutting the green sides back

The natural instinct when one face looks patchy is to cut the green side back to match. This makes the problem worse. Leave the green growth and let it advance to cover the gap over the following seasons. If the hedge has been reduced so severely that no green remains on any face, it cannot recover; replacement is the only option.

Diagnosis 5: Phytophthora root rot

Phytophthora is a water mould that attacks root systems in waterlogged or poorly draining soil. It is more common in clay-heavy gardens around Ash and Woodnesborough and in lower-lying plots that hold water after heavy rain. A hedge dying from Phytophthora typically shows:

  • Browning and dieback starting at the base and moving upward
  • Soft, dark, mushy roots rather than firm white ones when you dig around the base
  • Death of sections of the hedge in wetter areas, with adjacent sections remaining healthy
  • No improvement after additional watering or feeding

What to do: There is no chemical cure for established Phytophthora. Improve drainage first: install a French drain if the site is persistently wet. Remove and destroy affected plants; do not compost them. Before replanting, rest the soil for at least a year and improve its structure with grit and organic matter. Do not replant the same species into a site where Phytophthora has been active; the pathogen persists in wet ground. Some phosphonate-based products can slow spread in adjacent healthy plants, but are not a cure.

Diagnosis 6: Dog urine

Dog urine contains urea at a concentration that burns plant tissues with repeated exposure. The pattern is distinctive: a narrow, often vertical, brown band at the very base of the hedge, usually at a corner, a gate post, a lamp post or a section of path where dogs are walked regularly. The browning stays localised rather than spreading upward or sideways.

What to do: The most effective immediate measure is to dilute the affected soil by watering the base thoroughly daily for a week, which washes urea salts out of the root zone. Long term, physical barriers, low fence panels set in front of the hedge base, or deterrent sprays reduce repeat exposure. Once the urine source is interrupted, most conifers will push new growth into the gap within one season. Removing the most badly affected base shoots can speed recovery. If the gap is large, infilling with a young plant of the same species is the quickest solution.

Leylandii, thuja and lawson cypress: which recovers best?

The three commonest conifer hedges in East Kent gardens behave differently when stressed:

  • Leylandii (x Cuprocyparis leylandii): fast-growing but relatively brittle when things go wrong. Susceptible to Seiridium canker, cannot regenerate from old wood at all, and once badly browned may look poor for several seasons. Aphid damage is usually the most recoverable problem.
  • Thuja plicata (western red cedar): more resilient than leylandii to aphids and scorch, and recovers better from drought events. Still cannot regenerate from old wood. The species to consider if you are replanting after repeated leylandii problems.
  • Lawson cypress (Chamaecyparis lawsoniana): the most susceptible of the three to Phytophthora and to cypress aphid. Can suffer severe dieback in wet winters. See our Lawson cypress care guide for trimming timing and disease notes.

When to call a professional

If you have worked through the questions above and are still unsure, or if browning is spreading rapidly and covers more than a quarter of the hedge run, a professional assessment is worth having before assuming the whole hedge is lost. We offer site visits across Sandwich, Deal, Worth, Ash, Woodnesborough, Eastry and Sandwich Bay. In most cases a single visit identifies the cause and sets out a practical recovery plan.
