April hedge care in Kent: what to cut, what to leave, what to do instead

April is the month when most people look at their hedge, notice it has grown a lot, and pick up the trimmer. This is the guide for why that is usually a bad idea, which species are the exception, and what will actually help.

Updated September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

April and the nesting season

Under the UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is a criminal offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. The nesting season runs roughly 1 March to 31 August. April is, in most years, the peak month for active nests in East Kent hedges. Robins, blackbirds, wrens, sparrows and blue tits are all typically mid-brooding in April, with pigeons often on their second clutch. This is not a reason to panic, but it is a reason to leave most hedges completely alone until September.

The hedge has been growing hard since March and is starting to look untidy. That is entirely normal. It is not a reason to cut it. The growth that feels irritating in April becomes the dense, well-established new wood that takes a clean trim in September. Cutting it now, besides the legal risk, often produces a sparse, struggling hedge by autumn.

That said, not all hedges are equal in April. Here is a species-by-species comparison of what tends to be safe and what should be left alone across gardens in Sandwich, Deal, Woodnesborough, Eastry and the surrounding villages.

April cutting comparison: by hedge type

Hedge type April status Notes
Privet (Ligustrum) Do not cut Dense cover, popular nesting site. Wait until June for a light summer trim if confirmed empty, September for the main cut.
Beech and hornbeam Do not cut Deciduous, leaves now expanding. Not ideal nesting cover but do not cut: one annual cut in late July or August is the normal schedule.
Laurel and cherry laurel Do not cut Provides year-round dense cover, birds nest from February onwards. Late summer cut only.
Yew Do not cut Slow-growing; the main cut is late August to September. No advantage to cutting earlier.
Leylandii / conifers Can trim (with check) Less sheltered than broadleaf hedges. First trim of the year is typically April to May. Nest check required first. Stay inside the green: never cut into brown wood.
Box (Buxus) Low risk, check first Compact edging box offers little nesting cover. A careful trim is lower risk than a tall mixed hedge, but check all the same. Late May is the more established first-cut date.
Hawthorn and blackthorn Do not cut Both are premier nesting sites for several priority species in Kent. Leave entirely until late August or September.
Holly Do not cut Dense, year-round cover and a well-used nesting site. Main cut is late July or August.
Photinia (Red Robin) Do not cut A light summer trim can go in mid-June if confirmed empty. April is too early.

What to focus on in April instead

April is the most productive month of the year for hedge maintenance that is not cutting. The plants are coming out of dormancy, growth is beginning and anything you do now amplifies what the hedge does over the rest of the season.

Feed the hedge

If you only feed your hedge once a year, do it now. A balanced slow-release granular fertiliser (7-7-7 or similar) broadcast along the root run on both sides, lightly raked in with a fork, sets the hedge up for the entire growing season. Established hedges in the heavy clay soils around Sandwich benefit from feeding every April: the clay locks up nutrients and the hedge can look thin and pale by midsummer if it never gets any help.

Mulch the base

Lay 7 to 10 cm of well-rotted compost or bark chip along the hedge base. Keep it a few centimetres clear of the stems to avoid collar rot. Mulching in April suppresses spring weeds, retains moisture through the drier May and June period, and gradually feeds the soil as it breaks down. In the sandy, free-draining soils around Sandwich Bay and the coastal strip, mulching is especially valuable for moisture retention.

Check for frost damage and bare patches

Walk the hedge in April and look for sections that browned off over winter. Frost-damaged growth on photinia, escallonia or griselinia will be papery and brown. You can remove dead wood from individual stems carefully without triggering the nesting-season rules, as long as you are not touching a section with active nests. See our guide on filling gaps in hedges for what to do about sections that have died back further.

Water new hedges

Anything planted in autumn or late winter needs consistent moisture as growth begins. April can be deceptively dry in East Kent, particularly in the free-draining coastal soils. New hedges in Worth, Ash or Eastry gardens should get a slow, deep watering at the base every week if rain is scarce. Wilting in April sets a new hedge back weeks.

Check for box tree moth

If you have box hedging, April is when box tree moth caterpillars become active in earnest after overwintering as small larvae. Check for webbing, stripped leaves and green frass (caterpillar droppings) at the base of stems. If you catch an infestation early in April you have more options. See our box tree moth guide for the full identification and treatment detail.

Monitor for aphids on privet

Privet aphid colonies begin building in April as new growth appears. The first sign is usually curled, yellowing leaves at the shoot tips. A blast with a hose knocks off early colonies without chemicals. If the infestation is established, see our hedge aphids guide.

Further watching: wildlife and hedges

A short video published on YouTube in February 2026, titled Wildlife-friendly hedge trimming, covers the relationship between hedge management and the nesting calendar. Worth watching as a reminder of why April is genuinely the wrong month for most cuts.

When neighbour disputes come up in April

April is also the time when overhanging branches become noticeable as growth accelerates. You are legally entitled to cut overhanging branches back to the boundary line, but the same nesting-season rules apply: if there is an active nest in the section you want to cut, you wait. See our overhanging hedge branches guide for the full legal position, and our hedge heights and neighbour law guide if the dispute is about the hedge's overall height.
