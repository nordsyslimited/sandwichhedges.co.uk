Is my hedge dead or dormant? A winter diagnosis guide

A bare or brown hedge in winter doesn't mean it's gone. Most hedges in East Kent look worse than they are between November and March. This guide walks you through the questions to ask before you dig anything out.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Every autumn we get calls from people who are convinced their hedge has died. Most of the time it hasn't. Deciduous hedges drop their leaves. Semi-evergreen species like privet and beech thin out in a cold winter. Conifers develop brown patches on shaded interior growth as a matter of course. The hedge that looked lush in August can look very bleak in January.

That said, hedges do die. Waterlogging, disease, drought, or simply old age can kill plants that looked healthy the previous year. The trick is telling the difference before you spend money having something removed that would have come back in spring, or, equally, leaving dead material standing when it needs to come out.

Work through the questions below in order. Each one narrows the diagnosis.

Step 1: Is your hedge deciduous or evergreen?

Q1: Does your hedge normally hold its leaves through winter?

YES — evergreen

Leylandii, laurel, yew, thuja, box, holly, escallonia, griselinia

↓ Go to Step 2

NO — deciduous or semi-evergreen

Beech, hornbeam, hawthorn, privet, hazel, blackthorn, field maple, forsythia

↓ Go to Step 3

Step 2: Evergreen hedges — reading the colour

Q2: Is the entire plant a uniform brown or rust colour, or are only parts of it brown?

All or most of the hedge is uniformly brown

Browning spread right across the plant, including the growing tips and outer faces

↓ Go to Step 2a (scratch test)

Brown patches with healthy green growth elsewhere

Outer faces or top is still green; brown is concentrated inside, on the windward face, or at the base

↓ Likely stressed, not dead — see Evergreen stress section

Step 2a — The scratch test (30 seconds, decisive)

Find a young stem about finger-thickness. Use your thumbnail or a penknife to scrape a short section of bark from one side. Look at the tissue underneath:

Green or white-green tissue

The plant is alive. Browning has a cause, but the plant has not died. See the recovery section below.

Brown, dry, or grey throughout

This stem is dead. Test 5–6 more stems at different heights and positions before concluding the hedge is gone. If all return the same result, the hedge has likely died.

Test stems at the top, middle and base. A hedge dead from the base up (e.g. from waterlogging) will fail the test at lower stems first. One dead stem in isolation does not mean the whole run is lost.

Step 3: Deciduous and semi-evergreen hedges

Q3: What time of year is it, and what does the hedge look like?

October to mid-April: hedge has few or no leaves

This is normal dormancy for beech, hornbeam, hawthorn, hazel, blackthorn and field maple. Privet is semi-evergreen and will thin considerably in cold winters.

↓ Check for flexible buds — see Step 3a

Late April onwards: no leaf flush when neighbouring hedges are breaking

This is the warning sign. If the hedge has not leafed up by late April when others of the same species have, do the scratch test.

↓ Go to Step 2a (scratch test)

Step 3a — Bud flexibility check

On a dormant deciduous stem, find the buds at the tips of young growth. Buds on a living plant in winter are:

  • Firm but not completely dry and shrivelled
  • Often still tightly closed (this is normal in January and February)
  • Slightly flexible when you press a twig between your fingers rather than snapping brittly

Dead stems are completely brittle, snap rather than flex, and the buds are dry and papery with no internal moisture. If in doubt, make a short cut and check the colour of the inner wood (white or cream = alive; brown or tan throughout = dead for that section).

Common causes of brown or bare hedges that are not dead

Evergreen stress: the most common false alarm

The most frequent call we get in late winter is from homeowners with a leylandii or laurel run that has gone patchy brown after a cold, windy period. In most cases the explanation is:

  • Wind scorch: the windward face of an exposed hedge loses moisture from its foliage faster than the roots can replace it in frozen soil. The result is browning on the side facing the prevailing north or north-east wind. It looks alarming but it is surface damage. New growth will cover it from September onwards.
  • Normal interior die-back: all dense conifers and evergreen shrubs drop interior foliage that is permanently shaded. You'll find it if you push a hand inside the canopy. It does not mean anything is wrong.
  • Frost scorch on new growth: a late spring frost after the hedge has started pushing new growth can burn the soft growing tips. The hedge looks brown from a distance. It is not dead; it has just been caught by a frost.
  • Waterlogging: standing water around the roots over winter causes root death, and the signs show in the foliage by late winter or early spring. Symptoms concentrate at the base of the hedge and in the lowest sections of a run. This is one case where live tissue may not recover if the root damage is extensive.

Beech: the hedge that refuses to look alive in winter

Beech (Fagus sylvatica) confuses people every winter. Unlike most deciduous hedges, beech holds its dead brown leaves through winter (a property called marcescence) and only drops them when new growth pushes them off in April. A beech hedge in January can look entirely brown and dead. It is almost certainly dormant. The scratch test on young stems will show green tissue; the buds will be cigar-shaped and firm. If you are seeing this on a beech hedge that was healthy last summer, it is dormant, not dead.

Privet: the semi-evergreen that looks worse than it is

Privet (Ligustrum ovalifolium) is semi-evergreen along the East Kent coast, meaning it normally keeps most of its leaves through mild winters. After a harder frost period it can drop a significant proportion of them and look sparse or partially bare. A privet with flexible, budded stems and some remaining leaves is dormant. It will flush out again in March. The scratch test confirms.

Confirming the hedge has died: what to do next

If the scratch test returns brown or grey tissue on stems throughout the hedge, and there are no living sections, the plant has died. At that point the options are:

  • Remove and replant: the usual route. The root system of a dead hedge will need to be cleared before replanting, either by digging out or grinding out. How much you clear depends on the replacement species. Conifer stumps should be removed before planting a new conifer or semi-evergreen screen to avoid honey fungus transfer.
  • Wait for confirmation: if you found the hedge dead in January and you're not sure, wait until May before committing to removal. A genuinely dead hedge will still be dead in May. Anything that flushes out was dormant.

If only sections of the hedge have died — common with laurel after particularly cold winters, or with leylandii that has been hit by honey fungus at the root — those sections can sometimes be removed and the gaps replanted. The replanted sections will need several years to catch up in height. We cover the options in our gappy hedge renovation guide.

For hedges along the coast from Sandwich Bay through Deal and into Worth, the coastal exposure does mean more winter wind scorch than you'd see further inland. Brown patches on the seaward face of an evergreen screen in late winter are nearly always scorch, not death. The hedge will recover. We see it every year on the properties we maintain.
