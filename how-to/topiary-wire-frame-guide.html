Using wire frames and cutting templates for topiary shapes

A wire frame will not make you a topiarist overnight, but it will make a cone look like a cone rather than a rough pyramid. Here is how to choose, position and use one on yew, box and privet in Kent gardens.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Most domestic topiary in East Kent is cut freehand, by eye, with a good pair of hand shears. For simple geometric shapes on mature plants, that works perfectly well. Where frames earn their place is on three types of job: training a young plant into a shape for the first time, cutting a shape you need to repeat exactly on matching pair plants, and recovering a form that has gone lopsided after a year of uneven growth.

The basic principle is simple. The frame sits over or around the plant as a three-dimensional guide. You clip back to the wire, working in passes rather than trying to cut the whole shape in one go. The wire tells you where to stop. You do not need to be able to visualise the finished shape in three dimensions, which is what trips up most beginners cutting freehand.

Nesting season: check before you clip

Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs roughly 1 March to 31 August. If you are trimming topiary plants during that window, check inside the plant from underneath before cutting. The safe main clipping season for most Kent topiary is September to February. A light tidy in late May is acceptable only if the plant is checked first.

Step 1: Choose the right frame type

Frames come in two categories: formers for training young plants and cutting guides for established plants.

Training frames

A training frame is left in the plant and grows with it. It is sized generously, typically 10 to 15 per cent larger in all dimensions than the finished shape, to allow for several seasons of infill. The plant grows through and around the frame, filling the form from inside. Clipping back to the frame each season encourages dense, tight growth rather than long, loose shoots. After three to five years, the shape is self-supporting and the frame can be removed.

For box balls in Sandwich and Deal front gardens, a simple galvanised sphere frame is the most common choice. For yew cones and pyramids in older Kent garden designs, a cone frame made from rigid galvanised wire suits yew's moderate growth rate better than a flimsy plastic one, which tends to distort as the plant pushes against it.

Cutting guides

A cutting guide is placed over the plant at clipping time and removed afterwards. It is a template, not a training aid. For a cone, two panels of plywood or galvanised mesh cut to the cone profile, joined at the apex in an X, are enough. For a sphere, the same X approach with curved edges. For cubes and hedging plinths, flat boards held at the correct spacing with clamps or cord act as plumb guides for the flat faces.

The cutting guide approach works well for Sandwich Bay gardens with large, mature yew specimens that are clipped once a year. The guide takes five minutes to assemble, is used for the clip, then stored flat in a shed or garage.

Step 2: Position the frame accurately

Accurate positioning matters more than the precision of the frame itself. A perfect sphere frame, slightly off-centre, produces a shape that leans. Take a few minutes here to avoid having to correct it later.

  1. Find the central stem or the geometric centre of the plant. For multi-stemmed plants, this may mean choosing the dominant leader.
  2. Place the frame and step back ten paces. Look at it from the front, then from the side. It should look plumb and centred. For a matching pair, compare both frames before cutting either plant.
  3. For cone and pyramid frames, check the vertical axis with a spirit level or a plumb line held against the apex.
  4. Peg the frame if the soil is soft or the wind is likely to shift it during cutting. A loose frame is worse than no frame at all.

Step 3: Clip to the frame

Work in horizontal passes from the base upwards on cones and pyramids, or from one side to the other on flat-faced forms. This avoids overworking an area already cut to level while the rest of the plant still has long growth obscuring the frame.

Use hand shears rather than a powered trimmer for topiary work, except on very large flat-topped plinth shapes. A trimmer removes material faster but it is easy to cut past the frame on a rounded form. Hand shears give you immediate feedback through the blade resistance when you approach the wire.

Cut just inside the frame, not tight against the wire. Leave 1 to 2 centimetres of shoot behind the wire so the frame does not press the foliage flat, which can mark or discolour it. Clear the clippings from inside the frame as you work, as an accumulation of cut material can disguise the wire and lead to an overcut.

Notes by species

Yew is the most forgiving topiary plant in East Kent. It clips to a hard edge, holds its line through winter, and browns back less than box or privet if you catch the stem wood. The coastal mild winters around Sandwich Bay mean yew rarely suffers the dieback that harder inland frosts cause further north. Cut yew hard once in late August; it needs no second clip most years.

Box clips more finely than yew, which makes it suited to intricate shapes. However, box blight (Cylindrocladium buxicola) and box tree moth (Cydalima perspectalis) have made maintaining healthy box significantly harder in Kent over the past decade. Inspect the plant before cutting. If you see bare stems, stripped foliage or fine webbing inside the frame, treat for box tree moth first rather than cutting around the damage. For more on box pests and diseases, see the box tree moth guide.

Privet is faster-growing and cheaper than yew or box, which makes it common in Eastry and Woodnesborough gardens where topiary is used as a low-maintenance visual feature rather than a garden statement. It needs two to three clips per season to hold a shape cleanly, but it fills gaps quickly if the frame forces a cut a little short. Use a privet frame that is slightly smaller than the finished shape to account for this vigour.

Step 4: Remove the frame and check

After cutting, remove the frame and step back. Turn the plant (or move yourself around it) and check the shape from multiple angles. Common issues at this stage:

  • Flat spots on sphere or cone shapes: usually caused by the frame shifting during cutting. Correct with hand shears, working slowly in a circular motion around the flat area.
  • Overhang at the base of cone shapes: the base of a cone often has the most growth and is the most likely to have been cut short in an early pass. Leave it slightly long and return to it last, once the apex shape is confirmed.
  • Striped finish on yew: usually caused by cutting in one direction only with hand shears. Cross-cut in a second pass at right angles to the first, using the first pass as a guide.

Step 5: Seasonal maintenance

Once the shape is established, most topiary plants need one main clip and one light tidy per year. For the main clip, use the frame as described above. For the tidy, work freehand using the established shape as your guide: by this point, the form is clear enough that the frame is not needed.

Store galvanised frames flat or in a dry shed between seasons. Avoid folding or bending the rods: a kinked frame produces a kinked cut. Light rust on galvanised wire is cosmetic and does not affect performance; heavy rust that pits the wire may cause it to snap under the pressure of the plant's growth, which can damage the shape.
