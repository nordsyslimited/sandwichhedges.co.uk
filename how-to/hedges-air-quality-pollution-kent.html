How hedges filter air pollution in East Kent

Research shows dense evergreen hedging can noticeably reduce particulate matter near roads. Which species filter best and how to get the most from them.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Hedges have always been part of East Kent's character, as field boundaries, garden screens and windbreaks along the coast. What most people do not know is that a well-maintained, dense hedge also acts as a filter for traffic pollution: slowing wind, trapping fine particles and creating a low-pollution zone on the sheltered side.

This matters more than it might sound. Parts of East Kent, including stretches of the A256 through Sandwich, the A258 toward Deal and the approach roads through Ash and Eastry, carry significant volumes of diesel traffic. Research from the University of Surrey and work commissioned by the Royal Horticultural Society suggests that the right kind of hedge in the right position can reduce PM2.5 concentrations (fine particulate matter) at breathing height by 50 to 60% in the immediate vicinity.

This is not a reason to plant a hedge instead of tackling vehicle emissions at source. But it is a reason to think about hedge species choice and density if you are planting near a road, and to maintain existing hedges in a way that keeps that barrier working.

1. What hedges actually filter

The two main pollutants that hedges can influence are particulate matter (PM2.5 and PM10, the fine dust particles produced by combustion engines and tyre and brake wear) and, to a lesser extent, nitrogen dioxide (NO2). Hedges do not remove NO2 significantly from the air column; that requires much larger-scale vegetation. But fine particles are a different matter.

A dense hedge acts as a physical baffle. It slows the wind speed, which causes heavier particles to fall out of suspension rather than travelling further downwind. The leaf surface itself traps a proportion of particles directly, particularly on leaves with rough or waxy surfaces and complex three-dimensional structure. The effect diminishes within 10 to 15 metres of the hedge on the sheltered side, but within that zone it is measurable.

2. Which species filter best

The key qualities for air pollution filtering are year-round evergreen foliage, dense canopy structure and complex or rough leaf surfaces. The research literature points consistently to a shortlist of species:

Yew (Taxus baccata): Dense, year-round, slow-growing. The fine needle-like foliage creates an excellent physical barrier once the hedge is established. Yew is also one of the most resilient species in East Kent's chalk and clay soils. Its one limitation for this purpose is slow establishment: a yew hedge takes five to eight years to reach a useful filtering density.

Western red cedar (Thuja plicata): Fast-growing, dense, year-round foliage with high surface area. Studies from the University of Surrey specifically cite western red cedar as one of the most effective species for particle trapping. It is not as commonly used as leylandii in East Kent gardens but it is worth considering for new boundary plantings near roads.

Privet (Ligustrum ovalifolium): Semi-evergreen in a mild Kent winter. Privet loses some leaves in cold snaps but typically holds most of its foliage through the winter months in the coastal climate of Sandwich and Deal. It is a reasonable performer for air quality, particularly given its dense growth habit when trimmed regularly.

Laurel (Prunus laurocerasus): Large, glossy leaves with a high surface area. Cherry laurel is a good particle trapper, though the large smooth leaf surface is less effective than rough or complex surfaces. It is fast-establishing, which matters if the goal is early pollution protection.

Leylandii: Dense year-round foliage and fast growth, but it requires more careful management to maintain a dense base. See our leylandii guide for the detail on keeping these hedges in useful condition.

3. Positioning for maximum effect

A hedge positioned at the boundary of a garden adjacent to a busy road provides the most direct benefit. It needs to be tall enough to redirect airflow at the height where people are spending time: a 1.2 m hedge has limited benefit for a first-floor window or a raised terrace; a 1.8 to 2 m evergreen hedge makes a material difference to ground-level particle concentrations in a garden patio or play area behind it.

The hedge should also be continuous with no significant gaps. A section with a gate or a gap left for access reduces the filtering effect along that run. Where a gap is unavoidable, plant a denser section on either side to compensate.

Perpendicular placement is most effective: a hedge running parallel to the road, at right angles to the prevailing wind direction from the road, creates the best barrier. East Kent's coastal conditions mean wind direction is variable, but the prevailing south-westerly typically pushes road pollution toward the north-east: hedges on the north-east boundary of gardens near the main roads are in the best position.

4. Maintaining density for maximum effect

The filtering benefit of a hedge depends entirely on its density. A thinned or gappy hedge, particularly one that has lost growth at the base, allows pollution to pass through rather than being deflected. Regular annual trimming, maintaining a wider-at-base profile (see our guide on hedge profiles and batter angle) and replacing any dead sections are part of keeping the hedge functional, not just tidy.

Avoid very hard renovation cuts on pollution-filtering hedges in mid-summer: heavy cutting leaves the hedge open and reduces the filtering benefit for the rest of the season. If renovation is needed, do it in late February or early March before the growing season, so new growth fills the gaps through spring and summer.

5. What hedges cannot do

It is worth being clear about the limits. A hedge reduces particle concentrations in its immediate vicinity; it does not create a clean-air zone across a whole garden. It has no meaningful effect on NO2, ozone or carbon monoxide in the air. It is not a substitute for emission controls at source. And a gap in an otherwise continuous hedge significantly reduces the effect along that run.

What it does do is create a measurably better environment within a few metres of a busy road, at the specific locations where people spend time: by the back door, in the patio area, near a children's play space. That is a worthwhile benefit, particularly in gardens that adjoin the main roads through Sandwich, Deal, Worth and Eastry.
