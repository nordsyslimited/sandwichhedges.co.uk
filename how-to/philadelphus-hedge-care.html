Philadelphus (mock orange) as a hedge or screen

Mock orange is one of the best-smelling boundary shrubs you can plant. It is also one of the most reliably clipped bald by people who mean well. Here is what actually works.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Philadelphus coronarius and its cultivars have been common in English garden boundaries for a long time. The June and July flowers are a cream-white with a strong, sweet scent, and the plant itself is undemanding: chalk soil, partial shade, dry summers, all fine. It does not need much care, but the one thing it does need is not to be treated as a hedge you clip with shears twice a year.

Below is a direct comparison of how the two approaches play out, followed by the practical guide to managing philadelphus properly.

Nesting season and philadelphus

The correct pruning window for philadelphus, immediately after flowering, falls in July and early August, which is still within the nesting season under the UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981. Nesting season runs roughly 1 March to 31 August. The dense leafy growth of philadelphus makes it popular nesting cover, particularly for dunnocks, blackbirds and wood pigeons, which can have multiple broods through summer. Always check for active nests before cutting.

Before you start: safety

  • Eye protection: safety glasses when using a powered trimmer, even for short sessions.
  • Ear defenders: petrol and electric trimmers both exceed safe noise levels within minutes.
  • Gloves: sturdy gloves. Philadelphus is not thorny but secateurs can slip.
  • RCD adaptor: essential for any corded electric trimmer. No exceptions.
  • Ladder awareness: for tall established plants, a long-reach pole trimmer or loppers are safer than a ladder for anything above head height. If a ladder is needed, have someone footing it.

Formal clipping versus selective pruning: the comparison

Formal clipping (shears or trimmer) Selective pruning (secateurs after flowering)
Flowers Lost within one to two seasons Maintained or improved year on year
Shape Tight, formal edge Informal, arching; not suitable for a crisp line
Effort Low per session, but sessions add up One session per year, takes longer but infrequent
Long-term health Plant gradually weakens, becomes congested Renewed by removing old stems annually
Wildlife value Nesting cover, but no flowers for insects Good nesting cover plus summer insect food source
Best use Only if structural shape matters more than scent Informal screen, boundary, mixed planting, wildlife garden

How to manage philadelphus for maximum flowering

When to prune

Philadelphus flowers on shoots produced during the previous summer. The rule is simple: cut immediately after flowering, in July or early August. Cut before the flowers open and you remove the buds. Cut in autumn or winter and you achieve the same thing, just later. The window between the last flowers dropping and the end of nesting season (31 August) can be tight. If the plant is clearly empty of active nests, prune promptly in July. If there are nesting birds present, wait until the brood has fledged, which may push you into late August.

What to cut

Two operations, done at the same session each summer:

  • Shorten the flowered shoots: cut each stem that carried flowers back to a pair of strong buds or to a healthy side shoot lower down. Do not cut it all the way to the base unless the stem is very old and congested.
  • Remove old stems: take out about one in three of the oldest main stems at or near ground level. Old stems, usually the thickest ones, become congested and produce shorter, less floriferous sideshoots. Removing them opens the plant up and encourages the vigorous new stems that flower best. Use loppers or a pruning saw for anything more than thumb-thickness.

The whole job takes 20 to 30 minutes on a well-maintained plant with secateurs and loppers. Do not use hedge shears on philadelphus unless you are specifically trying to suppress flowering.

Renovation pruning on an overgrown plant

An old, congested philadelphus that has been clipped or left unpruned for years can look like a tangle of crossing woody stems. The approach here is the same as for any mature deciduous shrub: remove the oldest, thickest stems at the base, which opens the plant up and stimulates new growth from the base. On a severely overgrown specimen, cutting all the old stems hard to about 30 cm from the ground in July after flowering will cause a significant burst of new growth, though you will lose one season's flowers from the plant while it regenerates. Alternatively, spread the renovation over two or three years, removing a third of the old stems each summer.

Choosing a philadelphus cultivar for a boundary

For a full-height informal screen to 3 metres, the straight Philadelphus coronarius is vigorous and reliable on chalk. 'Virginal' (double-flowered, also tall) gives a prolonged display but is less manageable. For a smaller garden boundary, 'Belle Etoile' stays to about 1.5 m with a semi-spreading habit and single flowers with a pink blush at the centre. For a really compact screen at around 1 m, 'Manteau d'Hermine' has small double flowers and a tidy habit, though it is less vigorous on very dry chalk soils.

All cultivars tolerate the free-draining chalk and sandy soils common around Sandwich, Worth and Eastry. In a damp, shaded corner, 'Beauclerk' is more tolerant of heavier soil than the species.

The Garden Ninja YouTube channel, run by BBC Garden Rescue presenter and RHS-qualified garden designer Lee Burkhill (66,000 subscribers), covers the management of informal flowering shrubs including philadelphus in his general guides to garden boundary planting. His video on hedge and shrub maintenance is at youtube.com/watch?v=_Dc3B2I7bu4. The overall approach, working selectively with secateurs rather than shearing everything flat, is what the plant needs.
