Leycesteria formosa (pheasant berry) as an informal garden hedge

Pheasant berry is one of those plants that surprises people. It grows fast, flowers for months, the berries bring in blackbirds and thrushes, and it copes happily in part shade where almost nothing else will hedge-up. The catch is that it is not a hedge in the formal sense, and it will spread if you give it room. Here is how to use it well.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Eight things to know before you plant leycesteria as a hedge

  1. It is not a formal hedge plant. You cannot clip it flat and expect it to stay that way. Treat it as an informal screen.
  2. It self-seeds prolifically. In a garden without hard edges (paving, gravel, a mown lawn), expect seedlings to appear wherever birds drop the berries.
  3. The pruning window is late February to mid-March. Hard annual pruning produces the best flowering and prevents the plant becoming a woody, sparse skeleton.
  4. It flowers on new growth. A hard prune does not reduce flowering; it improves it. Do not be shy about cutting back.
  5. In part shade it is outstanding: one of very few large shrubs that screens effectively under the canopy of a tree or behind a north-facing wall.
  6. Wildlife value is real and significant: berries for pheasants and thrushes, nectar for bumblebees from June to October, hollow stems for solitary bees.
  7. It is hardy in East Kent but the hollow stems die back in hard winters. Prune out any dead stems cleanly in February; the crown will regenerate from the base.
  8. It is not suitable for a small enclosed garden without active management. In an open front garden or along a lane, it needs watching. In a large back garden or a wilder boundary section, it is excellent.

What leycesteria formosa actually is

Leycesteria formosa goes by several names: pheasant berry, Himalayan honeysuckle, and granny's curls (a reference to the arching, hollow-stemmed growth habit). It is native to the Himalayas and western China, introduced to British gardens in the 19th century, and now naturalised in parts of southern England, where it seeds freely into hedgerows and scrubby ground.

It is a large deciduous shrub, eventually reaching 2-2.5 m in height and spreading to a similar width, with bright green hollow stems, heart-shaped leaves, and distinctive pendant flower spikes: white tubular flowers in claret-coloured bracts, produced from June through to the first frosts. The berries that follow are initially green, then purple-black, with a slightly sweet, unusual flavour that pheasants find irresistible and that some people describe as toffee-ish.

Is it right for your boundary?

Best uses

  • Informal screening along the back of a property where a clipped hedge is not required.
  • A wildlife section in a larger garden, deliberately planted for birds and insects.
  • Screening under trees or on a north-facing boundary where most evergreens struggle.
  • A fast-growing temporary screen while a slower, more permanent hedge establishes.
  • A country garden boundary in a larger property around Worth, Ash or Eastry, where the self-seeding is less of a concern.

Where to avoid it

  • A small enclosed front garden where self-seeding would quickly become a problem.
  • Any boundary where you need a precisely defined, flat-faced formal hedge.
  • Adjacent to a vegetable patch or area where ground is regularly cultivated and seedlings would be troublesome.
  • Directly against a fence or wall that it will soon outgrow sideways against.

Nesting season: check before late-winter pruning

The UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to damage an active bird's nest. The leycesteria's arching stems and dense growth make it a popular nesting site for dunnocks, blackbirds and wrens. Nesting season runs roughly 1 March to 31 August. The late-February pruning window is safe, but by early March you should check the plant carefully before cutting. If you find a nest in use, leave that section and return once the birds have fledged.

How to prune leycesteria for use as an informal screen

The annual cut: late February to mid-March

The most effective approach is a hard annual cut to roughly 30 cm above ground level, while the plant is still dormant. This may look dramatic, but it is the right thing to do: leycesteria produces its flowers, and therefore its berries, on the current season's growth. Old stems become increasingly hollow and prone to wind-rocking as they age. Cutting hard produces vigorous new stems, better flowering, and a much tidier plant through summer.

Use loppers for stems up to 2 cm in diameter, a pruning saw for anything thicker. Clean cuts matter: ragged cuts left at shoulder height create entry points for disease and look poor. Cut back to a point just above a healthy bud or to a low side shoot if one is present.

Removing winter die-back

In a hard winter, the tips of older stems can die back. These show as hollow, discoloured sections when you cut into them in February. Cut back to healthy green wood. The plant will regenerate from the base even if quite a lot of the top growth has been killed.

Mid-season tidying

Beyond the annual cut, leycesteria needs very little attention. If an arching stem is growing in the wrong direction or flopping onto a path, tie it back or remove it entirely at the base. Do not try to clip the plant to shape in summer: this removes the flowering shoots and leaves cut stumps that look worse than the untouched stems.

Managing self-seeding

The berries are the plant's main wildcard. Left to ripen fully in October and November, they will be scattered by birds into every bare patch of soil within range. In a garden with paving, gravel or a well-maintained lawn around the leycesteria, this is a minor inconvenience: seedlings are small and easy to pull. In a garden with open beds, a vegetable patch or rough ground adjacent, it needs more active management.

Practical options

  • Cut before the berries ripen: a hard cut in September, before the berries mature to purple-black, removes the seed source entirely. You lose late flowers and berries, but the plant regrows quickly and the wildlife value through the summer has already been given.
  • Remove fallen berries: tedious but effective for smaller plants. Sweep them up from paving, rake them out of beds. Wear gloves; the dark juice stains hands.
  • Pull seedlings early: leycesteria seedlings are easy to identify from late spring onwards: pairs of smooth, slightly pointed leaves on reddish stems. Pull them when small; they are much harder to remove once the root system has gone deep.
  • Hard edges: a mown lawn or paving around the plant means seedlings are cut or noticed and removed before they establish. Ground cover or mulch also reduces successful germination.

Wildlife value in practice

In the right position, leycesteria is one of the most wildlife-productive shrubs you can plant in an East Kent garden. The flowering season runs from June to October or beyond in a mild autumn, with new spikes produced continuously. Bumblebees, particularly buff-tailed and white-tailed bumblebees common throughout the Sandwich and Deal area, work the flowers consistently.

The berries are eaten by blackbirds, thrushes, fieldfares (in a good autumn), and, where pheasants are present in the countryside around Worth and Ash, by the birds that gave the plant its name. Waxwings, which visit East Kent in some winters when the Scandinavian berry crop fails, will take leycesteria berries if they persist into December.

The hollow, bamboo-like stems are used by some species of solitary bee as nesting sites: mason bees and leafcutter bees in particular will investigate cut stems left at the right diameter. If you cut the plant in late winter, leave a few old stems intact at 60-80 cm, cut cleanly across the top, to provide nesting material through spring and summer before removing them in the following February.

Establishment and planting

Leycesteria is sold as pot-grown stock in most garden centres from spring through to autumn. It grows quickly from the first season: a plant purchased in April can reach 1-1.5 m by the end of its first summer in reasonable conditions. Bare ground, a position in sun to part shade, and one good watering per week in dry spells in the first summer is all it needs.

For a hedge-like screen, plant at 60-75 cm centres. Any closer and the stems become congested and airless; any further and the screen takes longer to close up. On the reasonably fertile soils around Sandwich and Deal, establishment is rarely a problem. On chalk or thin sandy soil, mulch generously and water through the first summer.
