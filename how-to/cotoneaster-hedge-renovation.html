How to renovate an overgrown cotoneaster hedge

Cotoneaster is one of the most forgiving hedging plants for a renovation cut. Here is what we do when a cotoneaster hedge has got well and truly out of hand.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Cotoneaster is a surprisingly common hedge plant in older East Kent gardens. Around Sandwich, Deal, Eastry, Worth and Ash you will find both Cotoneaster simonsii and C. franchetii used as formal boundary hedging, often planted in the 1970s and 1980s. Left without attention for a few years they thicken and spread reliably; left for a decade or more they can double in width and lose the base density that made them useful as a boundary hedge in the first place.

The good news is that cotoneaster takes a renovation cut far better than most semi-evergreens. Unlike box or yew, which need careful staging, most cotoneaster species will push vigorous new growth from old brown wood. That tolerance makes a hard renovation not just possible but straightforward, provided you pick the right season and work in the right order.

What follows is the approach we use on overgrown cotoneaster hedges across East Kent. The same method applies whether the hedge is a tidy simonsii boundary or a franchetii screen that has wandered two feet wider than it should be.

Nesting season: check before you cut

Under the UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs roughly from 1 March to 31 August. Cotoneaster is excellent nesting cover because of its dense twiggy growth, and the berries attract fieldfares, redwings and blackbirds to East Kent gardens through autumn and winter. The safe renovation window is September to February. If you do need to work between March and August, check the hedge carefully for active nests first and do not cut any section where a nest is occupied.

Before you start: kit and safety

Cotoneaster renovation involves thicker stems than a standard annual trim, so the kit list is longer than usual.

  • Eye protection (mandatory): hedge trimmer clippings travel surprisingly far; with cotoneaster the stem tips can be springy and unpredictable
  • Gloves: sturdy leather or reinforced work gloves; older cotoneaster stems have rough bark that chafes quickly
  • Ear defenders if using a petrol trimmer
  • Ladder safety: use a pole trimmer rather than a ladder wherever the hedge height allows; if a ladder is unavoidable, have someone footing it and never overreach sideways
  • RCD adaptor for electric tools
  • Loppers and a pruning saw: a hedge trimmer alone will not deal with the thicker base stems; loppers handle growth up to about 4 cm in diameter, a pruning saw for anything heavier
  • Check for wasp nests before starting, particularly in late summer and early autumn when nests are at their largest

What counts as renovation pruning?

Maintenance trimming keeps an established hedge at its current size and shape: a light pass with the trimmer once or twice a year, removing the new season's growth and keeping the outline clean. Renovation pruning is something different. It is the decision to cut back hard, to reshape a hedge that has outgrown its space, to reduce a hedge that has grown too tall for a neighbour or a boundary, or to restore density to a hedge that has become sparse and leggy at the base.

Renovation is called for when the hedge has spread significantly beyond the boundary line, when the top has reached an unmanageable height, or when the lower third of the hedge has become bare wood with little foliage because light cannot reach it. All three problems are common in neglected cotoneaster hedges, and all three are solvable with a well-timed hard cut.

When is the right time?

Late winter, from January to early March, is the ideal window for a cotoneaster renovation. By that point the berries have been stripped by winter birds, the nesting season has not yet started, and the plant is dormant or just beginning to stir. A renovation cut taken in January or February leaves the hedge in the best position to put energy into new growth as soon as the soil warms in spring.

September to November is the other workable period, once nesting season is over and while the soil is still warm. The advantage of an autumn cut is that the hedge has the whole winter to settle before spring growth; the disadvantage is that you lose the berries earlier in the season, before the winter thrushes have had the benefit of them.

Kent's mild coastal winters widen the window a little. Around Sandwich Bay and Deal, ground frosts below -5C are uncommon, which means a renovation cut taken in mid-February carries little risk of frost damage to the fresh cut faces. In a colder winter, wait until March is in sight before cutting, to avoid fresh cuts opening during a cold snap.

How hard can you cut a cotoneaster back?

Most cotoneaster species used in British hedging, including simonsii, franchetii, lacteus and watsonii, will regrow vigorously from old, brown wood. This makes them considerably more forgiving than many evergreens. As a rough guide, you can cut back to within 30 to 45 cm of the base on a well-established hedge, provided there is green tissue somewhere on the interior stems. That 30 to 45 cm acts as the regeneration point: new shoots will push from dormant buds along the old wood above and around it.

The rule is: never cut below the lowest visible green growth. If the interior is completely bare, with no green shoots visible at all, a full hard cut on both sides at once is a bigger risk than the plant can easily absorb. In that case, cut one face hard this winter and leave the other face and the top untouched. The uncut side keeps the root system supplied while the cut face recovers. Come back the following autumn or winter and cut the second face, then reduce the top to the height you want.

Step-by-step renovation process

  1. Walk the interior and check for green growth. Look for any sign of live buds or new shoots on the inside of the stems. Mark sections where you can see no green at all.
  2. Confirm the season is right. See above. If it is between March and August, check carefully for active nests before proceeding.
  3. Gather tools. Loppers for stems up to 4 cm, a pruning saw for anything thicker at the base, a hedge trimmer for the main face, PPE, bags or a skip for the arisings.
  4. Clear the base first. Remove any brambles, nettles or other weeds that have grown in along the base of the hedge. This makes it easier to see the stem structure and to work safely at ground level.
  5. Cut the main face. Work systematically along one face of the hedge. Start with the hedge trimmer, removing the outer layer of growth back to the desired line. Where the trimmer encounters stems too thick to cut cleanly, switch to loppers. Keep the cut line consistent along the length of the hedge.
  6. Cut the other face. Repeat on the second face. If you found sections with no green growth at step 1, leave those sections of the second face this year and return next winter.
  7. Cut the top. Work from the ends inward. Set a cane and string line at the desired height before you start: cotoneaster can look level by eye but be noticeably uneven once you step back. Work inward from each end toward the middle, checking against the string line as you go.
  8. Remove arisings promptly. Bag and clear all the cut material. Cotoneaster berries and seeds will root if left on bare soil, particularly along the base of the hedge. Clear everything before it rains.
  9. Feed and mulch in spring. Apply a balanced general fertiliser along the base in March or April and water it in. A mulch of composted bark, 7 to 10 cm deep and kept clear of the stems, helps retain moisture through the first summer after renovation.

What to expect during recovery

The first four to eight weeks after a hard renovation cut, the hedge can look disconcertingly bare. This is entirely normal. Cotoneaster pushes new shoots from dormant buds on old wood, but this takes time, and the hedge will appear worse before it looks better. Resist the temptation to assume failure and cut again.

By mid-spring following a winter renovation cut you should see clear new growth beginning to push from the old stems. Through the first growing season the hedge will look thin and uneven. By year two it fills noticeably, particularly if the spring feed and watering were kept up. By year three the hedge should be back to a dense, maintainable shape.

Berry production will be reduced in year one after renovation, as the plant directs energy into new growth rather than fruiting. By year two, berry production usually returns and can be as good as before. The wildlife value of a healthy, well-managed cotoneaster hedge, particularly for winter thrushes arriving in East Kent from Scandinavia, is worth waiting for.
