Hedge profiles and batter: wider at the base for a healthier hedge

Most people cut their hedges with flat vertical sides. Here is why a slight taper makes a real difference to base density, and when it actually matters.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

When we trim a hedge, we aim for a slight taper from base to top: the bottom wider than the crown, by a few centimetres on each face. It is one of those things that experienced cutters do without thinking about, but which makes a measurable difference to the long-term health of the hedge.

This guide explains what a batter angle is, why it matters for specific hedge types and heights, and when a flat profile is perfectly acceptable. The comparison table below is the quickest way to see the trade-offs.

Nesting season: 1 March to 31 August

It is an offence under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 to damage or destroy a nest in active use. Nesting runs approximately 1 March to 31 August. The safe cutting window for most hedges is September to February. Only trim between March and August after checking carefully that the hedge is completely empty.

Before you start: safety

Wear eye protection against flying debris and ear defenders with any powered trimmer. Sturdy gloves are essential. For hedges above chest height, a pole or long-reach trimmer is safer than a stepladder; if a ladder must be used, have someone footing it and never overreach sideways. Keep electric-trimmer cables behind you and use an RCD adaptor. Check for wasps' nests and brambles at the base before starting.

What is a batter angle?

The word batter comes from masonry: a battered wall tapers toward the top, so the base is thicker and more stable than the crown. In hedge trimming, it means the same thing. Instead of cutting both faces perfectly vertical, you hold the trimmer at a slight angle, so the base of the hedge is wider than the top.

A typical garden hedge batter is 5 to 10 degrees from vertical on each face. For a hedge 1.5 metres tall, this means the base might be 15 to 25 cm wider per side than the crown: a modest difference that is barely visible in photographs but that changes the light conditions at the base of the hedge significantly.

Flat-sided vs tapered profile: a comparison

Wind stability Flat faces present a full surface to the wind; more flex and movement in exposed positions Slight aerodynamic improvement in exposed gardens near the coast
Ease of cutting Easier to judge and cut consistently, especially with a guide line Requires a steady hand or practice; a cane guide helps when establishing a new batter

How much taper?

For most garden hedges in Sandwich, Deal and the surrounding East Kent villages, a batter of 5 to 8 degrees on each face is enough. This translates to roughly one hand-width of difference between the base width and the top width for every metre of hedge height. It is not dramatic, and from a distance the hedge looks like a traditional straight-sided block.

Steeper batter, up to 15 degrees, is used on topiary and very formal yew hedges to create a sharper geometric silhouette. At that angle the difference between base and top is very visible and needs to be cut consistently every year to look intentional rather than accidental.

On leylandii, which can grow 60 to 90 cm per year in a mild Kent season, a batter is especially worth maintaining from the first cut. Once a tall leylandii hedge has been cut flat-sided for years, the lower growth has often died back significantly into the brown interior, and no amount of re-shaping will bring it back. Yew, beech, privet and laurel are more forgiving, but all respond well to a consistent profile.

When a flat profile is fine

Not every hedge needs a batter. Low box edging along a path or parterre looks better with crisp vertical faces: the aesthetic is geometric precision, and a taper on a 30 cm box hedge would look odd rather than refined. The same is true of any hedge under about 80 cm. The light differential at that height is small, and the base stays dense regardless of profile.

Informal hedges, hawthorn, blackthorn and native mixed hedgerow in the East Kent style, do not follow formal profiles at all: they are trimmed back to shape rather than to a geometric template, and the question of batter angle does not apply.

Establishing a batter on an existing hedge

If your hedge has been cut flat-sided for years and you want to introduce a batter, the transition is gradual. You cannot simply cut the base out wider on the first attempt: that removes established growth and leaves bare patches at the bottom. Instead, hold the trimmer at the target angle on each face and cut to the existing surface, then repeat the same angle the following year. The hedge will adjust its growth pattern toward the new profile over two or three seasons.

A string line or cane guide set at the target angle makes it much easier to cut consistently along the length of a hedge. Drive canes at each end, run a line at the target slope, and use the line as a visual reference as you work along the face.
