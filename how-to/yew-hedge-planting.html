How to plant a yew hedge

Yew (Taxus baccata) is the choice for a formal hedge that will outlast the garden. It grows on chalk and clay alike, tolerates shade, and once established needs only one cut a year. What it will not tolerate is waterlogged soil. Get drainage right from the start and the rest is straightforward.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Yew is the longest-lived and arguably the most rewarding hedging plant you can put in an English garden. The formal yew hedges around Sandwich, in the old rectory gardens of Eastry and along the lanes near Worth are some of the finest examples of what a well-maintained yew hedge becomes over decades: dense, clean-topped, and deep dark green against a bright sky.

It has a reputation for being difficult. It is not, as long as you respect two things: drainage and patience. Everything else is routine hedging practice.

Yew is toxic: read this before you plant

All parts of yew are poisonous to humans, most animals and livestock. The red berries are the most visible hazard; the fleshy aril is not itself toxic, but the seed inside is. Leaves and bark are also poisonous. This matters if you have young children in the garden, if the hedge borders a field with cattle or horses, or if you have dogs that chew plants. Yew is not automatically wrong for these situations, but plant and trim with awareness: dispose of clippings rather than composting them, and keep children and animals away during trim work.

Planting checklist at a glance

Before going into detail on each point, here is the full checklist. Tick off each item as you work through it.

  1. Check drainage: yew will not survive long in waterlogged soil
  2. Decide bare-root (November to March) or pot-grown (year-round)
  3. Order the right size: 60–90 cm whips for most garden situations
  4. Clear the planting strip of turf and all perennial weeds
  5. Prepare the trench: fork over to 25–30 cm and improve drainage if needed
  6. Set out your spacing: 3 plants per metre, 30–35 cm apart in a single row
  7. Plant at root-collar depth: not too deep, roots fanned out in the trench
  8. Firm in well, removing air pockets
  9. Apply the formative cut: reduce each plant by one-third immediately after planting
  10. Water in and mulch; fit rabbit guards if needed

1. Check drainage first

This is the most important step. Yew is exceptionally susceptible to Phytophthora root rot, a waterborne fungal disease that kills sections of hedge when roots sit in saturated soil. If your planting area holds water for extended periods after heavy rain, do not plant yew until drainage is improved.

To test drainage, dig a hole 30 cm deep, fill it with water, and return an hour later. If the water has not drained away, the drainage is insufficient for yew. Improvements include incorporating coarse grit into the trench bottom, raising the planting level slightly (a low raised bed), or installing a French drain running alongside and below the planting trench. On the well-drained chalk soils around Eastry, Worth and Ash, this is rarely an issue. On lower-lying clay ground near the Stour valley, it needs checking.

2. Bare-root or pot-grown?

Bare-root yew is available from November to March and is significantly cheaper than pot-grown plants. For a new hedge of any length, bare-root is the practical and economical choice. The plants establish readily provided they are planted promptly, the roots are kept moist until planting and the soil is properly prepared.

Pot-grown yew can be planted year-round, which makes it useful for filling gaps in an existing hedge or for small-scale additions in spring or summer. It is more expensive. If you are planting in late autumn or winter and cost matters, bare-root is the right choice.

3. Order the right size

Bare-root yew whips are sold in several sizes: 20–40 cm, 40–60 cm and 60–90 cm are the most common. Smaller whips establish more reliably than larger ones. For a new garden boundary, 60–90 cm whips are the standard choice: big enough to look like something from day one, small enough to establish without excessive care.

When the plants arrive, check that the roots are moist and not dried out. Plant within a day or two. If you cannot plant immediately, heel the plants into a shaded spot with roots covered in damp soil.

4. Clear the planting strip

Mark the planting line with canes or a string line. Strip the turf along a 60 cm-wide strip and remove all perennial weeds. Bindweed, couch grass and thistles compete directly with young yew for moisture and nutrients in the first year and are very difficult to remove once the hedge fills in. Take the time to clear thoroughly before planting.

5. Prepare the trench

Dig a continuous trench 25–30 cm wide and 20–25 cm deep along the planting line. Fork the trench bottom to break up any compaction. On chalk, incorporate some garden compost to hold moisture in the first summer. On clay, fork in coarse grit to improve drainage. If your drainage test (item 1 above) showed slow drainage, this is the moment to add a layer of coarse gravel in the trench bottom before you plant.

6. Set the spacing

Plant three yew whips per metre, spaced 30–35 cm apart in a single row. At this spacing, the plants form a recognisable hedge within three to four years and a fully knitted structure within five to seven. Yew grows more slowly than beech or hawthorn in the early years, but it is growing steadily throughout.

A double staggered row fills in faster but significantly increases costs and complicates future trimming access. For most garden situations, a single row is entirely adequate.

7. Plant at root-collar depth

The root collar (where the stem meets the roots) must sit at soil level. Fan the roots out in the trench bottom, making sure they lie flat and are not crumpled or coiled upwards. Backfill with the excavated soil in stages, firming with your boot as you go to remove air pockets. Each plant should feel firmly seated when you give it a gentle tug.

8. Firm in well

Yew dislikes being rocked by wind in its first winter. After backfilling, firm the soil around each plant thoroughly, pressing down with your heel along the whole length of the trench. In exposed positions, a temporary stake and tie for the first year provides additional stability. Check the plants again after any severe frost or gale and re-firm any that have been disturbed.

9. Apply the formative cut

Cut each plant back by roughly one-third immediately after planting. This is the step that produces a dense, well-covered hedge rather than a tall, open one. The formative cut forces energy into the base of the plant. Without it, yew tends to grow straight up, leaving a gap at the base that may never fully close.

Use clean, sharp secateurs. The cut should be diagonal, just above an outward-facing bud. The offcuts can be collected and disposed of in the green-waste bin. Do not compost yew clippings.

Yew and the nesting season

Planting is fine year-round. All trimming and shaping, however, must respect the bird nesting season. Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to damage or destroy an active nest. Yew makes excellent nesting cover. Nesting season runs approximately 1 March to 31 August. The safe trimming window is September to February.

10. Water in and mulch

Water each plant in generously after planting, even in November when the soil may already be damp. Apply a 10 cm layer of bark mulch along the full planting strip, keeping it 5 cm clear of the stems to prevent collar rot. The mulch holds moisture, suppresses weeds and moderates soil temperature.

Fit rabbit guards or spiral sleeves around each plant if rabbits are present. In open gardens on the chalk downland around Worth and Ash, this is a real risk. Rabbits will bark young yew whips in winter.

What to expect in years one to five

Year one: modest visible growth while the root system develops. Water during dry spells from April to September. Keep the mulch topped up. Do not trim in year one.

Year two: more visible growth, perhaps 20–30 cm. A very light trim in late August or early September helps thicken the structure. Keep this light: you are encouraging bushy side growth, not trying to reach the final height yet.

Years three to five: the hedge fills in noticeably each year. The characteristic dense, dark-green surface of a mature yew hedge begins to appear. From year three, a single annual trim between late July and September maintains good shape. By year five, a properly planted yew hedge in East Kent is a hedge in the full sense: solid, formal, low-maintenance.
