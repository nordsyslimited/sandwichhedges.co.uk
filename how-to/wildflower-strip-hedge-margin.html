Wildflower strip beside a hedge: seed, plug plants or leave it?

A metre or two of wildflower margin alongside a hedge turns a tidy boundary into a genuinely useful wildlife corridor. The question most people face is which approach suits their garden, their soil and the amount of time they want to spend on it.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

People who care about garden wildlife quickly discover that a hedge alone is only part of the picture. The boundary layer at the base of the hedge, the strip of ground between the lawn or path edge and the hedge stems, determines how much use birds, insects and hedgehogs actually make of the structure above it. A close-mown strip offers almost nothing. A wildflower margin offers habitat, food and shelter in a space that takes surprisingly little maintenance once established.

There are three broadly sensible approaches for establishing a wildflower margin alongside a garden hedge. This guide looks at each one honestly, with particular attention to East Kent's chalk and loam soils and the species that thrive in those conditions.

Nesting season: manage the strip carefully between March and August

Once a wildflower margin is established it becomes nesting habitat in its own right. Ground-nesting birds use long-grass margins, and the hedgehogs using the strip as a corridor may move young. Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, disturbing active nests is an offence. Carry out any ground preparation or cutting of the margin between September and February, outside the nesting window of 1 March to 31 August.

The three approaches compared

Before going into detail, here is a summary of how the three approaches compare across the factors that matter most for a garden wildflower margin.

Factor Sow from seed Plug plants Leave to self-seed
Cost Low (£5-20 for a large area) Moderate to high Nothing
Time to flowers 1 to 2 growing seasons Often flowers first year Unpredictable: 1 to 3+ years
Effort required Moderate: ground prep needed Low after planting Very low
Species diversity High (you choose the mix) Medium (limited range available) Dependent on seed bank
Reliability Good with correct prep High Variable
Best for Bare ground, new margins Established grass, quick results Rural or semi-wild sites

Sowing from seed

Who it suits

Sowing a native wildflower mix from seed gives you the most control over species composition and the best value for money over a large area. It works best on bare or mostly bare ground, which makes it the natural choice after a hedge renovation that has left the base area disturbed, or in a garden where the strip beside the hedge is currently bare soil or largely weed-free.

Ground preparation

Wildflowers are adapted to low-fertility soils, which is exactly what you find in a strip that has been in the shade beneath a hedge for years. This is actually an advantage. If the ground has been used for lawn, however, it needs preparation: remove the turf or suppress the grass before sowing, because grass competition is the main reason wildflower seed sowings fail. Yellow rattle seed sown into an existing lawn helps by weakening the grasses, but for a dense sward, stripping or flailing and raking first gives a much better result.

Lightly rake the soil surface and sow the seed at the rate on the packet, usually around 1 to 2 grams per square metre for a meadow mix, mixed with horticultural sand to help distribute it evenly. On East Kent's chalk and free-draining loam, the seed does not need covering deeply; a light raking to bring soil over the seed is enough.

When to sow

Autumn sowing (September to November) is generally more reliable for native species than spring sowing. Most native wildflower seed benefits from a cold period over winter before it germinates in spring, which autumn sowing provides naturally. Spring sowing (March to April) works for some species but may give patchy results for others.

What to expect

In the first year after an autumn sowing, germination begins in spring but many species do not flower until their second year. The strip may look like a collection of unfamiliar rosettes and young plants through most of the first year, with only a few early species such as ox-eye daisy flowering. By the second year, the full range of species in the mix should be establishing and flowering. Yellow rattle is annual and will flower in the first year from an autumn sowing.

Plug plants

Who it suits

Plug plants are small pot-grown wildflowers that you plant directly into the margin, either into bare ground or into existing grass. They cost considerably more per square metre than seed but give quicker, more predictable results and work well where introducing seed into established grass is difficult. They are also a good way to introduce specific species (such as field scabious or greater knapweed) into an existing margin that has already self-seeded with some plants but is missing others.

Planting into grass

When planting plugs into an existing grassy strip, plant them in irregular clusters rather than rows, 20 to 30 cm apart, using a dibber or bulb planter to make a hole. Firm in well and water if the weather is dry. The key to success in an established sward is to plant the plugs in autumn or early spring, when the grass competition is at its lowest, and to mow or strim the surrounding grass short before planting so the plugs are not immediately overwhelmed.

Species to look for

For East Kent's well-drained chalk and loam soils, some of the most useful plug plants for a hedge margin are ox-eye daisy, field scabious, greater knapweed, wild marjoram, bird's-foot trefoil, yellow rattle (available as plug plants as well as seed), cowslip, and common mallow. All of these are visited regularly by bumblebees, solitary bees and butterflies, and several support specialist insects found in this part of Kent.

What to expect

Plug plants often flower in their first full growing season, particularly spring-planted plugs. Autumn-planted plugs establish roots over winter and typically flower strongly the following summer. The main risk in the first year is drying out in a warm spring before the plants are established; a good soaking at planting time and one or two waterings in a dry spell in the first few weeks after planting reduces this risk considerably.

Leaving it to self-seed

Who it suits

The 'leave it' approach means simply stopping mowing a strip beside the hedge and letting whatever seeds are in the soil germinate and grow. It costs nothing, requires almost no effort and can produce surprisingly good results, particularly in older gardens or on boundaries beside farmland, where the soil seed bank may already hold native wildflower seeds from previous decades.

What you get

In a typical suburban garden, leaving a strip beside a hedge to self-seed will initially produce a mixture of coarse grasses, nettles, brambles and dock, with any wildflowers appearing slowly over several years as conditions change. This is still of real value to wildlife: nettles are the larval food plant of several butterfly species including comma and small tortoiseshell, bramble provides late-season nectar and berries, and rough grass provides cover for hedgehogs and slow worms.

In gardens on the edges of Sandwich, Worth, Woodnesborough and Eastry where field boundaries are close by, the seed bank beside an old hedge can be remarkably diverse. Stopping mowing and cutting back once a year in late September, removing the cuttings, may reveal cowslips, knapweed or field scabious already present but suppressed by regular cutting.

When to intervene

Even with the 'leave it' approach, some management is needed to stop the most vigorous plants (rank grasses, nettles, brambles, docks) crowding out everything else. An annual cut in September, with the cuttings removed, keeps the strip in balance. If the strip becomes dominated by one or two species, introducing a few targeted plug plants or broadcast-sowing yellow rattle can shift the balance towards greater diversity without requiring a full ground preparation.

Which approach for your garden?

The honest answer depends on your soil, the current state of the strip and what you want to see within the next one to three years. If the strip is bare or recently disturbed, autumn seed sowing is the most cost-effective option and will give the most species-diverse result over time. If the strip already has established grass and you want results without lifting turf, plug plants planted in autumn or early spring are the most reliable route. If you are in an older garden near farmland hedgerows and patience is not an issue, simply stopping mowing and doing an annual September cut costs nothing and may reward you considerably.

All three approaches improve on close-mown grass beside a hedge. The wildlife corridor function, connecting your hedge to the wider landscape, works at any scale and with any mix of species.
