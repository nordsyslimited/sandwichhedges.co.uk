Hedge plants for shaded gardens in Kent

A north-facing boundary or a garden enclosed by high walls is not a lost cause for a decent hedge. The species list just gets shorter. Here is what genuinely works in shade in East Kent, and what to avoid.

Updated September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

We see this in Sandwich and Deal quite a bit. The Victorian terraced houses have long narrow gardens, and the side passage or back boundary can spend most of the year in deep shade from the house or from next door's extension. People plant privet because that's what their parents planted, and it limps along thin and gappy because it really wants more light.

The good news is there are several species that genuinely prefer or tolerate low light, and some of them make excellent formal hedges. The checklist below is what we'd recommend first; the sections after it explain the detail for each one.

Quick-reference checklist: shade-tolerant hedging for Kent

Best choices for shaded plots in East Kent

  • Yew (Taxus baccata) - outstanding in deep shade; the only formal evergreen that thrives in full shade
  • Cherry laurel (Prunus laurocerasus) - large-leaved evergreen, very shade-tolerant, fast-growing
  • Portuguese laurel (Prunus lusitanica) - slightly slower than cherry laurel; elegant, dark leaves; tolerates dry shade
  • Holly (Ilex aquifolium) - native evergreen; tolerates partial and deeper shade; good for spiky security
  • Hornbeam (Carpinus betulus) - best deciduous choice for shade; holds winter leaves; excellent on clay
  • Hazel (Corylus avellana) - informal; good for dappled or north-facing light; strong wildlife value

Borderline in partial shade only: privet, box, hawthorn. Poor in shade: leylandii, photinia, griselinia, pittosporum, beech.

Gardeners' World's Monty Don covered general hedge maintenance technique in a useful episode, demonstrating the importance of matching trimming approach to species habit: The Best Hedge Cutting Tips For Your Garden (Gardening With Monty Don, YouTube). The same principle applies to shade hedges: you are managing within the plant's natural tendency, not fighting it.

Yew: the best shade hedging plant in the UK

Taxus baccata is unique among formal hedging plants in growing equally well in deep shade and full sun. It will form a dense, dark green hedge under a large tree canopy, on a permanently north-facing boundary or in the shade of a tall building. No other commonly planted formal evergreen hedge plant comes close to this shade tolerance.

Its slowness is the trade-off. Yew adds around 20-30 cm per year in reasonable conditions, and less in deep shade. On a tight budget or time frame this matters. But yew lives for centuries and looks better every decade. If you have the patience, a yew hedge in a shaded garden will outlast every other option by generations.

Yew is also the formal hedge species that most reliably clips back hard. A neglected yew can be reduced drastically and will recover over several seasons in a way that leylandii cannot. On well-drained soil (yew dislikes waterlogged ground even in shade) it is essentially the ideal formal hedge plant. See our full yew hedge care guide.

Cherry laurel and Portuguese laurel

Both laurels are reliable performers in shade. Cherry laurel (Prunus laurocerasus) is the faster of the two: in reasonable conditions it can add 45-60 cm per year, which makes it a practical choice when you need a screen quickly. It is shade-tolerant and very adaptable, growing in dry shade under tree canopy as well as in north-facing positions.

The characteristic large, glossy leaves show some brown edge damage in hard winters in exposed positions, but in a sheltered shaded garden they are largely protected from frost scorch. If you are clipping with power shears, use a hedge trimmer with blades wide enough to cut rather than tear the large leaves: torn leaves go brown at the cut edge and look untidy until the next trim.

Portuguese laurel (Prunus lusitanica) is slower, slightly more compact, and arguably more elegant. It handles dry shade better than cherry laurel, making it a good choice under the canopy of a large established tree. The smaller dark green leaves and red stems give it more texture and interest. See our guides to laurel hedge care and Portuguese laurel hedge care for trimming timing and technique.

Holly

Native holly (Ilex aquifolium) is a woodland plant that has evolved to grow under other trees. It tolerates partial shade and deeper shade better than most evergreens, and in a shaded garden its spiny foliage and red berries bring both security and seasonal interest that yew and laurel do not offer.

Holly is slower to establish than laurel and prefers reasonable moisture. On a dry shaded boundary (under the eaves of a building, for example) it can be reluctant. Give it a mulch and reliable watering for the first two years and it should establish. Female varieties berry best; for berries, plant at least one male plant nearby or choose a self-fertile variety like 'J.C. van Tol'. See our holly hedge care guide for more.

Hornbeam for deciduous shade hedging

If you want a formal deciduous hedge in shade, hornbeam (Carpinus betulus) is the answer. It is native, tolerant of heavy clay soils (common in the Worth, Ash and Woodnesborough areas of East Kent) and grows well in north-facing aspects where beech would struggle. Like beech, hornbeam holds its dried brown leaves through winter, giving it a formal look even when bare.

Hornbeam is also more tolerant of wet conditions than beech, which matters in shaded spots that tend to stay damper. If your shaded boundary is also on heavy clay, hornbeam is the clear choice over beech, which prefers chalk or well-drained soil. See our hornbeam hedge care guide and the beech vs hornbeam comparison for the full detail.

Hazel for informal shade hedging

For an informal boundary in a shaded or dappled-light garden, hazel (Corylus avellana) is worth considering. It is a woodland species, tolerant of varied shade, and its winter catkins give it interest when everything else is dormant. Hazel does not make a formal clipped hedge but it works beautifully as a laid hedge or an informal boundary planting, and it supports a large number of insect species.

It responds well to coppicing if it gets too large, so management is straightforward. For a naturalistic garden or a wildlife-friendly planting in a shaded area, hazel is often a better fit than the formal evergreen options. See our hazel hedge care guide.

What does not work well in shade

Leylandii is a sun-loving conifer and will not perform in shade. It thins out, loses its shape and grows slowly. If your shaded boundary has a leylandii hedge already in trouble, it is struggling for light, not neglect.

Photinia (red robin) puts on its characteristic red new growth in response to sun and trimming. In shade, the new growth comes through but it stays green rather than red, which defeats the purpose of planting it. It also grows poorly in shade.

Griselinia and pittosporum both prefer a sunny, sheltered site. In a shaded garden they are slow and can become sparse.

Beech is a woodland tree at home in dappled shade under other trees, but as a formal hedge it really prefers an open sunny site with good drainage. In a shaded garden it tends to be slow and uninspiring.

Privet (Ligustrum ovalifolium) is often called shade-tolerant and it is, up to a point. It manages in partial shade reasonably well, and in many Kent town gardens a privet hedge in a partially shaded side passage works fine. But in full shade it becomes leggy and thin at the base. If the shade is deep and permanent, start with one of the species above rather than hoping privet will cope.

Planting a hedge in a shaded spot

Shaded soil is often drier than it appears. Under the eaves of a building or in the rain shadow of a wall, soil can be parched even when neighbouring beds look moist. Check the soil a spade's depth down before planting: if it is dry and pale, work in extra organic matter and plan for more irrigation in the first summer.

Work in a generous amount of well-rotted compost or leaf mould when planting: shade-grown hedges benefit from richer soil because root competition from trees and walls is often higher. Mulch immediately after planting, 7-10 cm deep, and maintain the mulch for the first two seasons.

Most shade hedges are slower to establish and slower to grow than the same species in sun. Give them time. A yew or laurel in deep shade may look static for the first year; by the second or third season, if moisture and nutrients are adequate, it will begin to move.

Nesting season reminder

Shade hedges, particularly holly, laurel and yew, are favoured nesting habitat precisely because their dense foliage is sheltered and hidden. Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, cutting a hedge with an active nest is an offence. Nesting season runs roughly 1 March to 31 August. The safe cutting window for most shade hedges is September to February. Always check before trimming in late summer.
