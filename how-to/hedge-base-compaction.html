Soil compaction at the hedge base: eight signs to look for and how to fix it

Compacted soil at the hedge base cuts off water and air from the root zone. It is one of the more common and least-noticed causes of slow hedge growth, dieback from the base and persistent bare patches in otherwise healthy plants.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Most hedge problems that arrive from the base upwards, slow growth, yellowing lower foliage, sparse sections that do not fill in, have their cause in the soil rather than in the plant. Compaction is not glamorous, and it rarely announces itself dramatically, but it is worth checking before you try feeding, replanting or renovating a hedge that simply may not have the soil conditions to respond.

Eight signs of compaction at the hedge base

  1. Standing water at the hedge base that takes more than 30 minutes to drain after heavy rain
  2. Surface so hard that a bamboo cane cannot be pushed in by hand
  3. Hedge growth noticeably slower or sparser on the affected side compared to the rest of the run
  4. Lower foliage yellowing or dying back without an obvious disease or pest cause
  5. No visible earthworms when you dig a 15 cm test hole in the root zone
  6. Surface cracking in dry weather, particularly on clay-heavy soil
  7. Roots appearing at or above the soil surface
  8. No mulch present and grass or weeds growing right to the base of the hedge stems

If you can tick three or more of those signs, compaction is a reasonable working diagnosis. What follows is an explanation of why it happens, how it affects the hedge, and what actually fixes it.

Why compaction matters

Soil is not just the material that holds hedge roots in place. A healthy root zone is about 50 per cent air and water by volume, with particles and aggregates that allow oxygen and moisture to move through. When the soil compacts, that pore space collapses. Roots cannot penetrate, water sits on the surface instead of draining through, and in dry weather the surface bakes hard and sheds rainfall rather than absorbing it. The hedge above ground responds exactly as you would expect: slow growth, yellowing from the base, and eventually dieback on the worst-affected sections.

This is not a dramatic or sudden failure. Compaction builds slowly, usually over years, which is partly why it is easy to overlook as a cause. The hedge gets gradually worse rather than suddenly worse, and the connection between foot traffic or hard surface conditions and growth decline is not always obvious.

What causes compaction in East Kent hedge bases

Foot traffic close to the hedge line is the most common cause. Collecting clippings, using the hedge base as a path, children using the gap between hedge and wall: even light foot traffic repeated over several seasons compacts the top 10-20 cm of soil significantly. The root zone of most hedges extends 30-60 cm either side of the stem line, well into what most people would consider general garden ground.

Heavy clay soil is a factor in a significant part of East Kent. The clay vale between Eastry and Woodnesborough, the lower-lying land behind Sandwich and parts of Deal's hinterland have clay-heavy soils that compact readily once the organic matter content drops. Chalk-based soils around Sandwich Bay and Worth are less prone to deep compaction but can seal at the surface after heavy rain on bare ground.

Loss of mulch and organic matter leaves the surface vulnerable to surface cap: the impact of rainfall on bare soil breaks down soil aggregates and seals the surface. Grass and weeds growing to the base compete for moisture and add their own root network that can lock the soil surface further.

The fix: aeration first, mulch second

Aeration

For mild to moderate compaction, where the cane goes in with some effort and water does drain eventually, a garden fork is sufficient. Push it vertically into the soil every 15-20 cm across the full width of the root zone, to a depth of 15-20 cm. Rock it gently forward and back to open the holes without lifting and inverting the soil. Work methodically across the whole base, not just the worst patches. The holes allow water and air to reach the root zone immediately, and earthworms colonise them quickly once the conditions improve.

For severe compaction on clay soils, where the cane will not penetrate at all and standing water persists for hours, a hollow-tine fork removes plugs of soil and gives more lasting relief. If you have access to a hollow-tine aerator, use it. Otherwise a stout border fork is better than nothing, and the approach is the same.

The best time to aerate is in autumn, when the soil is moist but not waterlogged. November is good for most of East Kent. Avoid aerating in a dry summer when clay soil may shatter and produce hard clods rather than opening cleanly.

Mulching after aeration

Apply a 7-10 cm layer of coarse bark mulch or wood chip across the full root zone immediately after aerating, whilst the holes are still open. Keep it 10 cm clear of the hedge stems to avoid promoting rot at the base. The mulch protects the loosened surface from re-compacting under rain impact, retains moisture during the dry months (June to August in East Kent can be genuinely dry, with villages around Sandwich Bay seeing well under 600 mm of rain a year), and encourages the earthworm activity that continues the improvement naturally.

Mulch needs refreshing every two to three years as it breaks down into the soil. The decomposed material adds organic matter, which is part of the long-term improvement in soil structure.

Preventing re-compaction

  • Do not walk on the mulched root zone. If the hedge base sits in a natural traffic route, place a simple board or paving slab path adjacent to the hedge rather than on its root zone.
  • If grass is growing to the base, suppress it with a 30 cm clear strip of mulch each side of the hedge line.
  • Refresh the mulch layer every two to three years, before it has fully broken down and the surface is left bare again.

When compaction is part of a bigger problem

Compaction alone can slow a hedge substantially, but it sometimes accompanies other issues: poor drainage further down the soil profile, old construction rubble or hardstanding at a shallow depth (common in East Kent garden plots on former farm or orchard land around Sandwich and Ash), or root damage from previous works. If aeration and mulching have no visible effect after one growing season, it is worth checking the sub-surface conditions with a deeper dig to 30-40 cm, or getting advice on the specific site.
