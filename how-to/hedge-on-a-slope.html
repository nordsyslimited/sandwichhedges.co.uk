Trimming and planting a hedge on a slope: a decision guide

Slopes introduce two separate questions: whether the top of your hedge should follow the gradient or step down in levels, and how to trim safely when the ground is uneven under your feet. Here is a practical guide to both, built around the decisions you actually need to make before you pick up a trimmer.

Updated September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Sloping gardens are common in East Kent, from the gentle inclines leading down from the chalk ridge around Worth and Kingsdown to the steeper drops on the seaward side of Sandwich Bay and the undulating plots in villages like Ash and Woodnesborough. A hedge on any of these slopes needs slightly more thought than one on flat ground, but the decisions are not complicated once you have a framework.

This guide walks through the main forks in the road: how steep is your slope, what approach suits it, how do you plant and establish the hedge, and how do you trim it safely once it is growing. Work through the decisions in order and you will end up with a clear plan before you start.

Decision 1: how steep is the slope?

Measure the slope before you make any other decision. Stand at one end of where the hedge will run and stretch a string line or straight board to the other end. Use a spirit level to hold the string horizontal. Measure the vertical drop from the string to the ground at the far end. Divide that drop by the horizontal distance: the result is the gradient.

  • Under 10 cm per metre (1 in 10, or about 6 degrees): gentle slope. The gradient is subtle enough that both follow-the-slope and step-and-level options look similar. Work as you would on flat ground, with minor adjustments to your cutting line.
  • 10 to 25 cm per metre (roughly 1 in 4 to 1 in 10): moderate slope. The choice between a hedge that follows the contour and one that steps down in sections matters here and becomes a visible stylistic decision. Both are manageable.
  • More than 25 cm per metre (steeper than about 1 in 4): steep slope. A follow-the-slope approach is almost always the right answer here. Step-and-level on a very steep slope requires very tall stepped sections, which become difficult to trim evenly. Planting and safety when trimming need more care.

Decision 2: should the top follow the slope or step down in levels?

Monty Don covers the fundamentals of hedge establishment and shape in the BBC Gardeners' World video How to grow a hedge (youtube.com/watch?v=QUrnehX5kw0), which is useful background before you commit to a shape. The slope decision then comes down to the look you want and the context of the hedge.

Follow-the-slope (informal, continuous gradient)

The top of the hedge runs at a constant height above the ground, following the gradient of the slope. Looking along the hedge from the side, the top is at an angle, like a wedge. From the front, the hedge looks like any other hedge: consistent height, consistent depth.

Best suited to: rural or semi-rural boundaries, wildlife hedges, informal gardens, long boundary hedges where you want to blend with the landscape rather than make an architectural statement. Also easier to maintain, because the cutting height at any point is the same from the ground up: you do not need to recalibrate your eye for different sections.

Step-and-level (formal, tiered sections)

The hedge is the same height above sea level for a section, then drops to a lower level for the next section, creating a stepped profile from the side. Each flat section is level and formal. From the front, the steps are invisible: you see only a flat top within each section.

Best suited to: formal front gardens, period properties, situations where the hedge is a designed element rather than simply a boundary. The steps need to be deliberate and precise: an unintentional or ragged step looks worse than a continuous slope. Plan the step positions before the hedge is established so the plants at each level grow to the right target height.

Which looks right on your specific slope?

The simplest test: hold a string line along the proposed hedge line and look at it from the road or from the garden's main viewpoint. If the angle of the slope catches the eye and draws attention to itself, step-and-level will look more intentional. If the slope is gentle enough to read as flat at a glance, follow-the-slope will be almost indistinguishable. When in doubt, follow the slope: it is easier, more forgiving, and reads as natural.

Decision 3: which species for your slope?

All common hedging species will grow on slopes, but some work better than others on steeper or drier ground.

Hornbeam is the best general-purpose choice for moderate to steep slopes in East Kent. Its root system is vigorous and multi-directional, which helps stabilise the slope. It tolerates the free-draining chalk and sandy loam soils around Sandwich, Worth and Eastry, and copes with drier conditions better than beech. One cut per year, in August.

Beech (including copper beech) works well on well-drained slopes with neutral to slightly acidic soil. On the chalk soils of the Worth and Kingsdown ridge, beech is at home. Avoid beech on south-facing slopes in heavy or wet soil: it dislikes waterlogging. One cut per year, in late July or August.

Hawthorn and blackthorn are the right choice for exposed, rural or coastal slopes. Both are tough, stabilise soil with a thorny thicket of roots and stems, and provide wildlife value. Neither needs a formal cutting regime: an annual trim to shape is enough. Good for the field edges around Ash and Woodnesborough where a stock-proof boundary is the primary requirement.

Privet (Ligustrum ovalifolium) is a practical choice for formal garden hedges on slopes in Sandwich, Deal and the villages. It establishes faster than hornbeam, roots well on slopes, and responds to cutting reliably. The main limitation is that it is semi-evergreen rather than fully deciduous or fully evergreen: in hard winters, East Kent's inland privet hedges can lose more leaves than coastal ones. Two or three light cuts per growing season for a tight formal finish.

Yew is excellent on slopes once established, particularly on well-drained chalk. Deep roots, tolerance of dry conditions and very long life make it the right choice for a permanent feature hedge where you do not mind waiting three to five years for it to fill out. Avoid on steep slopes where you want quick establishment: yew is slow to start.

Leylandii is problematic on slopes. The shallow horizontal root system that makes leylandii a risk on dry slopes (it blows over more easily than deeper-rooted species) also reduces its soil-stabilising value. If you need a fast screen and leylandii is the only option, plant on a gentler section of the slope and stake thoroughly for the first three years.

Decision 4: how to plant on a slope

Nesting season: 1 March to 31 August

If you are removing an existing hedge or scrub to plant a new one, check for active nests before cutting any vegetation. The safe window for clearing ground vegetation is September to February. Bare-root hedging plants are available November to March, which aligns neatly with the safe working window. Container-grown plants can be planted any time the ground is workable.

On a gentle slope, plant exactly as you would on flat ground. On a moderate to steep slope:

  1. Mark the line. Use canes and a string line to mark where the hedge will run. On a slope, the string must be pulled tight or it will follow the slope rather than marking a straight horizontal line: run the string from cane to cane in short sections and check it from the side.
  2. Create individual planting terraces on steep ground. For each plant, dig a horizontal shelf into the slope about 30 cm wide and 30 cm deep. The plant goes into this terrace sitting level, not angled to follow the slope. The level shelf helps water pool around the roots rather than running straight past them.
  3. Plant at the correct spacing for the species. 30 to 45 cm for privet, hawthorn or blackthorn; 45 to 60 cm for hornbeam, beech or yew.
  4. Water thoroughly after planting and apply a 5 cm mulch of bark or wood chip around each plant, kept clear of the stem. On a slope, the mulch also slows surface runoff.
  5. Consider staking on exposed or steep slopes. On slopes facing prevailing south-westerly winds around Sandwich Bay and the coast, even modest plants benefit from a short stake in the first year to prevent root rock.

Decision 5: how to trim safely on a slope

Before you start: safety on slopes

  • Footing first. On a slope, your footing is the most important safety factor. Assess the ground before you switch the trimmer on: wet grass, loose soil, leaves or moss all increase slip risk significantly.
  • Never work with your back to the slope downhill. If you slip, a running trimmer blade should not be between you and the ground. Face the slope and work across it or uphill.
  • Pole trimmer from below, not ladder from above. A long-reach pole trimmer used from a stable position below or to the side of the hedge is far safer on a slope than a ladder balanced on uneven ground. Ladders on slopes should be avoided altogether.
  • RCD adaptor: mandatory for all corded electric tools used outdoors.
  • Eye protection, ear defenders, gloves.

Trimming a follow-the-slope hedge

Set your cutting height as a constant distance from the ground, measured at each point. The easiest method is to use a single guide cane at your shoulder height at the start of each working section, moved along every few metres. Keep the trimmer bar at a constant angle relative to the slope, not relative to horizontal. Take your time and check back along the cut from the end of the section: the eye adjusts to a slope quickly and the most common error is a slight unintended level creep that makes the hedge taller at the bottom of the slope than the top.

Trimming a step-and-level hedge

Use a string line for every level section, without exception. Pull a string line at the target height between two canes at each end of the section and use it as your cutting guide. Check the string is truly horizontal with a spirit level before you start cutting. On a long section (over 4 metres), the string will sag slightly in the centre: check the midpoint too and adjust if needed. Cut the sides of the steps clean and vertical: an angled or ragged step edge undoes the formal appearance the entire approach is meant to create.

Steep-slope trimming: work in sections

On steep ground, divide the hedge into working sections of two to three metres. Cut a section, then step back, plant your feet, and reassess before moving to the next section. Fatigue on slopes leads to sloppy footing: short sections with deliberate repositioning between them are more effective and safer than trying to cover long runs without stopping. Have a second person present if the slope is steep or the ground is wet.
