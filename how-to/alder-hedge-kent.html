Alder hedge in Kent

Common alder is one of the few hedging species that actually improves wet, heavy ground. A species guide for the low-lying gardens around Sandwich Bay, Worth and the Stour valley.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Heavy clay and seasonally wet ground are common in the lower-lying parts of East Kent, particularly around Worth, Sandwich Bay and the Stour valley. Most popular hedging species (privet, laurel, beech, hornbeam) will tolerate brief waterlogging but struggle with persistently wet ground.

Common alder (Alnus glutinosa) is the exception. It is a UK native that grows naturally along riverbanks and in wet woodland, and it makes a genuinely good hedge for the kind of ground where other species fail.

Why alder works where others don't

Alder has root nodules containing nitrogen-fixing bacteria (Frankia alni), which means it can colonise poor, wet, nitrogen-deficient soils and gradually improve them. On the kind of heavy, poorly drained ground found in some Sandwich Bay gardens and along the Stour corridor, this matters.

It is semi-deciduous: the leaves drop late (November) and the small woody catkins and seed cones provide food for siskins, redpolls and other finches through winter. As a boundary hedge, it also offers decent cover for nesting birds from early spring.

Alder vs alternatives for wet and heavy ground

If you're choosing between species for a difficult wet site, here is how alder stacks up against the most common alternatives.

Alder vs common osier willow

Willow is faster at 1 to 1.5 metres per year, but needs hard cutting every year or it quickly becomes unmanageable. Alder is more docile, needs cutting once a year, and has a tighter, denser growth habit for a garden boundary. For a formal or semi-formal hedge, alder wins easily.

Alder vs elder (Sambucus nigra)

Elder tolerates wet ground well and grows fast. The flowers and berries have excellent wildlife value. However elder can become ungainly and the wood is brittle; it needs renewal cutting every few years and doesn't hold a tidy line the way alder does. Use elder in a mixed native hedge rather than as a single-species boundary.

Alder vs hazel

Hazel prefers well-drained soil and will grow on clay but not on genuinely waterlogged ground. It is a better wildlife hedge on dry or loamy soils. If your ground is only seasonally damp (heavy clay that holds water in winter but dries out in summer), hazel or hawthorn are probably the better choice. If the ground stays wet for weeks at a time, go with alder.

Common alder vs Italian alder

Italian alder (Alnus cordata) grows faster at 60 to 90 cm per year compared with 30 to 45 cm for the native species. It tolerates thinner soils and is good for fast screening on sites that are heavy but not genuinely waterlogged. For persistently wet ground or low-lying East Kent marshes, though, the common alder is the right choice. Italian alder is better suited to sites that are heavy but not wet.

Planting and establishment

Plant bare-root whips in autumn to winter (October to March); alder establishes well from bare root and this is the cheapest and most reliable approach. For an informal hedge, space plants at 60 to 90 cm apart in a single row. For a more formal or dense screen, use 45 cm spacing or a double staggered row.

In wet ground, no soil improvement is needed: alder wants the conditions other species find difficult. Keep it watered in its first summer if conditions are dry, which is less likely on wet sites, but worth noting if the summer turns out dry.

Nesting season: 1 March to 31 August

Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 it is an offence to cut or damage a hedge containing an active nest. Check the hedge carefully before any summer tidy. The main annual cut for alder falls in late August or September, after most birds have fledged.

Trimming and maintenance

Once established (year 2 onwards), alder needs one cut a year. Late August to September is the right time, after the nesting season and while there is still enough warmth for the cut ends to harden before winter. Alder grows at a measured pace, so one cut keeps it tidy for twelve months without difficulty.

Use a hedge trimmer for a tidy, battered profile (slightly wider at the base than the top). Alder can also be cut hard if the hedge has outgrown its space: it is a coppice species and responds well to being cut back to 30 to 40 cm from the ground, breaking strongly from old wood. This is best done in winter, when the plant is dormant.

Common questions

Can you use common alder as a garden hedge?

Yes. Common alder (Alnus glutinosa) makes an effective informal to semi-formal hedge for wet, heavy or low-lying ground. It can be maintained at 1.5 to 2.5 metres and responds well to an annual late-summer cut. It is a UK native, provides cover and food for birds and insects, and fixes atmospheric nitrogen, improving the soil over time.

How often should an alder hedge be trimmed?

Once a year in late summer or early autumn, usually August or September after the nesting season, is enough for most alder hedges. Alder grows at around 30 to 60 cm per year once established, so an annual cut keeps it at the size you want. If you want a sharper, more formal look you can add a light tidy in June, checking first for active nests.

Does common alder tolerate waterlogged soil?

Yes. Common alder is one of the most waterlogging-tolerant hedging species available in the UK. It naturally grows along riverbanks and in carr woodland where the ground is seasonally flooded. Its root system hosts nitrogen-fixing bacteria, so it can improve thin, wet soils over time. Italian alder (Alnus cordata) is more tolerant of dry periods but less suited to genuinely wet ground.

What is the difference between common alder and Italian alder as hedging?

Common alder (Alnus glutinosa) is the UK native and the right choice for genuinely wet or waterlogged sites. It grows more slowly, perhaps 30 to 45 cm per year, and has significant wildlife value. Italian alder (Alnus cordata) is a faster grower, 60 to 90 cm per year, and tolerates drier or thinner soils, but provides less wildlife benefit and is not native. For East Kent coastal marshes and low-lying ground around Sandwich Bay, the common alder is usually the better fit.
