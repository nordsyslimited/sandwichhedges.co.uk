Pre-cut hedge preparation: 10 checks before you start trimming

The five minutes before the trimmer starts often decide how the whole job goes. A checklist from people who do this five days a week across Sandwich, Deal and the villages of East Kent.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Ten checks before you pick up the trimmer

  1. Check the date: nesting season runs 1 March to 31 August
  2. Walk the hedge: look and listen for birds flying in or out of the same spot
  3. Check the weather: dry day, light wind, avoid frost or temperatures above 25°C
  4. Inspect the blades: clean, free of hardened sap, no missing or damaged teeth
  5. Test blade sharpness: should cut cleanly, not pull or tear stems
  6. Electric trimmer: test the RCD adaptor before you start
  7. PPE: eye protection, ear defenders, cut-resistant gloves, non-slip boots
  8. Clear the base: stones, wire and long grass before you start
  9. Boundaries: let a neighbour know if cutting near a shared fence or wall
  10. Cuttings plan: know where the trimmings will go before they're on the ground

The difference between a job that goes well and one that doesn't is usually the five minutes spent before the trimmer starts. Seasoned hedge cutters in East Kent develop these checks until they're automatic: they happen without thinking, every single visit, before the first blade run. This guide breaks them down.

Before the blade touches anything

The nest check

Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is a criminal offence to damage or destroy a nest that is in active use. The main nesting season runs roughly from 1 March to 31 August. Before cutting at any point in that window, the hedge needs a proper check, not a cursory glance.

Walk slowly along both sides of the hedge, at the same height as the hedge top if you can manage it, and watch for birds flying repeatedly in and out of the same spot. Listen for alarm calls. A gentle scratch or brush of the outer branches can prompt a sitting bird to move; if it does, the hedge is occupied and cutting must wait. Even outside the main window, a quick check in early September is worthwhile: wood pigeons, house sparrows and collared doves occasionally raise late broods.

See our detailed guide to wildlife-friendly hedge trimming for the full legal picture.

Weather

Wet foliage drags on trimmer blades and clogs the cut. On a damp day, privet and laurel in particular will produce a sticky green paste rather than clean clippings. Frost makes leaves brittle and can scorch the cut tips on evergreens: the exposed tissue freezes before it can begin to seal. High winds push clippings across neighbouring gardens, and gusts above about 15 mph make it hard to maintain a straight cutting line.

The ideal conditions are a dry morning in still air, with temperatures between 7°C and 20°C. In East Kent, September and October deliver this more reliably than any other month, which is part of why they are the peak cutting season here.

Tools in order

Blade inspection

Sap from previous cuts is mildly corrosive and, once hardened, makes the blades sticky. Hardened sap causes blades to drag and can make them seize momentarily mid-cut, which is both a poor result and hard on the motor. Before cutting, wipe the blades with a dry cloth and apply a light spray of multi-purpose oil or blade oil along the full length of both cutting bars. Work the blades back and forth by hand a couple of times to distribute it.

Check for missing or chipped teeth. A single missing tooth on a reciprocating blade causes the opposing tooth to cut unevenly, leaving a ragged finish and accelerating wear on the entire bar.

Sharpness test

Draw a small green stem slowly through the blades by hand, with the power off. A sharp blade shears the stem cleanly with moderate pressure. A blade that pushes the stem sideways before cutting it, or that requires you to work it back and forth, needs attention. Options: replace the blade bar if teeth are badly worn, have it professionally re-ground (most tool service shops do this), or use a fine flat file on the front face of each tooth. Filing is a reasonable job for blades that are dulled rather than damaged.

Electric cable and RCD

For corded electric trimmers, check the full length of the cable for nicks, cracks or damage, paying particular attention near the plug and the strain relief at the tool end. Any visible damage means the cable needs replacing before use. The RCD (residual current device) adaptor is non-negotiable for any corded power tool used outdoors: plug it into the socket first, test the trip button, and then plug the trimmer into it. It will not prevent all accidents, but it will cut the power in a fraction of a second if the cable is severed.

Personal protection

Eye protection

Hedge trimmer blades travel fast and fling stem fragments, seed casings and dried leaf matter in all directions. Safety glasses are the minimum; full-wrap goggles give better coverage for dense hedges or any work where you're cutting above shoulder height and debris falls towards your face. Contact lens wearers should switch to glasses or use wrap-around goggles.

Ear protection

Even a 20-minute run on a petrol trimmer exceeds the daily noise exposure limits recommended for safe hearing. Electric trimmers are quieter but still loud enough to cause damage over a long session. Ear defenders or plugs are not optional if you're doing more than a brief tidy. Buy a pair of decent ear defenders and keep them with the trimmer.

Gloves

Ordinary gardening gloves are not adequate for hedge trimming work. The blades of a trimmer travel fast enough that a momentary contact is serious. Cut-resistant gloves (look for EN 388 Level 3 or above) provide meaningful protection while still allowing the grip needed to control the tool. They matter most for spiny species: hawthorn, holly, berberis and pyracantha leave sharp fragments in the cut line that make even non-motorised work painful without proper gloves.

Boots

Non-slip soles are important when working on wet grass or sloped ground. Steel-toe boots are worth considering for hedge work: you are carrying a heavy powered tool near your feet, often on uneven ground, and a stumble can bring the tool down. Flip-flops and training shoes are both common sights at accident and emergency departments after garden tool incidents.

Site preparation

Clear the base

A stone or old piece of wire caught in a hedge trimmer blade can shatter a tooth, damage the bar or kick back at the operator. It takes 60 seconds to walk along the base of the hedge and kick anything visible clear: stones, bricks, wire, old plant labels. Pay attention to the section at ground level where the hedge meets the lawn, as small objects migrate into this zone over time without anyone noticing.

Boundaries

If the hedge runs along a boundary between two properties, a brief word with the neighbour before you start avoids misunderstandings about noise and clippings landing in their garden. It also means someone else knows you're doing powered work, which matters in the event of an accident. Under the High Hedges legislation in the Anti-social Behaviour Act 2003, tall evergreen hedges over 2 metres on a boundary can be the subject of a council complaint if they block light or access. See our guide to neighbour hedge-height law for the detail.

Cuttings plan

Hedge clippings need to go somewhere, and it is easier to plan this before they are on the ground than after. For small hedges, a garden waste bin or compost heap may be enough. For larger hedges, a garden waste bag alongside the work makes collection faster than raking up afterwards. Privet and laurel clippings in particular are heavy and dense; underestimating the volume is a common mistake. If you are arranging a skip or a garden waste collection, book it before the job rather than after.

Lewis Garden Services Ltd's YouTube channel (over 130,000 subscribers) published a detailed walkthrough on what to check before anyone cuts a hedge. It covers tool setup and professional preparation habits that apply to any trimmer and any hedge.

The one legal check that tops everything

It is a criminal offence under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs roughly from 1 March to 31 August. No other preparation check can substitute for the nest check at the start of this list during that window. If there are birds in the hedge, the job waits. See our guide to wildlife-friendly hedge trimming for what to look for and what to do if you find a nest.
