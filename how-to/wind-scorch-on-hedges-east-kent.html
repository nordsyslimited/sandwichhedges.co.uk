Wind and salt scorch on hedges: what it is and what to do

A hedge that looks singed down one side after a blustery week is usually wind or salt scorch, and usually it recovers. The trick is not to panic and cut it back.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

After a hard south-westerly, or an onshore gale that carries salt inland, we often get calls from Sandwich Bay, Deal and the open ground around Worth and Ash about a hedge that has gone brown on one side. The leaves look burnt, the hedge looks sick, and the first thought is to cut it all back. In most cases that is the wrong move.

This guide covers how to recognise scorch, how to separate it from the problems that look similar, and what helps the hedge recover.

Nesting season: check before you cut

Under the UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs roughly 1 March to 31 August. The safe cutting window for most hedges is September to February. A light summer trim is only reasonable if you have watched the hedge and checked it is empty first. See how to check a hedge for nests.

What wind and salt scorch looks like

  • Edges and tips first. Leaf margins go brown and crisp, often with a clear line between green and brown.
  • One side only. The damage sits on the face looking into the wind, and is usually worst at the top and on corners of the hedge.
  • Sudden. It appears within days of a gale rather than creeping across the hedge over a season.
  • Twigs still alive. Scratch a twig with a thumbnail. Green underneath means living wood.

Why it happens

Strong wind dries leaves faster than the roots can replace the water, especially when the ground is dry or cold. Salt spray adds to it: in a gale close to the sea, salt can be carried well inland, and it burns leaf tissue and draws water out of it. Evergreens carry their leaves through winter gales, so they are hit harder than deciduous hedges, which are leafless when the worst of the weather arrives.

Newly planted hedges suffer most because their roots have not spread yet. If yours went in within the last two years, see newly planted hedge care.

What to do

  1. Leave the leaves on for now. Scorched foliage still shades the buds underneath. Cutting hard into a stressed hedge adds a second shock.
  2. Water if the ground is dry. A soaking at the base helps the roots supply the leaves again. Do not water the foliage with tap water as a salt cure on a long hedge; it is not practical and the roots matter more.
  3. Mulch the base. A layer of mulch keeps moisture in and the roots cooler. See mulching hedges.
  4. Hold off feeding until spring. Pushing soft growth in late autumn gives the next gale something tender to burn.
  5. Wait for spring growth, then tidy. Once buds break, cut out twigs that stayed dead. Shape the hedge at the usual time, inside the September to February window or after a nest check.

Stopping it happening every year

If the same hedge scorches every winter, the species or the site needs rethinking. A semi-permeable shelter row upwind slows the gale without causing turbulence, and tougher coastal species handle the exposure better.
