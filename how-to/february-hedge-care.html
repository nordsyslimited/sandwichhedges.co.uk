February hedge care in Kent: last chance before nesting season

The first of March is closer than it feels. February is your final window for renovation cuts, the last chance to feed and mulch before growth begins, and the month when professional hedge contractors still have good availability. Here is the complete February checklist for East Kent gardens.

Updated September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

February hedge care: your checklist at a glance

  • Finish renovation cuts: complete any hard reduction on deciduous hedges (beech, hornbeam, hawthorn, hazel, blackthorn, field maple) before the end of February.
  • Box: trim in late February just before spring growth starts, for a clean start to the season.
  • Do NOT cut: leylandii, other conifers, or tender evergreens (pittosporum, choisya, escallonia). Wait until May or later.
  • Feed: apply a balanced slow-release fertiliser along the root run in late February, ready for spring uptake.
  • Mulch: 7 to 10 cm of bark chip or well-rotted compost along the base, kept 5 cm clear of stems.
  • Finish bare-root planting: last chance for bare-root stock; order now if you have not already.
  • Check for frost damage: walk the hedge and note sections that browned or died back over winter.
  • Book autumn work: February is quiet for contractors; good availability before summer fills the diary.
  • Quick visual check before cutting: robins and doves can start nest-building in mild late February.

February is not quite December and not quite March. Some of the dormant-season tasks that were wide open in January now have a clock running. The 1 March nesting-season deadline is a firm legal cut-off for most hedge trimming, and February is the month to make sure everything that needs doing gets done. But February is also when growth begins to stir, the soil starts to warm, and the feeding and mulching jobs that felt premature in January now make clear sense.

Around the Sandwich Bay and Deal coastline, the mild Channel winters mean some early-nesting species, particularly robins, can begin building in the last ten days of February in a warm year. By the middle of the month, it is worth casting an eye over the hedge before you cut, just to be sure. Inland at Woodnesborough, Eastry and Ash, the calendar tends to run a week or two later, but 1 March is a firm rule regardless of local conditions.

The 1 March deadline is approaching

Under the UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. The nesting season runs roughly 1 March to 31 August. From 1 March, any hedge cutting that damages an active nest is a criminal offence, even accidentally. Treat the end of February as your final deadline for all renovation cuts and significant trimming. From March, hedge work shifts to feeding, mulching and maintenance of species with genuinely no nesting activity.

Before you start: safety

  • Quick nest check: watch the hedge for a minute before cutting. Look for birds flying repeatedly in and out of the same spot, particularly in sheltered south-facing sections.
  • Eye protection: safety glasses or goggles for any powered cutting.
  • Ear defenders: petrol and electric trimmers both exceed safe noise levels quickly.
  • Gloves: close-fitting and cut-resistant for spiny species such as hawthorn and blackthorn.
  • RCD adaptor: essential for corded electric trimmers, no exceptions.
  • Ladder awareness: ground can still be soft and wet in February. A long-reach pole trimmer is far safer than a ladder for the top of a taller hedge.

The February tasks, one by one

1. Complete renovation cuts: the last window

Any hard renovation that you planned but did not complete in January needs to happen now. Deciduous hedges, beech, hornbeam, hawthorn, hazel, blackthorn and field maple, are still fully dormant at the start of February and can take hard renovation cuts without stress. By the third week of February, the first signs of bud movement are often visible on hawthorn and hazel in East Kent, which is a signal that the dormant season is drawing to a close. You can still cut, but the optimal window is narrowing.

If your hedge needed renovation but you have run out of time, do not panic. A gentle tidy in March is possible if the hedge is clearly empty of nesting birds, and a proper renovation in the following autumn (September onwards) is the safest option. One season of extra growth on a hedge that should have been renovated in February will not cause irreversible damage.

2. Box hedges: cut now, before the growth surge

Box is worth a tidy in late February, just ahead of the spring growth surge. Box begins pushing new growth early, often before most other hedging species, and a late-February cut gives it a clean line to grow from through spring. It also removes any dead growth that became visible over winter, so the new season starts with a fresh surface.

Keep the cut light rather than a hard reduction. Box responds better to regular light trimming than to infrequent hard cuts. If your box hedge has got significantly out of shape, a harder trim in February is fine, but do it before mid-February to give the cut surfaces time to harden before the first warm days of March.

If you have box blight in your garden, see our box blight guide before cutting. Spreading infected clippings to healthy sections through careless pruning is one of the main routes by which blight spreads across a garden.

3. Holly and yew: light tidy if needed

Both holly and yew can be lightly trimmed in February. Keep it brief: the aim is a tidy rather than a renovation. Avoid cutting either species hard in February if a frost is forecast within the next few days. Both tolerate cold well when growing normally, but freshly cut surfaces are more vulnerable than intact growth. Early February in a settled period is fine; late February in a bitter cold snap is riskier.

4. Feeding: late February is the right time

Late February to early March is the single best window for feeding an established hedge. The hedge is still technically dormant, but the soil is warming and the roots are beginning to stir. A balanced slow-release granular fertiliser broadcast along the root run on both sides of the hedge and lightly raked in will be in position as growth breaks in March.

Use a balanced formulation, 7-7-7 or similar NPK, for a hedge in good condition. For a hedge that is growing slowly or looking thin, a slightly higher-nitrogen spring feed is worth using this year. Hedges on the chalk soils around Eastry, Ash and Woodnesborough can benefit from a magnesium supplement alongside the main feed, as chalk soils are often deficient in magnesium: Epsom salts at 30 g per square metre addresses this.

Do not feed leylandii and other conifers with a high-nitrogen fertiliser in February. Conifers are not actively growing yet, and excess nitrogen on dormant conifer roots can cause more harm than good. A balanced slow-release feed for conifers is fine in late February; leave high-nitrogen products until May.

5. Mulching: lock in the moisture now

Immediately after feeding, apply a mulch along the hedge base. A depth of 7 to 10 cm of bark chip, composted wood or well-rotted manure, kept 5 cm clear of the main stems to prevent collar rot, retains soil moisture through February and March when rainfall in East Kent can be unreliable. The free-draining sandy and chalk soils around Sandwich, Worth and the coastal strip lose moisture quickly as the soil temperature rises in late winter.

February mulching also suppresses the early-season weeds that begin germinating in the warmer patches of soil from mid-February onwards in a mild year, which saves considerable work later in the spring.

6. Finish bare-root planting: last chance

The bare-root planting window closes at the end of February. February-planted bare-root hedging stock can still establish well in East Kent, particularly in the well-drained soils around Sandwich and Worth that warm up quickly in late winter. However, you need to plant now rather than waiting: popular species, privet, hawthorn, beech, hornbeam and blackthorn, often sell out at reputable suppliers in the first half of February.

If you need to plant but the ground is frozen, heel the plants into a spare patch of unfrozen soil rather than leaving them in their packaging. They will wait there for a week or two without harm until the ground softens. See our guide to planting bare-root hedges for technique.

7. Check for frost damage

Walk the hedge in February and note any sections that have browned, died back, or suffered tip scorch over winter. Tender evergreens, particularly pittosporum, escallonia and choisya, are the most common casualties. In most cases, tip scorch on these species clears as new growth comes through in late March. Do not trim frost-damaged sections until you can see new green growth at the base of the damaged stems. Cutting brown wood before knowing whether it will regenerate risks removing sections that would have recovered.

If leylandii or conifer sections have gone brown over winter, see our conifer hedge going brown guide before doing anything. Brown patches on leylandii require a specific diagnosis and approach that is different from deciduous frost damage.

8. Book autumn trimming before the diary fills

February is the best month to book professional hedge trimming for the autumn. September, October and November are the peak months for hedge contractors in East Kent, and diaries fill from July onwards. Booking in February means a good choice of dates, a chance to discuss any renovation work ahead of time, and no risk of missing the autumn window. Most jobs around Sandwich, Deal, Worth, Ash, Woodnesborough, Eastry and Sandwich Bay can be quoted from a photograph for straightforward domestic hedges.

The video Pre-Nesting Season Hedge Reduction: Essential Steps for Gardeners (published February 2025) covers exactly the practical decisions around the last-chance reduction, including how to assess whether a hedge needs work and what a proper pre-nesting cut involves. Well worth watching before tackling a significant reduction for the first time.

February has a deadline that December and January do not. Use the month well, finish the cuts you planned, and put the trimmer away before March brings the birds back.
