Underplanting a hedge: ground cover for the hedge base

The strip of soil at the base of an established hedge is shady, dry and often invaded by weeds. Planted well, it can become one of the tidiest parts of a garden. Here is what actually works.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

The ground directly under an established hedge tends to be one of the most difficult spots in a garden. The hedge canopy intercepts rain, so the soil dries out faster than anywhere else. The roots of the hedge itself compete aggressively for water and nutrients. Shade deepens as the hedge matures. Left alone, the strip fills with nettles, goosegrass and couch grass, because those plants are adapted to exactly this kind of difficult edge habitat.

The answer is not to fight the conditions but to plant something that accepts them. Several reliable ground cover plants thrive in dry shade, spread steadily to crowd out weeds, and ask for almost nothing once established. The comparison below covers the most practical options for East Kent gardens.

What the hedge base environment is actually like

Before choosing a plant, it helps to be clear about what you are dealing with. An established privet or leylandii hedge creates deep shade and very dry conditions: rainfall is deflected by the canopy and the dense root mat absorbs most of what does get through. A deciduous beech or hornbeam hedge is a different situation, semi-shaded and less root-competitive. A native mixed hedge with gaps lets in enough light for a wider range of plants.

Soil type matters too. The free-draining chalk downland soils around Sandwich Bay and Worth create a particularly dry root zone under hedges. The heavier clay soils in parts of Ash and Woodnesborough hold moisture longer. If you are on chalk, choose plants tolerant of genuine drought; if on clay, waterlogging in winter is a bigger concern than drought in summer.

Which to choose for different hedge types

Under leylandii and other conifers

This is the toughest combination: deep dry shade with a thick surface root mat and a strongly acidic litter layer. Ivy is the most reliable choice, particularly the native Hedera helix. Once established, it forms a dense, weed-suppressing mat that asks for nothing and looks respectable year-round. Keep it trimmed back from the hedge stems themselves to avoid giving the ivy a climbing route up the hedge. Epimedium is another good option if you want something lower-growing with more seasonal interest.

Under privet and laurel

Slightly less extreme than conifers because privet and laurel let in a little more light through their canopy and are less root-competitive at the surface. Vinca minor, epimedium and dead-nettle all do well here. Vinca minor has the advantage of spreading steadily without becoming unmanageable, and its blue flowers in March and April are a welcome early sight.

Under beech and hornbeam

Deciduous hedges create a different rhythm: full shade in summer, open and light in winter. This suits a wider range of plants. Epimedium is particularly good here as it is semi-evergreen and thrives in exactly these dry-shade conditions once the hedge leafs out. Wild garlic works beautifully under a beech hedge if you have the space and can let it do its thing in April and May. Hart's tongue fern adds structure and is native to Kent's chalk landscape. Ajuga reptans adds colour in May.

Under a native mixed hedge

Native mixed hedges (hawthorn, blackthorn, dog rose, field maple) often have gaps at the base and allow more light through than formal clipped hedges. This gives you the most choice. Any of the plants in the table above will work, and you can also consider cow parsley and red campion as informal meadow-style planting if the hedge is in a rural or semi-rural setting.

How to establish ground cover at a hedge base

Timing matters. Autumn is the best planting season for most ground cover at a hedge base, ideally September to November. The soil is still warm from summer, which means root establishment happens quickly, but the plants are not under moisture stress as they would be in spring or summer.

Preparation makes a difference. Clear weeds thoroughly before planting, as ground elder, bindweed and couch grass will grow back through even vigorous ground cover if the roots are still in the soil. A thick mulch of bark chippings laid over the cleared area and left for a month before planting will suppress annual weed seedlings. Do not dig deeply into the hedge root zone, a light surface cultivation is enough.

Plant at the recommended density for the species. Vinca minor and ajuga establish faster at closer spacing (30 cm apart rather than 45 cm) when you want quick coverage. Epimedium and ferns are slower, so patience is needed in the first season.

Water in well after planting, particularly on the chalk and sandy soils around Sandwich Bay and Eastry where the topsoil can dry very fast in a dry autumn. After the first season, most of these plants are self-sufficient.

What to avoid

A few plants are tempting but cause problems under hedges. Greater periwinkle (Vinca major) is more vigorous than its smaller relative and can be difficult to control once established, smothering the hedge base and pushing out everything else. Spanish bluebells are beautiful in April but the bulbs multiply quickly and become almost impossible to remove if you change your mind. Bamboo and large ornamental grasses compete directly with hedge roots and are not worth the trouble in a narrow base strip.

Avoid planting anything that will require significant digging or disturbance near the hedge, as this damages the hedge root system and can unsettle established plants on the other side of the hedge in a shared boundary situation.
