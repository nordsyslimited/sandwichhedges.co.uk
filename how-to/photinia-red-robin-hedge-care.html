Photinia Red Robin hedge care in Kent

The red leaves are what everyone wants. The trick is knowing that the colour only appears on new growth, so when you trim determines whether you get a dazzling display or a flat green hedge.

Updated August 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Photinia Red Robin: quick checklist

  1. Trim in late April to early May (once the first red flush has hardened) for a second burst of red new growth through summer.
  2. Main cut: late August to September. This is the safe post-nesting window and gives the hedge time to push out one last set of growth before winter.
  3. Check for active nests before cutting at any point between 1 March and 31 August.
  4. Use a hedge trimmer or hand shears on the tips. Photinia's leaves are smaller than laurel, so the shredding problem is less severe, but secateurs still give a cleaner result on thicker stems.
  5. Avoid cutting in wet weather. Wet conditions spread Entomosporium leaf spot between plants.
  6. Renovate hard in late February. Photinia tolerates cutting back to old wood and will reshoot strongly.

Photinia x fraseri 'Red Robin' is one of the most popular garden hedges in East Kent. You see it outside houses in Deal and Ash, lining driveways in Worth and Woodnesborough: a dense, semi-evergreen hedge that starts each growing season with a vivid flush of copper-red new leaves before settling to glossy dark green.

It earns its place. It grows at a reasonable pace, tolerates a range of soils, handles Kent's coastal winds without complaint, and is fairly hard to kill once established. The main question people ask us is why the red fades, and whether there is anything to do about it. There is.

Why the leaves go from red to green

The red colour is only on new growth. As leaves mature and harden off, they turn the familiar glossy green. The hedge is doing exactly what it should. If your photinia hedge is entirely green all summer, it either has not been trimmed recently (so there is no fresh growth to show red) or it was trimmed at the wrong time.

The way to get maximum red is to time your cuts so you are always triggering new growth at a moment when you want the colour on display. The spring flush arrives whether you trim or not, but a second trim in late April or early May, once that first red flush has hardened, will prompt a second set of red shoots. By early July you have fresh red again.

Nesting season: check before you cut

The UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs roughly 1 March to 31 August. Photinia is dense enough to hide nests well: blackbirds in particular favour it. The safe cutting window for most hedges is September to February. The spring trim in late April to early May is only acceptable if you have walked the hedge carefully and are confident it is empty of active nests. If in doubt, wait.

Before you start: safety

Before you start the cut

Wear eye protection against flying fragments, sturdy gloves, and ear defenders if using a petrol trimmer. For hedges taller than chest height, use a long-reach pole trimmer from the ground rather than a ladder wherever the hedge allows. If a ladder is unavoidable, have someone footing it and never overreach sideways. Plug any corded electric trimmer into an RCD adaptor and keep the cable behind you and visible. Check the hedge for wasp nests before starting.

When to trim: the two-cut approach

Most Kent photinia hedges benefit from two cuts a year:

Cut 1: late April to early May. Wait until the first red flush has hardened to at least a pale green. If you cut while the growth is still soft and bright red you will lose most of that flush without triggering much new growth immediately. Cutting the hardened spring growth prompts a second set of red new shoots, which carry the colour through June and into July.

Cut 2: late August to September. This is the main cut. Nesting season is over, the hedge has had its summer growth, and a trim now gives it time to push out one final set of growth before autumn that hardens off before the cold. This is also when the hedge responds best to any shaping work.

If you only have time for one cut a year, make it the August to September one. The hedge will still produce its spring red flush on its own without any prompting, and the single late-summer cut keeps the shape in order.

Tools

A hedge trimmer (cordless battery or petrol) works well on photinia. The leaves are smaller than laurel, so the shredding problem is less of an issue, though a trimmer will still leave some ragged cut edges on larger leaves. For a formal tight finish, follow up with hand shears or secateurs on the outer surface. For a more natural shape, a trimmer alone is fine.

Use secateurs when cutting back individual stems for renovation, or when removing any obviously diseased stems entirely.

Common problem: Entomosporium leaf spot

Brown to purple spots on the leaves, sometimes with a yellow halo, are the signature of Entomosporium leaf spot, a fungal disease that is the most common problem on photinia in UK gardens. It thrives in wet conditions and spreads on wet foliage and wet tools.

Management: trim on dry days, remove and bin (do not compost) affected leaves, and if the problem is severe, thin the hedge slightly to improve air circulation. A copper-based fungicide applied in spring can help on badly affected plants, but improving the growing conditions is more reliable long-term. Clean your tools between plants if you notice infection spreading.

Renovating an overgrown photinia hedge

If a photinia hedge has been left to spread well beyond its allocated space, a hard renovation cut is very achievable. The plant tolerates cutting back to large old stems and will produce vigorous new growth from them. Late February to early March, just before nesting season, is the window for hard renovation. Cut back to the skeleton shape you want, apply a balanced feed in spring, and keep new growth watered during dry spells. By the following spring the hedge will be filling back in well, and the characteristic red display returns with the new growth.

Photinia in East Kent

Photinia Red Robin grows well in Kent's climate. The mild coastal winters mean it rarely suffers cold damage even close to the sea at Deal or Sandwich Bay, and the range of soils in the area (chalk, sandy, and loam) all suit it provided drainage is reasonable. It is not as forgiving as leylandii in waterlogged conditions, but for most garden soils in the Sandwich and Deal area it establishes without fuss and grows steadily once its roots are in.
