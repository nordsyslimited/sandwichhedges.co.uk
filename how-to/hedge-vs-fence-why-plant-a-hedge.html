Hedge vs fence: why a hedge usually wins long-term

A comparison from people who plant and trim hedges for a living in East Kent: cost, privacy, lifespan, wildlife value, and the situations where a fence genuinely is the right call.

Updated August 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

The question comes up constantly when people are having a boundary sorted out. Fence or hedge? The honest answer is: it depends on your timescale. But for most gardens in Sandwich, Deal and the villages of East Kent, a hedge wins over a 20-year horizon almost every time.

The Royal Horticultural Society put the case clearly in their planting guide: hedges capture air pollution, support wildlife and help prevent surface-water flooding in ways a fence simply cannot do. They also tend to be the more cost-effective option once established. The RHS set out the full argument in their YouTube video "How to plant a hedge and why it's better than a fence", produced on the Royal Horticultural Society channel (over 104,000 subscribers), published January 2025.

Why the long view favours a hedge

A close-board timber fence installed today will need replacing at least once in 25 years. That is two installations at current prices, plus staining or treatment every few years in between. A hedge planted this autumn from bare-root stock will, in 20 years, be a permanent, self-maintaining boundary that adds to the property rather than depreciating. It does not blow down in a November storm. It does not rot from the bottom up. And it gets better, not worse, as it matures.

In East Kent, the coastal-wind factor makes this difference particularly clear. Our area sees regular south-westerlies and periodic easterly gales off the Straits of Dover. A solid fence panel creates a turbulence pocket on the sheltered side, which can actually be worse than no windbreak at all. A hedge filters the wind gradually, reducing its force without redirecting it. Anyone with an exposed garden at Sandwich Bay, Worth or out towards the Eastry plateau will know how quickly an unsheltered plot desiccates in spring.

Environmental value: what a hedge gives that a fence never can

A mature hedge in a Kent garden does several things a fence cannot. It provides nesting habitat for birds including blackbirds, dunnocks, wrens and whitethroats. It creates a food corridor linking garden to garden for hedgehogs, voles and invertebrates. And it sequesters carbon and captures fine particulate pollution from road traffic. If your property sits near the A258 between Sandwich and Deal, or on any of the busier rural lanes through Ash or Woodnesborough, a hedge on the roadward boundary will filter a meaningful amount of pollution before it reaches the house.

Native hedging species common in East Kent add a berry crop on top of this. Hawthorn, blackthorn, hazel, field maple and elder are all used in traditional mixed hedges, and all provide food for birds and mammals from late summer through winter. Our guide to planting a native hedge in Kent covers species selection and timing in more detail.

When a fence is genuinely the right answer

None of this means fences are always wrong.

  • Instant privacy is essential. A hedge planted this November will not screen your garden until at least 2028 in most species. If privacy is urgent, plant the hedge now and put a temporary fence or screen on the garden side while it grows in. Remove the fence when the hedge is established.
  • The plot is very narrow. Established hedges take up 30 to 90cm of ground width on each side. In a narrow side passage or a very small courtyard, there may simply not be room.
  • The tenancy does not allow it. Fences are usually within a landlord's domain; hedges are not always permitted under tenancy agreements.
  • Stock need to be contained immediately. On a rural property with livestock, you need a stockproof barrier while the hedge establishes. Fence first, plant the hedge inside it, remove the fence in five years.

Which hedge species suits a Kent boundary?

For year-round privacy, cherry laurel and leylandii are the fastest options, putting on 60 to 90cm of new growth a year. The drawback is that both require careful trimming and neither tolerates being cut into dead brown wood. For a softer, wildlife-rich boundary, a mixed native hedge of hawthorn, blackthorn, field maple and hazel is the traditional choice and is particularly well suited to the chalk and clay soils found around Ash, Eastry and Woodnesborough.

Our species selection guide for Kent gardens covers the main options by soil type, aspect and intended use. If you are buying this autumn, our guide to bare-root vs pot-grown hedging explains why bare-root is usually the better value for a new run of any length.

Neighbour hedge-height law: worth knowing before you plant

If you plant an evergreen hedge that grows above 2m and a neighbour complains it is blocking their light or access, the council can issue a remedial notice under the High Hedges legislation (Anti-social Behaviour Act 2003, Part 8). This is separate from planning law. See our hedge heights and neighbour law guide for the full picture. The simplest avoidance: plan the species and the height before you plant, not once the hedge is already there.
