Photinia leaf spot: how to identify and treat it

Brown spots ringed with yellow appearing on your photinia red robin hedge are almost always entomosporium leaf spot, a fungal disease that spreads in wet weather. Here's how to diagnose it, treat it, and stop it coming back.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Photinia red robin (Photinia fraseri 'Red Robin') is one of the most popular hedging plants in Sandwich, Deal and the villages of East Kent. The brilliant red new growth is the appeal. The downside is that the same lush young foliage is particularly susceptible to entomosporium leaf spot, a fungal disease that causes small brown spots, often with a bright yellow halo, across the leaf surface. Left untreated, it defoliates the hedge and keeps coming back each season.

The good news: it is manageable. This checklist takes you through diagnosis, treatment and prevention in the right order.

Nesting season applies before cutting any photinia

Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs roughly from 1 March to 31 August. Photinia hedges are popular nesting cover, particularly for blackbirds, dunnocks and house sparrows. Any hard cutting to remove diseased growth must wait until the hedge is confirmed empty, or be carried out during the safe window of September to February. A quick visual check takes two minutes and keeps you on the right side of the law.

At a glance: the treatment checklist

Work through these steps in order. If you can only do some of them now, the first three give you the most benefit.

  1. Confirm the diagnosis (spots with yellow halos, on new and old leaves both).
  2. Remove all infected growth with clean, disinfected blades.
  3. Clear fallen leaves from around the hedge base immediately.
  4. Improve air circulation with a light trim to open the canopy slightly.
  5. Apply a fungicide if the infection is active and spreading.
  6. Adjust watering away from overhead spray on the foliage.
  7. Feed the hedge in spring to support recovery.
  8. Monitor and repeat treatment if spots return in the following season.

Step 1: confirm the diagnosis

Entomosporium leaf spot is distinctive once you know what to look for. The spots are:

  • Small and circular, typically 3-8mm across, though they can merge into larger blotches on severely affected leaves.
  • Brown to reddish-brown at the centre, with a distinct yellow or chlorotic halo around the edge.
  • Present on both the upper and lower leaf surfaces, which helps distinguish it from simple scorch or physical damage.
  • Often concentrated on young red leaves first, though established green leaves also become infected.

In wet conditions you may see tiny dark fruiting bodies at the centre of older spots. These are the spore-producing structures that spread the disease to neighbouring leaves.

What it is not: physiological leaf scorch from too much sun or frost shows as browning at the leaf margins without yellow halos or spots on the underside. Aphid damage produces distorted, sticky new growth rather than spots. If the leaves are yellowing uniformly rather than spotting, check the growing conditions and consider a soil test.

Step 2: remove infected growth

No fungicide will cure leaves that are already infected. Physical removal is the most important step. In September and October, after nesting season has ended, cut out stems with obvious heavy infection. Take them back to a healthy leaf junction, not just to the nearest spot.

Before you start: tool hygiene matters here

Keep a small container of diluted garden disinfectant (or a 1:10 bleach solution) near you and wipe the blades between cuts when removing diseased material. Cutting through infected tissue and then cutting healthy growth with the same dirty blade is one of the fastest ways to spread the disease within the hedge. Wear gloves, eye protection and consider an apron if you are spraying fungicide. Bag all infected clippings for household waste: do not compost them.

For a lightly infected hedge, removing the spotted leaves by hand, or going over it with shears and bagging the arisings, may be sufficient. For a hedge where most stems have visible spots, a harder cut in late February (outside nesting season) to remove the worst-affected wood often gives the cleanest restart.

Step 3: clear fallen leaves

Fallen infected leaves on the ground beneath the hedge are a major source of overwintering spores that re-infect the hedge the following spring. Rake them up and bag them promptly, especially in autumn. Do not leave them to break down as mulch.

This step costs ten minutes and makes a genuine difference to whether the disease returns at the same intensity next year. In Deal and Sandwich gardens where photinia is planted close to a path or patio, fallen leaves collect in corners and are easy to miss.

Step 4: improve air circulation

Entomosporium thrives when foliage stays wet for extended periods, as it does in a dense, closely planted hedge where air movement is restricted. A light thinning cut, opening up the centre of the hedge slightly rather than just shearing the outside, allows the hedge to dry more quickly after rain.

This is not a reason to radically reduce the hedge in one go: a stressed hedge with depleted foliage is actually more vulnerable to disease. The aim is a modest improvement in air flow, not a renovation cut.

If the hedge is planted against a wall or fence where one face gets very little air, that side will always be worse. There is little you can do about the aspect, but keeping the hedge slightly looser in those positions helps.

Step 5: apply a fungicide

For active, spreading infections, a fungicide gives useful additional protection alongside the physical measures above. Products containing tebuconazole (sold under various brand names for garden use) or trifloxystrobin have good activity against Entomosporium when applied correctly.

  • Timing: apply at the first sign of new spots in spring, then repeat every 10-14 days through the main growth flush (April to June). A follow-up application in September can help reduce autumn spread.
  • Coverage: spray both sides of the leaves to run-off. Most garden sprayers undercover, especially on a dense hedge. Work from underneath upwards.
  • Precautions: do not spray in wet or windy conditions. Keep pets and children away from the area until the spray has dried. Read the product label carefully regarding proximity to water features, ponds or drains. Many products are restricted near watercourses.

No fungicide will eliminate the disease completely, and resistance can develop if the same chemistry is used repeatedly. Alternate products with different active ingredients if you are treating over multiple seasons.

Step 6: stop overhead watering

If you are watering the hedge, direct the water at the base rather than spraying over the top. Wet foliage for extended periods is exactly what entomosporium needs. A soaker hose laid along the hedge base is more effective than overhead watering anyway, particularly in the dry spells that East Kent regularly sees in late spring and early summer.

Step 7: feed in spring

A hedge depleted by disease benefits from a slow-release balanced fertiliser in early spring (February to March). This supports the flush of new growth and helps the hedge outpace the disease rather than struggling in a weakened state. A general-purpose granular fertiliser applied to the drip line and watered in is sufficient. Do not over-feed with high nitrogen, which promotes the soft, sappy growth that is most susceptible.

Step 8: monitor the following season

Once the hedge has been treated, check it regularly from April onwards each year. A hedge that was severely affected in 2026 may show a recurrence in 2027 even after treatment, especially in wet springs. The aim over two or three seasons is to reduce the disease load progressively, not to eliminate it in a single intervention.

If the disease continues to overwhelm the hedge despite consistent treatment over several years, and especially if the hedge was planted too close (under 50cm spacing) in a low, damp position, it may be worth considering a partial or full replacement with a more disease-resistant species. Portuguese laurel, beech, hornbeam and hawthorn are all reliable alternatives for Kent gardens that offer good screening with lower disease risk.

Will the hedge recover its red colour?

Yes. Photinia's famous red-tipped new growth is the sign of healthy young leaves. Once infected leaves are removed and the hedge is producing vigorous new growth, the red colouring returns with each flush. The intensity of the red depends as much on light levels as on disease: a hedge on a sunny south-facing boundary will colour up more vividly than one in part shade.

The most common mistake we see is treating the spots without tackling the fallen leaves underneath. Remove the diseased growth, rake the base, keep the clippings bagged. That's the difference between managing the disease and just delaying it.
