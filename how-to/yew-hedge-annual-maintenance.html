Annual yew hedge maintenance: the full-year programme for an established hedge

Yew has earned its reputation as the ultimate formal hedge. It is slow, dense, tolerates hard cutting and lasts for generations. Even the best yew hedge needs a consistent annual programme to stay sharp. Here is what a full year of maintenance looks like for an established yew in East Kent.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

The hedges around Sandwich's older walled gardens, and the formal yew at the ends of Worth and Ash village gardens, are looked after on a simple annual cycle. Not complicated. Not expensive. But consistent. This guide is that cycle, written for established yew that is already in decent shape, not for a renovation job. If yours needs renovation, see the separate guide on three-year staged renovation.

The nesting season check

The UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs roughly from 1 March to 31 August. By late August most breeding is complete, but wood pigeons and house sparrows occasionally raise a final brood into September. Walk both sides of the hedge and check the canopy carefully before making the main August or September cut. See our guide on wildlife-friendly hedge trimming for the full legal detail.

Before you start: safety

  • Eye protection: safety glasses or full wrap goggles. Cut tips on yew carry fine woody fragments that travel several metres.
  • Ear defenders: for petrol or electric trimmers.
  • Gloves: cut-resistant gloves (EN 388 Level 3 or above). Yew berries and foliage are toxic: wash hands before eating or touching your face after working around yew.
  • Ladder caution: for yew hedges over 1.5 metres, a pole or long-reach trimmer is safer than a ladder. If a ladder must be used, have someone footing it and never overreach sideways.
  • Blades clean: yew sap stains and hardens quickly on blades. Clean and lightly oil before starting.

What "annual maintenance" means for an established yew

An established yew hedge, one that has been in the ground at least five years and is already close to its intended height and width, needs three things each year: one good trim (usually once a year, occasionally a tidy in early summer for the fastest-growing sections), a seasonal inspection for pest and disease problems, and an occasional mulch at the base if the soil is light. That is it. No feeding programme, no special treatments, no winter wrapping. Yew in Kent looks after itself between cuts if the soil is reasonably well-drained.

The hedges at the Bathurst Estate, where STIHL GB filmed their maintenance sequence, are extreme examples: a historic yew hedge 14 metres tall and 120 metres long, cut annually by a specialist team. The principles are identical for a garden yew hedge at 1.5 metres: cut once a year at the right time, keep the shape consistent, watch for problems.

Season by season

January and February: inspection, not cutting

The hedge is dormant and you can see it properly. This is the time to walk along both sides slowly and look for three things. First, brown or rust-coloured dead patches in the foliage, which can indicate Phytophthora root rot, a soil-borne fungal disease more common in heavy clay soils or areas prone to waterlogging. Any patches with a rust or copper tinge around the crown and discolouration at the stem base need attention. Second, scale insect colonies on older stems, visible as small white or brown disc-like clusters, particularly on the lower half of the hedge. Light populations are unlikely to cause serious harm; heavy infestations are worth treating in early spring before the new season's growth. Third, general die-back from wind or frost. In East Kent, serious frost events are less common than further inland: the coastal influence moderates the worst cold. But an unusually hard winter can scorch exposed yew on north-facing aspects.

No cutting is needed in January or February unless small sections of the hedge have died back and need removing to prevent the dead material spreading.

March and April: watching the new growth

Established yew begins to show new growth from late March in East Kent. The bright lime-green tips appearing against the deep green background are a healthy sign. This is the time to identify which sections have put on strong new growth in previous seasons and which are growing more slowly. Sections that consistently grow less strongly may have soil compaction at the base, root competition from nearby trees, or localised drainage issues. Take a note now for action later in the year. No cutting at this stage: the nesting season starts on 1 March.

May and June: the main growth flush

Yew puts on the bulk of its annual extension growth between April and June. The hedge will look increasingly shaggy through this period: that is normal and expected. Some gardeners worry that an untrimmed yew is suffering; it is simply growing. Resist the temptation to give it a tidy cut before the August window. A cut during active growth in May or June is not fatal for yew, but the exposed tips are vulnerable to sun scorch in a dry summer and the regrowth will mean you need to cut again later in the year.

July: the last pre-cut inspection

By July the main growth flush has slowed. This is the time for a final check for nests (wood pigeons in particular are late nesters), a look at the blade condition, and a decision about whether the hedge needs the full programme this year or whether a light tidy will do.

Late August to September: the main cut

Late August to mid-September is the right window for the main annual cut on established yew in East Kent. The nesting season is winding down, the hedge has completed its main growth flush, and there is usually enough growing weather remaining for the cut tips to settle before winter. The coastal influence around Sandwich, Deal and the Thanet villages means the growing season extends slightly later than further inland, making early September as reliable as late August for a final cut.

For the cut itself: top first, then sides. Keep the batter, that is, the hedge should be very slightly wider at the base than the top. Even a few degrees of inward taper makes a meaningful difference to how the lower hedge stays dense over the years, as the lower foliage receives more light. Work in overlapping passes on the sides, and check from a distance every few metres to catch any variation in the top line before it sets.

Yew can be cut quite firmly without risk. Unlike leylandii, which cannot regenerate from old brown wood, yew will push new growth from apparently bare stems if the main root system is healthy. This means the annual cut can be taken right back to last year's cutting line without worrying about permanent bare patches.

STIHL GB's documentary about maintaining the Bathurst Estate yew hedge includes a detailed look at the annual cutting sequence for a large formal hedge: STIHL's part in maintaining the world's largest yew hedge. The techniques shown, particularly the management of a consistent profile across a long run, translate directly to smaller garden hedges. The fundamentals are the same at any scale.

October and November: post-cut rest

Once the annual cut is done, the hedge needs nothing except observation. A brief flush of new growth within two to three weeks is entirely normal: yew responds to cutting with a small burst of new extension, particularly in a warm autumn. This growth does not need cutting again. It will settle into the winter colour and form part of next year's extension.

October and November are good months for mulching the base of the hedge if the soil tends to dry out in summer. Spread a 5 to 7 cm layer of well-rotted bark or garden compost along the base, keeping it clear of the main stems. Do not pile mulch against the lower trunk. Yew in Kent rarely needs supplementary feeding unless the soil is notably impoverished; a light mulch is usually sufficient.

December: dormancy

By December the hedge is in full dormancy. The cut is done, the season is over. Walk along it on a dry day and look for any areas of browning that appeared after the late August or September cut: a small amount of browning at the very tips is normal and settles out by spring. Widespread browning across a section is worth investigating in January.

Keeping height in check

An established yew hedge that is already at its intended height needs to have that height maintained precisely each year, not allowed to creep upward season by season. Yew grows approximately 15 to 30 centimetres per year once established, with young vigorous hedges at the upper end and mature hedges in place for more than 20 years at the lower end. The annual cut should return the hedge to the same height line each year.

If the hedge has crept above its original height over several years, do not try to restore the original height in one cut. Take 10 to 15 cm off the top each year until the target height is reached. This avoids the visual shock of a dramatic reduction and gives the hedge time to consolidate at each intermediate height.
