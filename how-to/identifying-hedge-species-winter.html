Identifying your hedge in winter: common Kent species

When the leaves are down and the hedge needs cutting, you need to know what you are looking at. Bare twigs, bark texture and bud arrangement tell you more than most people realise. This guide covers the species you are most likely to find in Kent gardens and field boundaries.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

November is when hedges become most revealing. Without leaves to distract you, the architecture of the plant is on full display: the way branches are arranged, the colour and texture of the bark, the size and position of buds, what fruit or seed clusters remain. These are the features botanists have always used for winter identification, and they work just as well for a hedge cutter trying to work out what is on the other side of the wire.

Knowing your species matters for a couple of reasons. It tells you how hard you can cut (yew regenerates from old wood; leylandii does not), when to cut (laurel in spring, hawthorn in autumn), and what not to do (box near box blight). It also tells you whether the hedge has historical or ecological value: a mix of five or six native species in a 30-metre run is likely to be a centuries-old boundary, and there are rules about removing those.

Kent's mild coastal winters mean the identification window runs longer than in the Midlands or the north. Many species here hold fruit and old berries well into January, and the bark stays clean and distinctive even in a wet November.

Winter: the safe cutting window

November through February is the core safe window for hedge cutting under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981. Nesting season runs from 1 March to 31 August: cutting during that period risks disturbing active bird nests, which is an offence. If your hedge holds fruit into December (haws, sloes, rose hips), consider waiting until January before cutting: birds from northern Europe arrive from October onwards specifically for this food.

How to use the table

Start with what you can see from a metre away: colour, overall structure, whether there are thorns or retained fruit. That usually narrows the field to two or three candidates. Then step closer and check the buds: opposite or alternate, their colour and shape. The bud arrangement is one of the most reliable features across all seasons.

Opposite buds (in pairs directly across from each other on the twig) are found on field maple, elder, privet, and spindle. Almost everything else has alternate buds. That single check eliminates half the candidates before you have looked at anything else.

Thorns and spines are obvious but worth interpreting carefully. Hawthorn has stiff, single thorns emerging from the stem. Blackthorn has similar-looking but longer thorns that often carry tiny thorn-lets along their length, and the bark is distinctly darker. Dog rose has hooked prickles (technically different from true thorns, but practically similar). Spindle and field maple have no thorns, which is useful when you are finding thorns in a hedge you thought was entirely one of those species.

Mixed hedges: the norm in East Kent

Most hedges around Sandwich, Deal and the villages to the north and west are mixed. A hedge along a medieval parish boundary at Ash or Woodnesborough will typically contain four to six species in a 30-metre stretch: hawthorn, blackthorn, field maple, hazel, dog rose and possibly spindle or wayfaring tree on chalk. This species diversity is partly what makes old Kent hedgerows so valuable as wildlife corridors.

Hooper's Rule, proposed by the ecologist Max Hooper in the 1970s, suggests that the number of woody species in a 30-metre stretch roughly corresponds to the age of the hedge in centuries: a five-species hedge is likely about 500 years old. It is a guideline rather than a law, but it is a useful frame when you are looking at a hedge that runs along what your OS map shows as a parish boundary. Those hedges often appear in the Hedgerow Regulations 1997 as "important hedgerows," which restricts what can be done to them without notifying the local planning authority.

Garden hedge species in winter

Garden hedges around Sandwich Bay and the coastal properties near Deal are often quite different from field-boundary hedges: they are more likely to be single-species and include garden cultivars rather than wild natives. Privet (the semi-evergreen oval-leafed Ligustrum ovalifolium), cherry laurel and leylandii are very common, and those are identifiable at a glance in winter from their evergreen foliage. Beech hedges in the garden hold their dead leaves, which turns them a warm honey-brown through winter. Hornbeam is similar but paler. Yew is dark, dense and always evergreen.
