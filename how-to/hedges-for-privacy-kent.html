Hedges for privacy and screening in East Kent gardens

Which species give you the fastest cover, which last the longest, and what to choose if you are near the coast. Written by people who plant and maintain these hedges in real Kent gardens.

Updated August 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Most people planting a hedge for privacy want two things: cover quickly, and no drama once it is in. The second requirement is often the one that catches people out. Leylandii gives you cover in three years, but if you lose track of it for a couple of seasons, you have a serious problem. Yew gives you one of the best hedges in the world, but you are waiting five or six years before it really screens anything.

We plant and maintain hedges across Sandwich, Deal, Worth, Ash, Woodnesborough, Eastry and Sandwich Bay. Here is an honest account of which species work best for privacy in East Kent conditions, and what to think about before you start digging.

What to consider before choosing a species

How tall do you need it?

Most residential gardens in East Kent need a hedge between 1.5 m and 2.5 m for useful screening. Below 1.5 m and you are not blocking much. Above 2.5 m on an evergreen species, you start running into potential neighbour-dispute territory under the High Hedges legislation. See our guide to hedge heights and neighbour law for the detail on that.

If you need a hedge of 2 m or more and the main species options here all fit within that range, check what height you actually need and plant accordingly. Privet left to its own devices will reach 4 m or more; laurel can exceed 6 m. Both need management to stay at a useful garden height.

How quickly do you need cover?

The fastest legitimate screening option is leylandii. In good conditions it puts on 60-90 cm a year and can give a useful screen in two to three years from small starter plants. The trade-off is management: it needs at least one annual cut, and it cannot be reduced hard once it has exceeded the green growth zone. If you stop cutting for two years, you have a problem. If that trade-off suits you, leylandii is fine. If it does not, look at laurel or privet instead.

For a more manageable medium-speed option, privet (Ligustrum ovalifolium) grows 30-60 cm a year, takes well to any height you want, and is very forgiving of hard cuts if you need to reduce it. It is semi-evergreen, which means it may drop some leaves in a hard winter, but in the mild coastal winters around Sandwich and Deal it rarely loses its cover entirely.

Coastal or inland?

Gardens along the coast at Sandwich Bay, the Deal seafront or anywhere exposed to the prevailing south-westerly or north-easterly winds face a different challenge from gardens inland at Worth, Ash and Eastry. Salt-laden wind scorches foliage and can kill plants that grow perfectly well three miles inland. For exposed coastal positions, the reliable options for screening are:

  • Griselinia littoralis: New Zealand broadleaf, with apple-green rounded leaves. Excellent salt tolerance, grows at around 30-50 cm a year, takes clipping well. Not fully hardy in severe winters, but East Kent's coastal climate is mild enough that it rarely suffers.
  • Escallonia: dense, small-leaved and tough in salt wind. Flowers in summer, which is a bonus. Reaches 2-3 m without much effort.
  • Privet: more salt-tolerant than it looks, particularly Ligustrum ovalifolium. Useful in sheltered coastal positions.
  • Pittosporum tenuifolium: excellent for a slightly more ornamental coastal screen, though less hardy than griselinia in hard winters. For full detail, see our pittosporum care guide.

For an in-depth look at coastal planting, see our guide to salt-tolerant hedges for Kent.

Species by species

Privet (Ligustrum ovalifolium)

The most versatile privacy hedge for an average Kent garden. Fast enough to give cover in three to four years from bare-root plants, takes any height from 1 m to 4 m, and responds well to hard renovation cuts if it has been neglected. The main maintenance commitment is two cuts a year (June and September). Semi-evergreen: may lose some leaves in a severe winter, but recovers fast in spring. Not ideal for exposed coastal positions. For full care detail, see our privet hedge care guide.

Cherry laurel (Prunus laurocerasus)

Fast, dense, evergreen and easy to establish. Large, glossy, deep-green leaves make a visually solid screen. Grows 30-60 cm a year and tolerates shade reasonably well, which makes it useful along north-facing fences and walls where other species struggle. Needs secateurs rather than a trimmer for the neatest finish because the large leaves shred badly at the cut edge. Can get large: allow 1.5-2 m depth at maturity for a tall screen. See our laurel hedge care guide for trimming timing and renovation advice.

Yew (Taxus baccata)

The best-looking formal screen of any hedge species in the UK, and one that keeps improving with every passing decade. Slow (20-30 cm a year) but very tolerant of shade and chalk soils. The red berries are toxic and this matters if children or pets have access to the hedge base. Takes hard renovation cuts surprisingly well and is very long-lived. Worth the wait if you are planting for the long term. See our yew hedge care guide.

Leylandii (x Cuprocyparis leylandii)

Fast and effective for a tall screen but requires a commitment to annual cutting and cannot be reduced back into dead brown wood. Read our leylandii rules guide before planting, particularly the section on the limits of renovation. Best in inland positions away from salt wind. Works well in the gardens around Worth, Ash, Woodnesborough and Eastry where exposed coastal winds are less of a factor.

Beech (Fagus sylvatica)

Technically deciduous but keeps its bronze-copper leaves through winter, which gives useful partial screening year-round. Dense in summer, lighter but still present in winter. Grows at 40-60 cm a year once established. Particularly good for tall hedges on chalk soils, which are common across East Kent. One cut a year in late August is all it needs. See our beech hedge care guide.

Portuguese laurel (Prunus lusitanica)

Smaller-leaved than cherry laurel, denser in growth, and arguably better-looking when clipped. More drought-tolerant too. Grows slightly more slowly than cherry laurel (20-40 cm a year), but the wait is worth it for a formal hedge. See our Portuguese laurel guide.

Before you start planting

Nesting season

The UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs roughly from 1 March to 31 August. If there is an existing hedge or vegetation in the spot where you want to plant, clear it outside the nesting season or check it carefully before removing it. The safe window for clearance work is September to February. See our guide to when to trim hedges for the full calendar.

Before you start: safety

  • Thorny species: hawthorn, blackthorn and pyracantha have serious thorns. Wear sturdy gloves and long sleeves when handling them
  • Powered tools for ground clearance: wear eye protection, gloves and ear defenders
  • Check for underground services before digging along any boundary. Dial Before You Dig or check your property survey
  • Yew berries and foliage are toxic to humans, horses, dogs and cats. This matters if children or animals access the hedge base

Planting: the basics

For most species, bare-root plants (available November to March) are cheaper and establish just as well as pot-grown if planted during their dormant period. Plant into well-prepared ground: fork out compacted soil to at least a spade's depth, mix in organic compost, and water thoroughly after planting.

Space plants at 3-5 per metre for most species. Dense planting speeds up cover but can lead to bare stems at the base later as light is shut out. Leylandii is better at 2-3 per metre; laurel at 2-3 per metre for a tall screen. For a thorough guide to establishment routine, see our newly planted hedge care guide.

Leave at least 45-60 cm between the hedge planting line and any solid wall or fence. This allows air circulation (reducing disease risk), room for the hedge to thicken at the base, and access for trimming from both sides.

A note on legal height

There is no automatic legal height limit for garden hedges in England and Wales. However, if an evergreen or semi-evergreen hedge over 2 m tall is blocking your neighbour's light or access, they can formally complain to the local council under the High Hedges legislation (Part 8 of the Anti-social Behaviour Act 2003). If the council upholds the complaint, they can require a reduction. Planting a hedge that you intend to keep at a reasonable height, and doing so, avoids most disputes. For the full legal detail, see our guide to hedge heights and neighbour law.
