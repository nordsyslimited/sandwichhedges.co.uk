Planning your autumn hedge planting: a September–November guide for Kent gardens

Autumn is the best time to plant a new hedge, but the preparation starts weeks before the plants arrive. This step-by-step guide covers everything from choosing species for your Kent soil type through to ordering, site preparation and what to expect in year one.

Updated September 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

The Royal Horticultural Society's guide "How to plant a bare-root hedge" (youtube.com/watch?v=qDJc9rqCAY4) covers the planting technique in detail, but what it cannot tell you is the preparation that needs to happen in September and October before bare-root stock is even available. In Kent — with its range of soils from chalk downland to heavy coastal clay — the planning stage matters as much as the planting technique. Get the species and site preparation right in autumn and bare-root hedges establish well. Skip the preparation and you lose a season at minimum, sometimes plants entirely.

Step 1: Decide exactly what you need before you order

Start with a measuring tape and a clear idea of the hedge's purpose. Measure the length of the boundary you want to plant and decide on a single row or a double staggered row. A double row produces a denser hedge more quickly and is the right choice for privacy or a wildlife hedge. A single row is sufficient for a formal low hedge or where space is limited.

Also decide at this stage whether you want a single-species hedge or a native mix. A single species (beech, hornbeam, hawthorn) is predictable in growth rate, appearance and maintenance requirements. A native mix (typically three to five species) produces a more varied, wildlife-friendly hedge but can look uneven in the first few years as different species grow at different rates. For most East Kent gardens, a beech-dominant or hawthorn-dominant mix in a double row is a practical, attractive starting point. See our full guide to choosing hedge species for Kent for more detail.

Step 2: Choose species suited to your Kent soil and position

East Kent has a wider range of soil types than many people realise. Getting the species right for your specific site makes the difference between a hedge that disappears in year two and one that doubles in size every year.

Chalk and free-draining soils (Deal, Barham, Barfrestone, Guston)

Beech (Fagus sylvatica), field maple (Acer campestre), hawthorn (Crataegus monogyna), blackthorn (Prunus spinosa) and yew (Taxus baccata) all do well. Beech is particularly good on chalk: it produces dense, wind-filtering growth and retains its dead leaves through winter for year-round screening. Avoid hornbeam on pure chalk in very dry positions, as it prefers slightly heavier soils.

Heavy clay (Worth, Woodnesborough, parts of Ash)

Hawthorn, blackthorn, field maple and dogwood (Cornus sanguinea) tolerate wet clay well and form dense hedges in relatively poor conditions. Hornbeam also does well on heavier soils. Avoid box and yew in poorly drained heavy clay: they will sit dormant for two years and then decline.

Coastal and exposed positions (Sandwich Bay, Pegwell Bay, Ramsgate margins)

Exposed coastal gardens in East Kent need species that tolerate wind, salt spray and sometimes very sandy soils. Sea buckthorn (Hippophae rhamnoides) is excellent for coastal shelter: it grows quickly, produces berries for wildlife and is very tolerant of salt. Hawthorn is equally tough in coastal positions. Escallonia and tamarisk are also options for coastal gardens where a more ornamental appearance is wanted, though they require more maintenance than a native mix.

Formal garden hedges (any soil)

For a formal clipped hedge rather than a wildlife boundary, yew is the best long-term choice for most Kent garden conditions. Hornbeam (Carpinus betulus) is an excellent alternative where yew would be too slow, and produces a similar formal appearance with faster establishment. Beech works well in formal settings on chalk. See our guides to yew hedge care and hornbeam hedge care for maintenance detail.

Site clearance and the nesting season

If your new hedge line runs through or adjacent to existing scrub, bramble or rough vegetation, clear it before planting. However, be aware of the UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981: vegetation clearance from 1 March to 31 August risks disturbing active bird nests, which is a criminal offence. September onwards is the safe window. Clear the site in September or early October before planting in November. If you are only marking a clear boundary on existing lawn or bare ground, this restriction does not apply.

Step 3: Calculate your quantities accurately

Underordering is the most common mistake when planning a bare-root hedge. Work through this calculation before ordering:

  1. Measure the run in metres. Measure along the ground, not on a plan, and allow for any corners or curves.
  2. Choose single or double row. Single row: 3–5 plants per metre (species-dependent). Double staggered row: 5–6 plants per metre across both rows combined.
  3. Multiply and add 10 per cent. Add 10 per cent to your total to allow for transplant losses and any plants that arrive in poor condition.
  4. Check your species for availability. Some species — particularly yew, hornbeam and native mixes — sell out early. Order by the end of September to guarantee your first choice of species and sizes.

Example: a 15-metre garden boundary, double row, hawthorn-dominant mix at 5 plants per metre = 75 plants. Add 10 per cent = 83 plants. Order 85–90.

Step 4: Order in September for November delivery

Bare-root season runs from November to late February. Most nurseries start lifting and dispatching bare-root stock in the first week of November, and popular species sell out before Christmas. Order in September to guarantee supply. When ordering:

  • Specify 40–60 cm transplants for most situations. Larger grades (60–90 cm) establish slightly more slowly because there is more top growth relative to the root system. Smaller grades (20–40 cm) are cheaper and establish quickly but look sparse for longer.
  • For yew, specify 30–50 cm transplants. Yew is slow, and you want established root systems rather than maximum height at planting.
  • Ask the nursery to hold your order for delivery in early to mid November, or specify a delivery week that suits your schedule. Do not accept delivery if you are not ready to plant within a few days: bare-root plants stored for more than a week are at risk of drying out or overheating.

Step 5: Prepare the site in October

Site preparation is best done four to six weeks before planting: dig, improve, mulch, and let the soil settle. Good preparation in October makes November planting quicker and the results significantly better.

Mark the planting line

Run a taut garden line or hosepipe along the proposed hedge line. Check it from both ends and from the house. Adjust if necessary before you start digging: it is much easier to move a line than it is to correct a planted hedge.

Clear and dig the planting trench

For a bare-root hedge, dig a trench approximately 45–60 cm wide and 30–40 cm deep. Remove all perennial weed roots, particularly couch grass, dock and bindweed, which will compete aggressively with young hedgeplants. For heavy clay, fork the base of the trench to break up the subsoil and improve drainage.

Improve the soil

Mix the excavated soil with one to two buckets of well-rotted compost or manure per linear metre. On chalk or very sandy soils, the organic matter is critical for water retention. On clay, add grit (not fine sharp sand) to improve drainage. Refill the trench with the improved mix and let it settle before planting.

Apply a weed-suppressing mulch

After planting, lay a 75–100 mm layer of woodchip or bark mulch along the planting line, kept clear of the plant stems. This is one of the most effective things you can do for bare-root hedge establishment: it suppresses weed competition, retains moisture and insulates roots from late frosts. A mulch mat or weed-suppressing membrane beneath the woodchip is useful in gardens where grass and weed pressure is high. Our guide to caring for a newly planted hedge covers maintenance through year one.

Step 6: Plant on a dry, frost-free day

When your bare-root plants arrive, unpack them immediately and check the roots are moist. If you cannot plant within 24 hours, heel them in temporarily: lay the bundles in a shallow trench, cover the roots with soil and firm down. Never leave bare roots exposed to wind or sun for more than a few minutes.

Plant at the same depth as the nursery mark on the stem (the soil tide-mark from the nursery bed). The root collar — where the stem meets the roots — should be at or just below soil level. Firm each plant with your boot after planting to remove air pockets. For double-row planting, offset the two rows by half the plant spacing: if plants in row one are 30 cm apart, plants in row two are planted at 15 cm from the first row and centred between the row-one plants.

For a full technical walkthrough of planting technique, see our guide to planting bare-root hedges.

Step 7: Formative pruning at planting time

This surprises most people: newly planted bare-root hedges should be cut back by one third to one half at planting time, not left at full height. This applies particularly to hawthorn and blackthorn. The reason is that bare-root transplants have reduced root systems compared to the top growth, and cutting back reduces moisture demand while the roots establish. A hawthorn planted at 60 cm and cut back to 35–40 cm will produce dense basal growth from the first spring onwards. Left at full height, it puts all its energy into the existing stems and produces a gap-prone, thin hedge base.

Yew, hornbeam and beech do not require as drastic a cut-back and can be left at their planted height, though trimming the leading shoot by a third is still beneficial. Our guide to formative hedge pruning explains the two-year training programme in full.

What to expect in year one

The first spring after planting is anxious for most gardeners: bare-root hedges often look dormant well into April, particularly yew, which can take until May before new growth appears. This is normal. The plant is establishing its root system first. Do not pull it out or assume it has failed until June.

Watering in the first summer is the highest-priority maintenance task. Even well-established species will struggle in a dry June without supplementary water. Water deeply and infrequently rather than lightly every day: aim for 10–15 litres per plant per week in dry weather, applied slowly to penetrate to root level. A soaker hose along the planting trench is the most efficient approach for a long hedge run.

By the end of year one, a well-planted and correctly maintained hedge will typically be 15–30 per cent taller than it was at planting, with noticeably denser branching at the base. By year three, the hedge will look established. The timeline varies by species: hawthorn and field maple develop quickly, while yew and hornbeam are slower but ultimately more permanent.

A bare-root hedge planted carefully in November in Sandwich will look like it has always been there by the time the garden is seven years old. The difficult part is the first summer.
