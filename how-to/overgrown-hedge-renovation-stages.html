Overgrown hedge renovation: the staged approach

Most neglected hedges can be rescued. The key is spreading the cuts over two or three years so the plant always has enough foliage left to survive.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Many hedges across Sandwich, Deal, Ash and the surrounding East Kent villages have been left untouched for ten or twenty years. The hedge that was planted as a neat privet screen in the 1990s has spread to two metres wide; the beech hedge at the back of a Woodnesborough garden now blocks the light to the vegetable patch. It looks daunting, but most common hedge species respond well to renovation if you go about it methodically.

The golden rule is simple: never cut everything at once. A hedge needs foliage to survive, and removing all of it in a single season can kill the plant outright. By staging the work over two or three years, you give the hedge enough photosynthesising growth to keep the root system alive while the cut sections regenerate. It takes longer, but the success rate is far higher than the drastic single-season approach.

Nesting season: the one rule everything else works around

Under the UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs roughly from 1 March to 31 August. The safe renovation window for most species is September to February. If work is unavoidable between March and August, check the hedge thoroughly for occupied nests first and do not cut if any are in use. See the full seasonal calendar for month-by-month guidance.

Before you start: kit and safety

  • Eye protection (mandatory): flying clippings from a hedge trimmer carry further than you expect
  • Ear defenders for petrol or electric trimmers
  • Heavy gloves: renovation cuts mean handling thick stems and old woody growth
  • Ladder safety: use a pole or long-reach trimmer wherever the hedge height allows; if a ladder is needed, always have someone footing it and never overreach sideways
  • RCD adaptor for corded electric tools: plug into the RCD before the trimmer, not the other way around
  • Wasp nests: check before starting, particularly in late summer and early autumn when nests are at their largest

Step 1: Assess the hedge before anything else

Before reaching for a saw or trimmer, walk the inside face of the hedge and look carefully at the interior stems. You are looking for green growth: even small shoots or leaves emerging from old, brown wood tell you that the plant is alive and capable of regenerating from that point. This matters because a renovation cut only works if there is live growth below the cut you are about to make.

If you can see green growth distributed across the interior, a two-year staged renovation is usually sufficient. If the interior is entirely bare and woody with no green tissue at all, treat it as a three-year job and proceed more cautiously, one face at a time. Also take note of the hedge's current width and height: a hedge that has spread from 60 cm to 150 cm is a job you can tackle yourself, but anything wider may justify calling in a professional to handle the volume of material.

Step 2: Year one cut (autumn or winter)

In the first autumn or winter, cut one face of the hedge hard. As a general rule, start with the face that is most overgrown, or the side facing away from the garden if the hedge borders a road or neighbour's land. Reduce that face by 30 to 50 per cent of its current width. Leave the opposite face and the top completely untouched. This is the most important point in the whole process: the uncut side keeps the hedge photosynthesising and the root system alive while the cut face begins to regenerate.

Species notes for year one timing:

  • Beech and hornbeam: cut hard on one side in January or February while dormant; will regenerate reliably
  • Privet: very forgiving; cut from September through to February
  • Hawthorn: November through February is ideal
  • Holly: October to January, after the berries have been stripped by fieldfare and redwing (the berry crop is an important winter food source for birds arriving in East Kent from Scandinavia)
  • Yew: September to October is the preferred window
  • Laurel: late August or September; use secateurs or loppers on thick stems rather than a trimmer, which shreds the large leaves

Step 3: Year two cut (autumn or winter)

By the second autumn, the face you cut in year one should be showing visible new growth. If more reduction is still needed on that side, return to it again. If it has responded well, switch to the opposite face and cut it equally hard, using the same 30 to 50 per cent reduction. Leave the top untouched again, unless the hedge is very tall, in which case you can begin reducing the top slightly, by no more than 20 to 25 per cent in one go.

At this point the hedge will look somewhat uneven, with one face freshly cut and the other having a season's regrowth. This is normal and temporary. The important thing is that the hedge is alive and responding.

Step 4: Year three and ongoing

By the third autumn, both faces should have regrown to a reasonable density. This is the year for any final top reduction and for bringing the overall profile back to the shape you want. After year three, resume a normal annual trimming schedule at the reduced size. The hedge will continue to thicken over the following two or three growing seasons.

Kent note: East Kent's mild coastal winters extend the renovation window compared with colder inland counties. Hedges around Woodnesborough, Eastry and Worth often survive cuts that might cause dieback in a colder January further north. If in doubt, err toward the later end of the window, February rather than December, particularly for yew and laurel.

Species that cannot be renovated this way

Leylandii and most other conifers (Lawson cypress, western red cedar, Leyland cypress) do not regenerate from old brown wood. If you cut them back to the bare interior stems they will not recover: the brown sections stay brown. The only safe approach with conifers is to trim within the green growth, never going deeper than the last year or two of new growth. Yew is the significant exception: it regenerates from old wood and can be cut hard, which is why it is widely used for formal topiary and clipped hedges. For more on conifers, see leylandii rules and renovation limits.
