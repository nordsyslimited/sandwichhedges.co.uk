How to remove a hedge safely

A step-by-step guide covering the legal checks, nesting bird rules and stump work that most people miss the first time round.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Martin Fish, RHS judge and broadcaster, covers the practical cutting-back process in his video "Hedge Cutting Explained and Demonstrated" (youtube.com/watch?v=pbLr1l7uNrs) via the Pots & Trowels channel. The cutting technique he demonstrates is exactly the same whether you are trimming or removing: get the material down safely before tackling the stumps.

Removing a hedge is more work than most people budget for, mainly because of the roots. But the job is very doable if you go through it in the right order. The steps below apply whether you have a 10-metre privet hedge in Sandwich, a leylandii run in Deal, or a laurel screen along a back garden boundary in Ash.

Check for nesting birds before you touch anything

The Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs roughly from 1 March to 31 August. If you find an active nest, work stops until the birds have fledged and left. The safe window for hedge removal is September to February. Even outside nesting season, always check the hedge from the base upwards before any cutting begins.

Before you start: the legal checks

Most garden hedges can be removed without any permission. But two situations require a check first.

Hedgerow regulations. If the hedge is in a rural or semi-rural setting (adjacent to a field, lane, footpath or road), is at least 30 metres long and pre-dates 1850, it may qualify as a protected hedgerow under the Hedgerows Regulations 1997. You must notify your local council at least 42 days before removal. If they object, you cannot proceed. See our full guide on hedgerow regulations and removal for the assessment criteria and notification process.

Planning conditions. If your property was developed within the last 30 years, or is a listed building, there may be a planning condition that protects specific hedges. Check your planning portal or contact your local planning authority if you are unsure.

Safety before you cut

Basic PPE for hedge removal

  • Eye protection (safety glasses or a visor): cuttings fly at speed, especially from power tools
  • Ear defenders when using a petrol or mains-electric trimmer for any extended period
  • Gloves: hawthorn, blackthorn, holly and pyracantha all draw blood
  • Steel-toed boots: stumps and roots are trip hazards at every stage
  • RCD adapter if using any mains-electric tools outdoors
  • Check for wasp nests before power-cutting into any dense section of hedge, particularly in summer or early autumn

If the hedge is over 2 metres tall and you need a ladder or steps to reach the top, make sure someone else is present. A ladder on uneven ground next to a hedge is surprisingly unstable.

Step 1: cut the top right down

Start by cutting the hedge down to as low a height as your equipment allows, working from one end to the other. A petrol hedge trimmer or a reciprocating saw will handle most stems up to about 5 cm diameter. For older, thicker stems, a chainsaw or a bowsaw gives more control.

The goal at this stage is simply to reduce the material to a manageable height (roughly knee height) before you dig. Dragging full-height stems around while you are digging stumps makes the job much harder than it needs to be.

Clear the cut material as you go and stack it away from your working area. Hedge clippings are slippery underfoot, especially on wet ground.

Step 2: cut the sides flush to the ground

Once the height is down, work along each side to cut lateral branches flush or close to the main stems. This exposes the base clearly and makes the next stage, digging, much more accurate.

On a privet hedge in a Kent coastal garden, this stage often reveals just how far the roots have spread: privet planted in a single row frequently sends lateral roots 1 to 1.5 metres out from the main stems on the sandy soils around Sandwich Bay and Deal.

Step 3: dig out the root stumps

This is the hardest part, and the right tool depends on the species.

Privet and box: a spade and a mattock are usually enough. Privet roots are relatively shallow (30 to 50 cm on typical Kent soils) and the root mass is manageable if you cut the main laterals with loppers or a pruning saw as you expose them. Work around each stump in a circle, severing the lateral roots as you find them, before levering the main stump out.

Laurel: the root ball on a mature laurel can be substantial. A spade and a crow bar, or a mattock, are the minimum. On chalk soil (common around Eastry, Ash and Woodnesborough), the roots often follow the chalk fissures and can reach well beyond what is visible at the surface.

Leylandii: leylandii stumps are usually straightforward once the tree is down. The root plate is often quite shallow on the East Kent clay and chalk soils. A mattock and spade work well. The stump itself can often be rocked free once the lateral roots are cut.

Beech and hawthorn: older established hedges with beech or hawthorn can have roots that are 20 or 30 years old. A mini-digger is worth hiring for a run over about 15 metres. Most tool-hire companies in the Sandwich and Deal area can deliver one the same week.

Step 4: treat stumps that regrow

Some species will resprout from cut stumps with considerable vigour. Privet, laurel and elder are the main ones to treat.

Apply a proprietary stump killer (containing triclopyr or glyphosate) to the cut face of the stump immediately after cutting, whilst the wood is still moist. For privet and laurel, this reduces regrowth significantly. Elder often regrows from root fragments as well as stumps, so you may need to monitor the area and re-treat emerging shoots over the following season.

Leylandii stumps very rarely regrow and generally need no treatment at all.

Step 5: dispose of the material

Hedge removal generates a large volume of material and planning disposal in advance saves significant effort.

  • Green bin: suitable for lighter cuttings and small volumes. Most Kent district councils (Canterbury, Dover, Thanet) accept garden waste with a size limit, usually branches no thicker than 10 cm.
  • Household waste recycling centre: the quickest option for most people, and free for Kent residents with a KentChoices account. Deal, Sandwich and Ash all have convenient sites nearby.
  • Skip hire: for larger jobs. A 6-yard skip handles a typical 15 to 20 metre run of privet or leylandii.
  • Chip on site: renting a garden chipper turns a large pile into a compact heap of woodchip that can be used as mulch. This is the most efficient option if you have beds or borders that need mulching anyway.

Note: hedge material from species on the Schedule 9 list of the Wildlife and Countryside Act (including Japanese knotweed, though it rarely grows in hedges) must be disposed of as controlled waste. In practice, this is not relevant to most garden hedges in East Kent.

Step 6: prepare the ground

Once the stumps and major roots are out, the ground will be uneven and compacted where you have been working. Fork it over to break it up, remove any remaining root fragments (especially from privet and laurel, which can regrow from root pieces), and level it.

If you are replacing the hedge, autumn is the best time to plant bare-root whips. Native species like hawthorn, hornbeam and field maple establish quickly on Kent soils and are significantly cheaper per metre than pot-grown or root-balled stock. See our guide to planting bare-root hedges for spacing and species choices.

If you are laying a patio, path or turf, allow the ground to settle for at least a few weeks before any hard landscaping, particularly if there were large stumps that leave voids as they decompose.

The two things that catch people out: forgetting the legal check on longer rural hedges, and not budgeting enough time for the stump work. Everything else is just hard graft.
