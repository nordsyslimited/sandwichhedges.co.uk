Common Hedge Cutting Mistakes And How To Avoid Them

Seven errors we see again and again on East Kent gardens, and the simple fixes that make all the difference.

Published 10 September 2026 Category: DIY
Seven mistakes at a glance
  1. Cutting during active nesting season without checking first
  2. Using blunt blades that crush stems rather than cut them
  3. Trimming the top before the sides (slip hazard and lost guide line)
  4. Cutting at a vertical face instead of a slight inward taper
  5. Taking too much off at once on an overgrown hedge
  6. Cutting into old bare wood on species that cannot regenerate from it
  7. Letting the hedge grow wider each year by always cutting beyond last season's line

Each mistake is expanded below with the fix and the reason it matters.

Most hedge trimming problems are not tool problems or plant problems. They are technique problems that compound quietly over several seasons until the hedge looks permanently ragged or patchy. The good news is that most can be corrected by changing the approach rather than replacing the hedge.

This checklist is drawn from the hedges we maintain across Sandwich, Deal, Worth, Ash and the surrounding East Kent villages. The species and conditions here, chalk soil, coastal exposure, salt-laden air in Sandwich Bay, tend to exaggerate small technique errors into visible problems faster than sheltered inland gardens.

Before you start: safety check
  • PPE: goggles or safety glasses, ear defenders with powered equipment, heavy gloves and long sleeves.
  • Ladders: never lean a ladder directly against a soft hedge. Use a free-standing platform ladder or a step ladder positioned on firm ground. Keep both feet on a rung; never stand on the top cap.
  • RCD: plug all electric hedge cutters into a residual current device. Work the cable over your shoulder, not along the ground where blades can reach it.
  • Wasp nests: tap the hedge gently at several points before starting and listen for buzzing. A disturbed wasp nest mid-cut is a genuine danger.
  • Nesting birds: see the legal callout further down this page.

Mistake 1: Cutting during nesting season without checking

This is the most serious mistake on the list because it carries a legal penalty, not just a horticultural one. Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it is an offence to intentionally damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Hedges are prime nesting habitat: blackbirds, robins, sparrows and wrens all favour dense boundary shrubs in East Kent gardens.

The fix is not to avoid cutting from March to August entirely, but to check carefully before each cut. Part the foliage with a stick and look before bringing the blades near. If you find a nest with eggs or live chicks, stop, mark the area, and return once the nest is confirmed empty. See mistake 2 in our dedicated hedge trimming safety checklist for a step-by-step approach to nest checking.

Mistake 2: Using blunt blades

Blunt blades do not cut stems cleanly: they crush and tear, leaving a frayed wound that browns at the tip and becomes an entry point for disease. On box hedges in particular, blunt cuts encourage the conditions that allow box blight to take hold. On leylandii, crushed stems turn brown and stay brown.

Sharpen reciprocating blades with a flat mill file at the start of each season and again mid-season if you are cutting a lot of material. For handheld shears, a sharpening stone on the flat faces of the blades makes a noticeable difference. The RHS demonstrates good hedge cutting technique, including the effect of blade condition, in their video guide (linked at the foot of this page).

Mistake 3: Cutting the top before the sides

Always cut the sides first, then finish with the top. The reason is straightforward: clippings from the top fall onto the uncut sides below. If you are on a ladder or platform, those loose clippings become a slip hazard underfoot. If you have cut a precise line along the top first, clippings landing on the side obscure the guide you have just created.

Work each side from the bottom of the section upward, then clear clippings before moving to the top. It is also easier to see whether the side profile is consistently tapered when you step back to assess before committing to the top cut.

Mistake 4: A vertical face instead of a slight inward taper

A hedge cut to a perfectly vertical face looks neat in the short term but causes problems at the base over several seasons. The upper canopy shades the lower foliage, which gradually thins and dies back, leaving bare stems at the bottom. This is one of the most common complaints on leylandii, privet and hornbeam hedges in Kent.

The correct profile, sometimes called a battered face, tapers slightly inward so the base is wider than the top. The angle is subtle: around five degrees is enough. It ensures light reaches the lower shoots and the whole face remains dense. Our guide to hedge cutting profiles covers this in more detail for different species.

Mistake 5: Taking too much off an overgrown hedge in one cut

The one-third rule applies to volume reduction: never remove more than a third of the total growth in a single season. Taking more shocks the plant, reduces the photosynthetic area it needs to recover, and can trigger stress dieback. On very overgrown hedges, particularly beech, hornbeam and privet, stagger the reduction over two or three seasons.

Hard renovation cuts on species that genuinely tolerate it, yew, hawthorn and blackthorn, can go further, but even these respond better to a phased approach. Reduce one face one year, the other face the next, before tackling the top.

Mistake 6: Cutting into old bare wood on the wrong species

Not all hedging plants can break new growth from old bare wood. Leylandii, ceanothus and most conifers will not regenerate from stems that have lost all foliage. Cut into them and the brown patch stays brown permanently. The safe approach is to cut only into green growth with visible buds or foliage.

Species that do regenerate from old wood, given time, include yew, box, hawthorn, beech, hornbeam and privet. Even these respond better when cuts stay within current or recent season's growth rather than going back to very old material.

Mistake 7: Letting the hedge grow wider each year

This is a slow creep that many gardeners do not notice until the hedge has doubled in width over a decade. It happens when each trim is made just beyond the previous cut line rather than back to it. A millimetre or two per cut adds up quickly.

The fix is to use a string line or a long straight edge as a guide and consciously cut inside the previous season's growth, not beyond it. On formal hedges, this discipline is what keeps the footprint stable year after year. If the hedge has already crept significantly, plan a measured reduction over two or three seasons rather than trying to claw back all the width at once.

Nesting season: know your legal window

The Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to intentionally damage or destroy an active bird's nest. In practice, nesting activity in East Kent runs from 1 March through to 31 August. Cutting your hedge during this period is not illegal, but doing so without checking for active nests first is an offence if a nest is disturbed.

The safest window for significant hedge cutting, reductions, renovation cuts, and formal reshaping, is September through to February. This aligns with the period when most hedging species are dormant or approaching dormancy, so it is also the best time horticulturally.

Light tidying cuts outside this window are generally fine as long as you check first. When in doubt, leave it until autumn.

Worth watching: RHS on hedge cutting technique

The Royal Horticultural Society's channel covers the fundamentals of correct hedge cutting in a short practical video. It addresses tool choice, the cutting angle and how to maintain an even line, all of which tie directly to the mistakes covered above. Search "What is the best way to cut a hedge RHS" on YouTube, or find it directly at youtube.com/watch?v=HaiwL5SVc28. The RHS channel has over 100,000 subscribers and its content is peer-reviewed by RHS advisers.

When to call a professional

If your hedge is already beyond the one-third rule in a single season, if it needs a significant height reduction near a boundary, or if it has developed bare patches from years of incorrect cutting, a professional assessment is often more economical than DIY recovery attempts.
