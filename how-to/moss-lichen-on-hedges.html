Moss, lichen and algae on hedges: does it matter and what should you do?

That green fuzz on your hedge stems, or the grey crust on the older wood, is not automatically a problem. Sometimes it is nothing. Sometimes it is an early signal worth paying attention to. Here is how to tell the difference.

Updated November 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Moss, lichen and algae on hedges are common in East Kent, particularly in coastal positions where sea mist raises the ambient humidity. A privet hedge in a shaded position in Sandwich or Deal will often develop a pale green algal film on its lower stems within a few years; a mature yew hedge at Sandwich Bay might carry lichen on the older wood for decades. Neither is a cause for alarm on its own. What matters is the pattern and whether it coincides with signs of declining growth.

Start here: what are you actually looking at?

Before deciding whether to act, identify which of these three things you have. They look similar at a glance but mean quite different things.

Green or bright-green fuzz or slime (moss or algae). Soft and slightly wet to the touch when damp. Algae forms a flat, smooth film; moss builds a thicker, almost cushion-like layer. Both grow on lower stems, in shaded areas and on north-facing sides. Common on privet, laurel, hornbeam and beech in sheltered or shaded positions. Particularly prevalent in coastal positions around Sandwich Bay, Deal and Eastry, where sea mist keeps humidity higher for longer stretches of the year.

Grey, silver, pale yellow or orange crust (lichen). Hard and dry to the touch. Does not wipe or wash off easily. Grows on older, slower-growing bark rather than on actively extending young stems. More common on sections of a hedge that have been left to bulk up without regular trimming, and on species such as yew, beech and hawthorn that carry old wood for decades. It often appears on one side of a hedge more than the other.

Black sticky or lacquered coating (sooty mould). A shiny, almost varnished film that coats stems and sometimes the upper surface of leaves. This is a completely different problem from moss or lichen. Sooty mould is a fungal growth that lives on honeydew, the sticky waste product of sap-sucking insects. Its presence always means insects are involved.

Diagnostic step 1: is the hedge growing normally?

This is the question that determines whether moss or lichen on your hedge is a cosmetic matter or a symptom to investigate.

If growth is normal and dense, the hedge is producing vigorous new shoots each season and responds well to trimming: the moss or lichen is cosmetic. Skip ahead to the removal section below.

If growth has slowed, the hedge looks sparse, or one section is noticeably less vigorous than the rest, the moss or lichen may be a secondary indicator of an underlying problem. Continue to step 2.

Lichen in particular colonises older, slower-growing bark. A hedge being clipped annually and producing vigorous new growth simply does not give lichen a stable enough surface to establish on the outer canopy. When lichen appears on sections you trim regularly, it usually means that section has slowed down. That is worth investigating.

Diagnostic step 2: where is the growth concentrated?

On lower stems and the base, below the main canopy. Common and largely harmless. It signals moisture retention and reduced air movement at ground level. This can also indicate that the soil at the hedge base is compacted or poorly drained. Worth checking the root zone, but rarely an urgent problem if the upper canopy is growing well.

On the outer canopy surface you trim regularly. If lichen is appearing on sections you clip each year, that section is not growing at the same rate as the rest. Check for three things: scale insect or aphid colonies on the stems; root zone compaction or waterlogging; dry or depleted soil with no organic matter in it. The lichen is a signal to look further, not the cause of the problem.

On actively growing foliage, leaves rather than stems. This is not lichen or moss. It is more likely a fungal disease or algal spray. Check the species care guide for your specific hedge type, as the causes and treatments vary considerably between privet, laurel, beech and conifers.

Diagnostic step 3: sooty mould only

If you have the black sticky coating described above, the diagnostic is different. Examine the affected stems closely in good light. You are looking for:

  • Flat oval shapes clustered on stems, brown, grey or cream in colour: these are scale insects, most likely brown scale or cushion scale.
  • Soft-bodied clusters on new growth or at leaf joints: aphids, often black or greenish.
  • Tiny white specks on the underside of leaves: whitefly.

The mould itself does not kill the hedge, but the insects producing the honeydew will weaken it over time by drawing sap. Treat the insect problem first. Once the honeydew supply stops, the sooty mould gradually weathers away with rain and wind over the following months. There is no need to scrub it off.

Removal or leave it?

Moss and algae on a healthy hedge: in most cases, leave it. If the appearance bothers you, brush lightly with a stiff hand brush on a dry day. This disrupts the growth without damaging the hedge. Avoid pressure washers: on a dormant deciduous hedge in winter the risk is minimal, but on conifers, evergreens and anything in active growth a pressure washer can blast off live growth, strip protective bark and open wounds to disease. It also does nothing to address the underlying conditions that allowed the growth in the first place.

Lichen on a healthy hedge: leave it. Lichen does not damage the plant. If a section is cosmetically very heavy with lichen, that is a signal to investigate growth rate rather than to scrub. Address whatever has slowed that section, and the lichen will naturally give way as growth resumes.

Moss or algae smothering the base or lower internal structure: thin the internal growth by selectively removing dead stems to improve air movement and light. The moss will reduce on its own as conditions change. This is worth doing as part of an autumn or winter tidy.

Preventing re-colonisation

The most reliable prevention is an actively growing hedge. A hedge clipped on schedule, fed with a balanced fertiliser in spring and mulched at the base simply does not give moss or lichen the stable, slow-growing surface they need. The outer canopy is continually renewing itself, and moss and lichen cannot keep up.

In coastal positions along the Sandwich Bay shore, Deal seafront and exposed garden edges further inland, algal growth on lower stems is almost inevitable given the humidity. It is worth accepting as a feature of the climate rather than treating it as a persistent problem. The hedges growing in those positions are generally healthy despite it.

Check for nesting birds before any heavy cleaning work

If removing heavy moss or lichen growth requires cutting back internal sections of the hedge, check for active nests first. Nesting season runs roughly 1 March to 31 August. It is an offence to damage a nest in active use under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981.
