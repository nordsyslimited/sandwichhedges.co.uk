Caring for a newly planted hedge

The first twelve months after planting decide whether your new hedge establishes properly or muddles along for years. A consistent routine of watering and mulching makes far more difference than the species you chose or the quality of the plants.

Updated August 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

We see a lot of hedges that were planted with real care, good bare-root whips in well-prepared ground, and then left to fend for themselves. In an average year in East Kent, that is a gamble. We get around 550 to 600 mm of rain a year here, which is less than the national average, and the sandy soils around Sandwich Bay and the chalk around Ash and Woodnesborough drain fast. A newly planted hedge without regular watering in its first summer can fail quietly, taking another year before the owner realises the plants are not coming back.

The good news is that the establishment routine is not complicated. It is mostly watering and mulching, with a light formative prune in the first winter and some basic weed control. Seven steps, none of them difficult.

Before you start: formative pruning and the nesting season

Formative pruning, the initial cut that encourages branching and bushiness in young plants, should ideally be done at planting time (autumn to early spring) or in the first late winter before growth begins. Avoid any cutting between 1 March and 31 August unless you have checked that there are no active nests. Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, damaging or destroying an active bird's nest is a criminal offence. Even young, newly planted hedging plants can attract nesting birds by their second spring. If you are formative pruning in late winter, you are well within the safe window; if you missed it and are considering a cut in April or May, look carefully before cutting anything.

Before you start: safety for formative pruning

Formative pruning of bare-root whips and young hedging plants involves secateurs and loppers rather than powered machinery. Even so: wear eye protection (small cut stems spring back), use sturdy gloves with thorny species such as hawthorn, blackthorn or Rosa rugosa, and keep blades clean and sharp. Dull blades crush stems rather than cutting cleanly, which slows healing and invites disease. See our guide to sharpening hedge shears and secateurs for the maintenance routine.

Step 1: water immediately after planting

Whether you are planting bare-root whips in November or pot-grown plants in spring, water thoroughly immediately after planting. Soak the root zone, not just the surface. For a row of whips, give each plant a good five to ten litres as you go, then water the whole row again once you have finished. If the ground is very dry or sandy, a short wait after the initial water (fifteen minutes) and then a second soaking helps water penetrate deeper rather than running off.

Step 2: apply a mulch along the full length

A 7 to 10 cm layer of composted bark, well-rotted garden compost or wood chip applied immediately after planting is the single most effective thing you can do for a new hedge. Mulch does three jobs at once: it holds moisture in the root zone, it suppresses weeds that would otherwise compete with the young plants, and it gradually improves soil structure as it breaks down.

Apply the mulch in a strip at least 50 cm wide centred on the hedge line. Keep it about 10 cm away from the stems to avoid the damp conditions that encourage stem rot and slug damage. Replenish the mulch in subsequent springs as it breaks down. On the thin chalk or sandy soils common in parts of East Kent, a mulch can make the difference between a hedge that just survives and one that genuinely thrives.

Step 3: water weekly through the first growing season

During dry spells from March through to October in the first year, water along the full hedge line at least once a week. How much depends on the soil: on freedraining sandy ground, aim for around 20 to 30 litres per metre of hedge run, delivered slowly so it soaks in. On heavier clay soils, check the top 10 cm of soil first and skip watering if it is still moist.

Water at the base rather than over the foliage where possible. Evening or early morning watering reduces evaporation. A slow trickle from a hose laid along the hedge for 20 to 30 minutes is more effective than a quick spray-over.

In a wet year, or for hedges planted in autumn and going into a normal wet Kent winter, this schedule can be relaxed. The problem is that East Kent summers can be genuinely dry, and a prolonged dry spell in June or July in the first year can kill young plants that were growing well just weeks before. Do not assume an established look in spring means the hedge can cope without water by July.

Step 4: keep weeds clear for the first two seasons

Weed competition is the other main cause of slow establishment. Grass and weeds growing right up to the stems compete directly with the young hedge plants for water and nutrients. The mulch will suppress most weeds in the strip immediately around the plants, but the mulch layer needs to be kept topped up and any weeds that do come through should be pulled by hand rather than strimmed, which risks damaging the stems.

Do not use a weedkiller along a newly planted hedge unless you are confident about the species and its sensitivity. Some hedging plants, particularly young bare-root specimens, are susceptible to herbicide drift or root uptake.

Step 5: formative pruning in the first late winter

Many gardeners skip formative pruning because they want the hedge to gain height as fast as possible. The result is often a sparse hedge with a dense top and a bare base, which is difficult to correct later. A light formative cut in the first or second late winter, typically January or February, sacrifices a few months' height gain but produces a much bushier, denser hedge within the same overall time frame.

For most deciduous hedging species (hawthorn, blackthorn, hornbeam, beech, privet), cut back by roughly one third of the plant's height after planting. This sounds drastic if the plants are only 30 to 60 cm tall to begin with, but it stimulates lower side shoots that would otherwise remain dormant. For evergreen species planted as pot-grown or root-ball specimens (laurel, privet, leylandii), a lighter tip-pruning is usually sufficient rather than a hard cutback.

Do not cut back yew hard in the first year. Yew is slow enough that you want to let it establish for a full season before applying any pruning pressure.

Step 6: manage the first summer drought

East Kent is one of the drier parts of England. The Sandwich Bay and Deal coastal strip can go four to six weeks without meaningful rain in a dry summer, and newly planted hedges on sandy ground are particularly vulnerable. If your hedge shows signs of wilting or leaf scorch in summer, increase watering frequency immediately rather than waiting to see if it recovers.

Signs of drought stress in a new hedge: leaves going limp or rolling inward, yellowing from the tips inward on deciduous species, or on conifers, tips going grey-brown rather than the characteristic bronzing some species show in cold weather. If the soil underneath the mulch is dry and dusty when you press a finger in, the hedge needs water regardless of what the plants look like above ground.

Do not trim a newly planted hedge in summer. The formative pruning happens in late winter only. Cutting in summer while the plants are already stressed by heat and drought is counterproductive. Our full summer drought guide covers the recovery routine if things have already got difficult.

Step 7: feed from year two onwards

In the first year, a well-mulched hedge in decent soil does not usually need additional fertiliser: the organic mulch releases nutrients steadily and the focus is on root development, not top growth. From the second spring onwards, a general-purpose slow-release fertiliser or a top-dressing of blood, fish and bone applied in March gives the hedge the nitrogen it needs to put on strong growth through the growing season.

Do not apply high-nitrogen fertiliser in the first summer. It encourages soft, fast top growth at a time when the plant's root system cannot yet support it, and the resulting sappy growth is more vulnerable to drought stress, wind damage and aphid attack. Our hedge feeding guide covers what to use and when in more detail.

What happens in year two

A hedge that has been watered and mulched through its first year should, by late spring of year two, be visibly bushy at the base and putting on noticeably strong growth. Continue mulching each spring and watering during dry spells until the hedge is fully established, which most species reach after two to three growing seasons on reasonable soils, or three to four on very poor sandy or chalky ground.

The formal trimming programme, one or two cuts per year according to the species, starts in earnest once the hedge has reached close to its intended height and width. Until then, the focus is entirely on establishment. See our month-by-month trimming calendar for when to start cutting each species.
