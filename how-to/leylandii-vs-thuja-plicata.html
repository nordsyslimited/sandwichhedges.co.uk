Leylandii vs western red cedar: which conifer screen for East Kent?

Both will give you a tall evergreen screen. The question is which one you want to be maintaining in ten years' time. Leylandii is faster and cheaper. Western red cedar is slower, better-looking, more forgiving of mistakes, and repels deer.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Leylandii (x Cupressocyparis leylandii) is by far the most planted conifer screen in East Kent. Drive anywhere from Sandwich to Deal to Worth and you will see it everywhere: along field boundaries, at the back of new-build gardens, screening utility areas. It got to that position by being cheap, fast and available.

Western red cedar (Thuja plicata) is its main rival for the same purpose, and it is a more interesting plant in almost every way. It is slower, which is a real cost if you need privacy quickly, but the trade-off is a hedge that is easier to keep in shape over time, more tolerant of moderate hard cutting, more resistant to deer, and arguably better-looking in a formal garden setting.

We maintain runs of both across East Kent. Here is what the comparison looks like in practice.

Nesting season: September to February is the safe window

Both leylandii and thuja can hold active bird nests. The UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs roughly 1 March to 31 August. The safe cutting window for conifer screens is September to February. Never cut a dense conifer hedge between March and August without checking it thoroughly for active nests first.

Leylandii: the case for speed

The honest argument for leylandii is that nothing else closes a gap that quickly at that price. If you have moved into a new-build on the edge of Sandwich or Deal where the garden boundary is open and exposed, leylandii planted at 90 cm will give you a workable 2.5 metre screen in about three years. Nothing else legal in the UK comes close to that rate.

The maintenance requirement is the cost. An established leylandii screen that you want to keep at two metres needs cutting at least twice a year, typically late spring and late summer. In a growing season with any warmth, it will put on 75 to 90 centimetres per side per year. Skip one season and you are chasing a hedge that is a foot over height on all faces.

The other discipline is never cutting into brown wood. Unlike yew, which will regenerate freely from a trunk cut back to ground level, leylandii has no capacity to regenerate from old wood. The brown interior of the hedge is dead. If you cut past the green growth zone (which you can see clearly as the outer 15 to 25 centimetres of foliage), that section stays brown permanently. The only ways to fix a leylandii hedge that has been cut too hard are to live with the brown patches or remove and replant.

Salt exposure is not a problem. Leylandii handles the coastal conditions along Sandwich Bay and the Deal seafront as well as anything. Wind scorch on the seaward face is common after a hard winter but it is cosmetic.

Western red cedar: the case for the slower option

Western red cedar (Thuja plicata, not to be confused with the unrelated Thuja occidentalis or white cedar) is used as a formal hedge more often in Scotland and the wetter north and west of England than in Kent, where leylandii has dominated. That is partly tradition and partly the speed difference at planting time. But it is worth reconsidering.

The growth rate of 30 to 45 centimetres per year means you only need to cut a maintained thuja hedge once or twice a year, not three times. The annual maintenance burden is lower. The hedge stays in shape more easily because it is not racing away between cuts.

The brown wood question is also different. Thuja plicata can tolerate moderate cuts back into older wood, provided some green foliage remains on each branch being cut. It will not regenerate from completely bare stems, but it is genuinely more forgiving of the kind of overcut that would permanently damage a leylandii. That extra margin of error matters when you are doing your own trimming or asking someone else to do it.

The winter bronzing is a genuine visual feature. As temperatures drop through October and November, a thuja hedge takes on a warm bronze-gold colouring that returns to bright green in spring. Whether you find this attractive or odd is a matter of taste, but it is markedly different from leylandii's unchanging dark green.

The scent is noticeable when cutting. The crushed foliage has a distinctive fruity fragrance, the reason it is sometimes called the pineapple tree. Most people like it. It is at its strongest during and immediately after cutting.

The deer question: relevant around Ash, Worth and Woodnesborough

For properties on the edges of farmland and woodland around Ash, Worth, Woodnesborough and Eastry, deer damage is a real consideration. Both roe deer and fallow deer are present across East Kent, and leylandii is among the plants they browse most readily. New plantings are particularly vulnerable: young leylandii at 60 to 90 centimetres can be stripped to bare stems by a single deer visit.

Thuja plicata's aromatic foliage is generally unpalatable to deer. Established hedges on deer-active land around Woodnesborough that are thuja rather than leylandii show markedly less browse damage. If your site has any deer pressure at all, this is one of the stronger practical arguments for thuja over leylandii.

What about the High Hedges Act?

Both species are covered by the high hedges provisions of the Anti-Social Behaviour Act 2003, Part 8. Any evergreen or semi-evergreen hedge over 2 metres that a council determines adversely affects a neighbour's reasonable enjoyment of their property can be the subject of a remedial notice. The legislation does not distinguish between leylandii and thuja: what matters is height and impact.

In practice, leylandii generates far more complaints than thuja because leylandii grows faster and more homeowners allow it to exceed their intended height before they act on it. Thuja's slower growth makes height management easier, which indirectly reduces the risk of getting to remedial-notice territory. For the full picture on the legal framework, see our leylandii height rules and neighbour law guide.

Which to choose?

Choose leylandii if speed and cost are the priority and you are committed to cutting it consistently. It is the right answer for a new-build boundary that needs to close up in three years. Accept that it demands regular, disciplined maintenance and that it cannot be cut back if it gets away.

Choose thuja plicata if you have deer pressure, if you want something that needs fewer cuts per year, or if you are prepared to wait an extra two or three years for the same height. The bronze winter colour suits some gardens better than leylandii's unvarying dark green. It is also the more forgiving plant to cut, which matters if you or your gardener are less experienced with conifer hedges.
