Myrobalan (cherry plum) hedge care in Kent

Cherry plum is one of the earliest-flowering hedgerow plants in the British landscape: white blossom in February before almost anything else stirs. Here is how it compares with hawthorn, blackthorn and field maple, so you can choose the right native species for your East Kent boundary.

Updated October 2026 By Richard & the Sandwich Hedges & Tree Services team

Myrobalan, or cherry plum (Prunus cerasifera), turns up in East Kent hedgerows far more often than people realise. It has been planted and naturalised here for centuries, partly because it is used as a rootstock for grafted plums, damsons and gages, and partly because it is an exceptionally tough, fast-growing plant. Walk along almost any older farm boundary between Sandwich and Ash in late February and you will see something smothered in tiny white flowers before the blackthorn or hawthorn have done anything.

As a garden hedge, myrobalan is underused. It gets overlooked in favour of hawthorn and blackthorn, perhaps because it has the word "plum" in the name and people assume it is a fruit tree rather than a hedgerow plant. It is both. Grown as a hedge, it behaves like a fast-establishing native boundary shrub and produces small edible plums as a bonus.

Side-by-side comparison

All four species below are deciduous native plants well suited to East Kent. The differences are in growth rate, thorn density, flower and fruit timing, and how you prune them.

When to choose each species

Choose myrobalan if

You want a hedge established quickly, you do not need impenetrable thorns, and early blossom for pollinators matters to you. Myrobalan is the right call on a freshly cleared field boundary around Worth or Eastry where you want something established and head-height within three or four years. It is also the right choice on chalk soils in the Sandwich area where a general-purpose native hedgerow is the goal and you are not specifically trying to deter livestock or deer.

The summer fruit is a practical bonus: small yellow or red plums appear in July and August, freely eaten by birds, and good for jams if you pick them first. In mild coastal Kent winters, the February blossom sometimes opens alongside the very last of the snowdrops.

Choose hawthorn if

Wildlife is the priority and you are patient. Hawthorn produces far more autumn berries for birds than myrobalan, and its May blossom is one of the most important nectar sources in the Kent countryside. The dense, hard thorns also make it a more effective physical barrier. Given twenty years, a well-managed hawthorn hedge is almost impossible to push through, which myrobalan, with its lighter thorning, never quite achieves. For a mixed native hedge combining both, the usual proportion is about two hawthorn to one myrobalan.

Choose blackthorn if

Security is the overriding requirement and you can wait for establishment. Blackthorn is the slowest of the four species listed here to reach hedge height from a bare-root whip, but the thorns are brutal and dense once the hedge thickens. It also spreads by suckering, which can fill gaps naturally or become a management issue if it spreads into a lawn or border. For coastal gardens in Sandwich Bay and Deal, where salt-laden wind is a genuine problem, blackthorn's wind tolerance is hard to match.

Choose field maple if

You want autumn colour and no thorns. Field maple, our only truly native maple, turns a reliable yellow-orange in October and holds the colour for two to three weeks. It responds very well to clipping, which is why it is used in formal hedges as well as mixed native ones. On the chalk downs around Sandwich, field maple is a natural component of hedgerows and grows without complaint.

Pruning a myrobalan hedge

Nesting season: the rule that governs the cutting window

The UK Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to damage or destroy an active bird's nest. Nesting season runs roughly from 1 March to 31 August. For myrobalan hedges, the safe cutting window is September to February. Because myrobalan flowers in late February and March, cutting in that window removes the blossom: for most hedges, the practical main cut is November or December, after the leaves have dropped and before the flower buds open. See our full hedge-cutting calendar for all common species.

Before you start: safety

  • Eye protection: safety glasses. Myrobalan has occasional sharp spurs that can catch you at face height.
  • Gloves: sturdy gardening gloves; the thorns are lighter than blackthorn but sharp enough.
  • Ear defenders: for petrol or electric hedge trimmers.
  • RCD adaptor: essential for any corded electric trimmer, no exceptions.
  • Ladder: prefer a long-reach pole trimmer over a ladder for hedges above shoulder height. If a ladder is needed, have someone footing it and never overreach sideways.
  • Wasp nests: check a mature hedge for wasps before cutting in autumn, particularly in September and early October.

Annual maintenance trimming

For an established myrobalan hedge, one cut per year in November or December is usually sufficient. Myrobalan grows more vigorously than hawthorn or blackthorn, so if you want a tight hedge below 2 metres, you may need a second, lighter pass in late August or early September. Avoid cutting in March while the blossom is open, and avoid June and July when the plant is in full leaf and putting on its main growth.

The plant responds well to both machine trimming and loppers. For a hedge above head height, a long-reach hedge trimmer makes the job much easier and avoids the ladder entirely.

Formative pruning in the first two years

Myrobalan whips planted bare-root in November to February should be cut back by one-third on planting, which encourages lateral branching and a bushy base. In the second winter, cut back again by one-third of the new growth. This gives a denser hedge from the base up, rather than a few tall, wispy stems with no coverage below shoulder height.

Ashridge Nurseries, one of the UK's leading specialist hedging suppliers, covers the principles of formative pruning for bare-root native hedging plants in their planting guides. Their YouTube channel has a guide to planting a bareroot native country hedge that covers first-year care in detail.

Renovation of an overgrown hedge

An overgrown myrobalan hedge that has gone to small trees rather than a managed boundary can be renovated hard in winter. Cut back to within 30 to 60 cm of the ground in November, and it will regenerate vigorously in the following spring. Some stems may die back, but the root system is vigorous and the plant almost always pushes back from the base. For a very large or old hedge, spread the renovation over two winters, cutting one side each year.

Myrobalan in East Kent hedgerows

In the villages around Sandwich, Ash and Woodnesborough, older farm boundaries often contain a mixture of hawthorn, blackthorn, field maple and myrobalan, sometimes with elder and dog rose as well. The myrobalan in these hedges usually started as rootstock that was never budded, or as a sucker from a grafted plum tree that escaped over the boundary. Either way, the species has been part of this landscape for a long time.

In Kent's mild coastal winters, myrobalan's February blossom is genuinely useful: queen bumblebees emerging early on a warm day in late February and early March can find nectar at a time when almost nothing else is flowering. That early pollen matters more in some years than in others, but planting myrobalan in a mixed hedge rather than a monoculture is a straightforward way to extend the season for pollinators.
