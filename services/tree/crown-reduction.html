Crown reduction in Sandwich & East Kent

Reducing the overall height or spread by cutting branches back to lateral limbs of a sensible size. Not topping: that's the wrong approach and we don't do it. Reduction protects the tree's future shape while relieving the pressure now.

Reduction, not topping

Cut back to a growth point, not a stub.

The right way to reduce a tree is to cut back to a lateral branch that's at least a third of the diameter of the cut you're making. That side branch takes over as the new leader and the wound seals cleanly. Done across the whole crown, the tree ends up shorter or narrower, still shaped like a tree.

Topping means cutting through the trunk or main limbs at a random height, leaving stubs. Topped trees respond with a hedge of weak epicormic sprouts, half of which drop off in the first big wind. The tree's structure is ruined and the surgeon has to come back every three years. It's bad practice, and it's the single most common reason a mature tree turns dangerous within a decade.

What reduction gets you

  • Lower overall height (typically a 15–25% reduction)
  • Narrower spread on the side that overhangs the neighbour or the drive
  • Less wind loading on marsh-edge and coast-exposed trees
  • Less shade on the garden without the tree being obviously smaller
  • A tree that keeps its natural shape and lifespan
Sandwich context

When reduction is the right call

Species we reduce most in East Kent:

  • Sycamore & lime: the classic town-garden reduction. Reduce 20% every five to seven years and they stay a manageable garden tree for life.
  • Willow: the marsh-edge escapees down towards the Delf and the golf course. Willows respond well to reduction; badly to topping.
  • Horse chestnut: mature ones on the older lanes towards Woodnesborough. Reduction is often the right call before decline sets in.
  • Poplar: the fastest-growing, most brittle tree around. Reduce to reduce failure risk on wind days.

Not every tree is a candidate

Beech and oak don't reduce well; they need small, careful cuts and staged reductions over multiple years. If a beech or oak really needs to come down in height, sometimes replacement is the honest answer. We'll tell you which side of that line your tree sits on.

TPO and CA: nearly always required

A crown reduction on any tree over 75mm diameter inside a Sandwich conservation area triggers a section-211 notice. A TPO tree needs a full application with eight weeks' notice. We file both, and we brief the DDC tree officer on the reduction spec before the job, making the process smoother when there's a heritage building nearby.

Good questions

Crown reduction FAQs

How much does reduction cost?

£350 to £1,200 per tree. A small ornamental reduced by 20% is nearer £350. A mature sycamore with a full rig-down and TPO application is nearer £1,200.

Will you top my tree if I insist?

No. Topping damages the tree and we won't take money for a job that ends badly. If reduction isn't what you want, felling and replacement is usually the better honest answer, and we'll quote that instead.

How often do I need it done?

Most reduced trees are back on a five-to-seven year cycle. Fast growers (willow, poplar, sycamore) closer to five. Slower (lime, horse chestnut) closer to seven.

Do I need permission?

For any tree over 75mm diameter in a conservation area, yes: six weeks' section-211 notice to Dover District Council. For a TPO tree, an eight-week application. Handled by us.
